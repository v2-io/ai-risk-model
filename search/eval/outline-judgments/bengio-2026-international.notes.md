# Notes on judging bengio-2026-international (IASR 2026)

*Written by the judge of `bengio-2026-international.json`, 2026-10-09, after reading lines 1–3136 in order. The Notes section (3137–7704, the bibliography) was searched for the query terms, not read. I did not look at the outline or the search tool's output before or while writing the judgments.*

## How the file is organised, and where its structure misleads

- **This is a rendering of the web page, not of the PDF.** Lines 1–135 are site chrome and two copies of the table of contents. The body runs from 436 to about 2733, the glossary from 2737 to 3113, and the Notes (bibliography) from 3137 to 7704. By bytes, about 37% of the file is the bibliography.
- **Most of the body's bytes are citation tooltips.** Each inline footnote is a markdown link whose title attribute holds the full reference: `[387](https://…#footnote… "J. Benton, …, Sabotage Evaluations for Frontier Models …")`. The body is about 1.35 MB as stored but about 0.48 MB once those are removed. If passages are indexed with the tooltips in them, a term like "sabotage", "sandbagging" or "whistleblowers" will match citation titles inside paragraphs that never use the word themselves. For example, the Table 2.2 row on line 1140 carries "Sabotage Evaluations" only through its tooltip and a one-word label. I haven't checked whether the index strips them. I read through a copy with them stripped, which keeps the line numbers the same.
- **The heading levels are flat where the document is nested.** The subsections `#### 2.1.3. Cyberattacks` and `#### 2.2.2. Loss of control` are at the same level as their own parts (`#### Key information`, `#### Updates`, `#### Evidence gaps`, `#### Mitigations`, `#### Challenges for policymakers`, `#### Box 2.x`). An outline built from markdown levels will show a subsection's contents as its siblings. The only thing tying them to 2.1.3 is order.
- **Every subsection repeats the same template headings:** Key information, body, Updates, Evidence gaps, Mitigations, Challenges for policymakers. Each of these labels appears 9 to 16 times as a `####` heading, so a heading alone says nothing until it is shown with its parent.
- **Several real sections are not headings at all.** "Contributors", "Acknowledgements", "Copyright and disclaimer", "Forewords", "About this Report", "Key developments since the 2025 Report", "Glossary" and "How to cite this report" are plain lines. In particular, **the glossary (2737–3113) falls under the last heading before it, `#### The value of shared understanding` in the Conclusion.** For definitional queries the glossary is often the grade-2 answer: hazard, misalignment, whistleblowing, sandbagging, loss of control, control. An outline that unfolds by section will see it as the tail of the Conclusion.
- **`pdf-text` blocks (figure text recovered from the PDF) often sit under the next heading rather than with their figure.** The text of Figure 2.12, the o3 chains of thought including "Should we purposely sabotage ourselves to get deployed?", is at 1594–1596, under "Long-term autonomous operation is not yet feasible". Figure 2.1's data is under `#### Updates` (1062) and Figure 2.2's under `#### Evidence gaps` (1072). Figure 2.10's is under the multi-agent heading (1482). The page markers also jump backwards in places (pages 47, 46, 48, 46, 47 around 1036–1056) because the web order differs from the PDF's.

## Things in the document worth knowing for the project (beyond the queries)

- **The report defines loss of control in two different ways.** The Executive Summary (474) and the glossary (2931) say "no clear path to regaining control". §2.2.2 (1528, 1537) says "regaining control is either extremely costly or impossible". §2.2.2 also limits itself to *active* loss of control. Its footnote (1551) and the glossary (2985) name a *passive* kind.
- **"Systemic risks" is also defined twice, and each definition quotes the EU AI Act differently.** The footnote at 541 contrasts the report's sense with the Act's "risks of large-scale harm". The glossary at 3077 quotes the Act as "risk that is specific to the high-impact capabilities of general-purpose AI models, having a significant impact".
- **"Resilience" loses a term in the glossary.** §3.5 (2589, 2597) has "resist, absorb, recover from, and adapt to", but the glossary (3029) has "absorb, adapt to, and recover from", without "resist".
- **Some words are used in more than one sense:**
  - "sabotage" means human reputational sabotage at 1030 and 1078, but AI sabotage in Table 2.2 (1140) and in the Figure 2.12 text.
  - "misaligned incentives" (1907) is the market-failure sense.
  - "hazard" has at least three uses: the glossary's harm-potential sense, Box 2.3's "information hazards", and the safety-engineering sense of system-level hazards and STPA.
- **The report says it is not prescriptive (406, 2043, 2733), but 2581 is normative:** "To avoid catastrophic harm, developers of open-weight models should not release models without evaluating risks". Its other uses of "should" in the body are posed as questions ("whether there should be rules", 1678; "whether and how incentives should be shifted", 2713), or occur in quoted material, examples and tables.

## The queries, for this document

- **catastrophic risk.** The report never defines it and does not use it in its own voice. The only literal use is Table 3.5's summary of Anthropic's ASL tiers (2216). Its own severity language is "extreme severity", "extinction of humanity" and "catastrophic outcomes" (1537–1541), plus "catastrophic harm" (2581). I marked the loss-of-control severity passage as must-read because it is where the report itself addresses the scale, but someone could fairly have said that only 2216 matters.
- **serious incident.** The report has no definition. It mentions serious incidents only through the EU Code of Practice (2254, 2705) and once in the §3.5 key information (2592). "Incident reporting" is defined (2909) and is everywhere. SB 53's own term, critical safety incident, is not used. It is described only as "incident reporting" (2256).
- **what must a developer publish or report before deploying a frontier model.** In this document nothing is a "must" in its own voice. Every answer reports someone else's obligation or commitment: the EU AI Act and its Code, SB 53, the Seoul commitments, the HAIP reporting framework, the frameworks in Table 3.5. I graded the passages that report legal or committed obligations as 2 and voluntary practice as 1. The query is still useful here, because an agent should come away knowing the report describes these obligations rather than imposing any.
- **humans can no longer shut down or correct the AI system.** This reads as a definition from another source (EU Code style). The closest match here is the glossary's "Control" (2817) together with the loss-of-control definitions. The report hardly addresses "correct" as such. The nearest material is training-time compliance meant to avoid modification (1582) and the irreversibility of open weights (2537–2539).
- **Questions the evaluation might add:**
  - One whose answer lives mainly in a table, for example "which developers' frameworks cover loss of control or misalignment" (Table 3.5, 2213–2238). The table's rows are long single lines full of `<br>`, and it is a good test of whether the outline opens tables.
  - One whose answer lives in the glossary, such as "how is risk defined" (3033). That would test the glossary-buried-under-Conclusion problem directly.

## On the format and the scoring

- **I graded the three whole-section answers as single stretches**, as the brief asks: cyber 1194–1312, bio/chem 1316–1432 and loss of control 1524–1678. `outline-check` credits a stretch if *any* passage overlapping it is read, and each stretch's gain depends only on its grade. So reading one paragraph of the 155-line loss-of-control section earns the same credit as reading all of it, and as much as finding a one-line glossary definition. For these queries the score will overstate how much of what must be read was reached. A coverage fraction for long stretches would measure it more truly.
- **The same stretch is often graded under several queries**, such as 474, 1600–1602, 2195–2238 and 2581. That is deliberate. The questions overlap in this document.
- **Quotes need content lines, not headings.** Heading lines belong to no indexed passage, so a quote taken from a heading can't find its stretch once the line numbers drift. The same goes for a table row quoted raw, because its `**` and `<br>` become stray letters. So each stretch here starts on its first content line, and its quote is that line's opening words with markdown, `<br>` and footnote links removed, the way passages store them. I checked that all 157 items resolve both by line and by quote alone (using `outline-check`'s own `resolve`). A judge who quotes a heading literally, as the brief's wording suggests, will get unresolvable quotes after any rebuild.
- Nothing in the format got in the way of saying something true. The one thing I couldn't express is "read this *instead of* that". For example, Table 3.5 summarises each framework more compactly than Box 2.2 does for three of them.
