# Source atlas: other-industry safety management, and press on the Anthropic "supply chain risk" designation

Line numbers refer to `…/scratchpad/src-text/<key>.txt` (pdftotext `-layout`). "p" is the physical PDF page, counted by form-feeds (`\f`), so p1 is the first page of the PDF whatever the printed folio says. Where the printed folio differs, I give it too. Ranges are inclusive. The one-line notes are signposts. They say what drew me to a passage, not what the passage means. Quotations in them are verbatim from the text file.

A reading tip: the CSB report is **double-spaced in extraction**, with a blank line between every text line. Its ranges therefore look about twice as long as they read. The HSE sheet is **two-column and interleaved**: each physical line carries a left-column fragment and a right-column fragment. Read it down the left half, then down the right half.

There the CSB lays three authorities' definitions of *safety culture* side by side (HSE, CCPS, Reason/Hopkins), says why it prefers the practice-based one, and settles on "the way we do things around here".
- **Extraction notes:** **Appendix B, the Logic Tree (p222–235), is image-only.** Lines 11223–11310 hold nothing but running headers. - **Passages:**
  1. **754–924 (p18–21).** The investigation declares its causal *level* before it gives any causes. It says it used the Columbia (CAIB) model, quotes CAIB on how investigations "identify the widget that broke… then locate the person" (803–819), and states "Simply targeting the mistakes of BP's operators and supervisors misses the underlying… causes… that have a greater preventative impact" (822–826). Footnote 6 (843–846) backs that preventive-impact claim by citing CCPS. The passage sets immediate and root causes against each other as a methodological commitment.
  2. **2344–2522 (p51–54).** The incident narrative, minute by minute. What drew me is the **graded attribution of every factual claim**: "in post-incident testimony, said that he was unaware" (2370), "stated in post-incident interviews" (2403), "The CSB determined that the miscommunication likely concerned…" (2463–2465), "CSB concludes that there was likely no flow out of the tower" (2520–2522). Footnote 39 (2445): "No BP managers other than frontline supervisors have acknowledged authorizing the startup." That is an absence of evidence, recorded as such.
  3. **5205–5294 (p107–108).** A **ranked taxonomy of *safeguards*** (CCPS Table 3: passive > active > procedural, 5236–5248), with the causal reason for the ranking. Procedures "rely on personnel consistently making correct and timely decisions while… potentially… stressed or fatigued" (5229–5233). It closes with a counterfactual design claim. Useful against the AI sources' loose use of "safeguard".
  4. **8669–8958 (p173–178).** The case built from **the organization's own internal documents**. The Telos safety-culture survey findings are quoted verbatim (8693–8762). Workers saw managers as "too worried about seat belts" (8782–8784). The 2005 business plan warned the site would "kill someone in the next 12-18 months" (8906–8908). The Business Unit Leader's email pairs the survey with "the best profitability ever" (8812–8816). The causal verdict is "Focus on injury rates… helped mask severe shortcomings in process safety" (8843–8845). Warnings are documented, dated and footnoted to specific memos.
  5. **9239–9420 (p184–187).** The analytical register. The metric-validity argument (personal-injury rate vs process safety; Kletz: "The lost time rate is not a measure of process safety", 9308–9310). Leading and lagging indicators as "driv[ing] down the road looking only in the rear view mirror" (9317–9321). Then "check the box" (9349–9367) and "Oversimplification", defined at 9373–9377 by borrowing HRO theory. Named failure patterns are each tied to a cited literature *and* to specific instances at the site.
  6. **10606–10800 (p210–213).** Root and contributing causes as **bulleted attributions of failure to named organizational actors** ("BP Group Board did not provide effective oversight…", "Senior executives: … did not provide adequate resources"). Recommendations follow, each with an ID (`2005-4-I-TX-R6`…), a named recipient (API/USW, OSHA…), "At a minimum" clauses, and requirements on the *process* by which the standard is to be written (ANSI openness, balance, due process; 10741–10753).
- **Short and distinctive:**
  - 4942–4954 (p101), "Distraction Not a Factor": a considered-and-rejected cause, stated as a finding.
  - 9964–10051 (p198–199). A regulatory gap ("OSHA does not require employers to evaluate these types of changes", 9970). ### hse-2003-chis7 — UK Health and Safety Executive, *Organisational change and major accident hazards*, Chemical Information Sheet No. CHIS7 (June 2003) — 520 lines, 8 pp

- **Read it whole (520 lines).** But mind the two-column interleaving described at the top. On p1 one sentence starts at the foot of the left column (59–63, "HSE's experience is that in many instances organisational changes are not…") and continues at the *top* of the right column (6–14, "…analysed and controlled as thoroughly as plant changes, resulting in reduced defences against major accidents… This is because… impacts of organisational change are less well understood"). That sentence is the document's central causal claim, and extraction splits it in two.
- **TOC:** none. A three-step framework is announced at 54–58 (right column, p1) and drawn in Figure 1 at 66–111 (p2). The figure extracted as text.
- **Glossary:** none. Terms are defined inline, in running prose. *Mapping*: "the painstaking process of understanding and tracking the detail of the change" (252–253, left column). *Intelligent customer*: "retain adequate technical competence to judge whether, and ensure that, work done is of the required quality and safety" (264–267, right column). *'Lead' vs 'lag' indicators*: 337–342, right column.
- **Extraction notes:** headings in bold have lost their spaces ("Commitmentandresources" 90, "Scenarioassessments" 217). The checklist glyphs carry meaning. The tick, rendered `�`, marks a "Do". `X` marks a "Don't" (212–247, 391–398). `!` marks a warning (286). The polarity of each item depends on reading those glyphs.
- **Passages:**
  1. **1–64 (p1).** Scope, the "What changes?" list (business process re-engineering, delayering, outsourcing…), and an **incident used as an exemplar**: the Hickson & Welch fire, 1992, five killed. It quotes the HSE incident report inside a sidebar (42–55, left column). The central causal claim is described above.
  2. **149–346 (p3–5).** Step 2, risk assessment. Two kinds of risk "should not be confused": risks *from* the change and risks from the *process* of change (158–164, right column). Mapping and scenario assessment follow, then the Do/Don't checklists, then the contractor "intelligent customer" duty. Then the "Risk alert" list of human-failure modes (325–334). Then the lead-vs-lag indicator guidance ("wherever possible should be 'lead' indicators measuring the control of risks rather than 'lag' indicators of the realisation of risk", 338–341).
  3. **349–480 (p6–7).** Transition and legal requirements. The modal register shifts from "should" to statute. "You must ensure that the change is not hurried through" (383–387, left column). "Be ready ultimately to change or even reverse decisions… however uncomfortable this might be" (370–372, right column). Nuclear Licence Condition 36 is quoted verbatim (448–456, left column). The COMAH duties follow.
- **Worth setting side by side:** 13–15 ("Following the guidance means that you will be complying with your general legal responsibilities") and 497–500 (p8, "This leaflet contains notes on good practice which are not compulsory"). One document makes two statements of its own normative force.

Not quite what the title suggests. Lines 8–11 (p1): "This document is an unedited version of an ICAO publication and has not yet been approved in final form." The published fourth edition may differ. One likely difference is flagged under passage 2. Printed folios are chapter-relative (2-5, 4-9…). I give them alongside PDF pages.

Terms marked with an asterisk are "already defined as such in Annexes and PANS" (256–257), so **provenance is marked per term**. *Safety* is defined as a state ("risks… are reduced and controlled to an acceptable level", 281–282). *Safety risk* is "The predicted probability and severity of the consequences" (328). - **Passages:**
  1. **1101–1233 (p29–31, printed 2-5–2-7).** Accident causation. **Causal theory is delegated to named models**: "The Reason Model proposes that all accidents include a combination of both active failures and latent conditions" (1111–1112); "Snook contests that practical drift is inevitable" (1207). The manual hedges its own model: "There are more sophisticated models" (1170–1172). It also makes a moral-causal claim: "latent conditions, when created, had good intentions" (1127–1128).
  2. **1438–1632 (p35–38, printed 2-11–2-14).** Classification and measurement machinery. The PDF has the same text, so this is not an extraction fault. It appears to be a typo for 4A/4B, and as printed it no longer covers the worked "4B = intolerable" example two paragraphs earlier (1593–1594).
  3. **1779–1956 (p43–46, printed 3-1–3-4), then 2092–2123 (p49).** Safety culture: "how people behave in relation to safety and risk when no one is watching" (1787). A hedged strong claim: "Safety culture is arguably the single most important influence on the management of safety" (1822–1823). A reciprocal-causation claim (1846–1847). A measurable proxy: more confidential and fewer anonymous reports signals progress (1882–1884). The closing caveat: scoring culture "can have unintended consequences by inadvertently encouraging the organization to strive to achieve the 'right' score" (2121–2123).
  4. **2550–2620 (p59–60, printed 4-9–4-10).** Setting safety-performance targets, including the **manual's own Goodhart caveats**: "Achieving the agreed SPTs is not always indicative of safety performance improvement" (2585–2586); "Safety reporting is an example where having a target could either discourage people not to report… or to report trivial matters" (2598–2600).

The passage explains what each demands of the regulator. Performance-based rules "require regulators to have the skills and expertise to assess the performance of the system, rather than to merely assess prescriptive compliance" (5012–5014).

## Across the set: what struck me

1. **Two causal registers, kept apart.** CSB and ICAO both separate *general causal theory*, which is always attributed to a named source (Reason, Snook, Hopkins, Kletz, CCPS, CAIB), from *case findings*, which the investigator asserts in its own voice with graded confidence ("likely", "CSB determined", "would have been less likely to occur"). ICAO even hedges its own adopted model (1170–1172). The AI documents I've seen quoted tend to blend the two. A schema might want "causal theory, attributed" and "causal finding about a case" as different kinds of assertion.

2. **Documents declare their own modal force, and it varies within a document.** IAEA codifies shall/should (285–356) and says who the implicit addressee is. HSE moves from "should" to "must" to statute, then disclaims compulsion (497–500). ICAO says it is "purposely avoiding being overly prescriptive" (87–89) and then offers a typology of prescriptive vs performance-based rules (4943–5015). CSB's recommendations add "at a minimum". Modal strength looks like its own dimension, and so does the document's stated self-assessment of it.

3. **Metric validity is the mature discipline's recurring lesson.** Personal vs process safety (CSB 9285–9333, 8843–8845). Lead vs lag (HSE 336–346; ICAO 2326–2373; CSB 9315–9333). Target gaming (ICAO 2121–2123, 2595–2620). Measurement tools that fail on socio-technical systems (ICAO 2766–2774). These sources treat *the indicator's relationship to the hazard* as a first-class object of assertion. That seems directly relevant to how AI documents use evaluations as safety evidence.

4. **Organizational change as a hazard, with a citation chain inside the set.** HSE CHIS7 is wholly about it. IAEA 4.13 requires attention to "the cumulative effects of minor changes" (973–976). ICAO 3.2.7 (1918–1923) covers it too. CSB 10.3 (9647–10051) finds BP's changes causal, cites CHIS7 by name (10034–10044), and identifies a regulatory gap: OSHA's MOC rule did not reach organizational change (9964–9970). The same concept appears as guidance, requirement, manual and finding.

5. **Term collisions worth logging:**
   - *Safeguard*: engineered or procedural barriers, ranked by reliability (CSB 5222–5248). In ICAO ch. 7 (4352–4388) it means *legal protections on disclosure*: "Protective orders, closed proceedings, in camera review". The former officials' letter quoted by NPR speaks of "safeguards against mass domestic surveillance and fully autonomous weapons" (npr-scr 66–67).
   - *Loss of control*: of a reactor core or source (IAEA 274, fn 6) vs organizational over-complexity (HSE 373–375, "prevent loss of control through over-complexity").
   - *Intelligent customer* (HSE 264) vs *informed customer* (IAEA 1199–1201): the same concept under two names.
   - *Supply chain*: IAEA's own suppliers, for whom responsibility is retained (1145–1156), vs the press's statutory "supply chain risk", which is defined around *adversaries* (npr-scr 47–50).
   - The actor names also drift in the press: DoD / Pentagon / Department of War / "@DeptofWar".

6. **Definition styles differ in ways that matter for fidelity.**
   - ICAO keeps a glossary and marks each term's provenance with an asterisk.
   - IAEA outsources meaning to an external glossary and uses footnotes for local terms.
   - CSB imports definitions with citations, and once lays out several rival definitions and picks one while stating why (6957–6975). That is close to what this project does.
   - HSE defines inline, in running prose.

7. **In the press items, the interesting layer is the gap between quote and paraphrase.** The same majority opinion is paraphrased by ABC/Reuters (22–23) and quoted by CNBC (16–18), and the two renderings emphasize different things. "The designation" is really two designations under two authorities (CNBC 33–37). Only NPR carries a funding disclosure. The primary documents (the two rulings, the Pentagon notice, the statute) are not in this set. Anything these items say a court held is a secondary quotation.
