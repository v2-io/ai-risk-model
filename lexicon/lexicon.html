ddd:def/def-ddd-domain-model.udproposed

domain · model · deep model · refactoring toward deeper insight

A domain is what a model is of: a sphere of knowledge, influence or activity. A model describes selected aspects of a domain, so that problems in it can be solved. Because a model selects, one domain can have many models, and that is why Evans needs bounded contexts (bounded context). A deep model keeps the essential and drops the superficial, as judged by the people who know the domain. A model gets deeper through refactoring toward deeper insight: revision prompted by a new understanding of the domain rather than by technical convenience.

domain

ddd:domain

A sphere of knowledge, influence, or activity. The subject area to which the user applies a program is the domain of the software.

Source
verbatimEvans 2015, Definitions, p. vi
Avoid
domainbare: MIT's AI Risk Repository uses "domain" for its seven top-level risk categories, a source term to translate
Invariants
  • It is what a model describes, not the model: a model "describes selected aspects of a domain" (p. vi).
  • The definition carries two readings in its two sentences. The first is absolute: a domain is any sphere of knowledge, influence or activity. The second is relational: something is the domain of a program when a user applies the program to it. — our reading
Not to be confused with
  • MIT's risk "domains" (seven domains, twenty-four subdomains): categories in one source's taxonomy, not spheres of activity. Evans's own nearest term is subdomain ("Generic Subdomains", p. 41), a part of a domain factored into its own model.
  • "For a given domain of interaction" (Gabriel, Keeling et al. 2024) and "problem areas" (Kierans et al.): contexts of use for an alignment judgment, closer to a scope than to a domain in Evans's sense. — our reading

model

ddd:model

A system of abstractions that describes selected aspects of a domain and can be used to solve problems related to that domain.

Source
verbatimEvans 2015, Definitions, p. vi
Avoid
modelbare: in this project's corpus "model" almost always means an AI model
Relations
ofdomain1..1
Invariants
  • It is selective: it describes "selected aspects of a domain" (p. vi). Leaving things out is part of what a model is, not a defect.
  • It is defined by use, not by completeness: it "can be used to solve problems related to that domain" (p. vi).
  • It is a system of abstractions: the abstractions are related to one another. A list of terms is not yet a model. — our reading of "system"
  • Its language and the model change together: "Recognize that a change in the language is a change to the model." (p. 3)
  • "A model is a distillation of knowledge." (p. 39)
  • Many models of one domain are normal: "Multiple models are in play on any large project." (p. 2)
Not to be confused with
  • AI model (the trained network): the usual sense in the corpus, and the reason this lexicon doesn't use the bare word.
  • This project's own "Model" row in CLAUDE.md's five-way table, "an ontology plus claims about how things behave". It differs from Evans's in both directions: it requires claims about behaviour, which Evans does not, and it drops the use ("can be used to solve problems"), which Evans requires. The two should be reconciled or the CLAUDE.md row renamed. — our reading
  • Source model (influx/source-models/): Evans's sense, applied to one source.
  • "Model alpha", "model beta", "model gamma": this project's iterations. Also Evans's sense.

ddd:def/def-ddd-bounded-context.udproposed

context · bounded context · ubiquitous language

A context is the setting that fixes what a word or statement means, so the same words can mean different things in different contexts, and a statement about a model can only be understood in one. A bounded context makes that setting explicit: a boundary within which one model is defined and applies. The ubiquitous language is that model spoken: one language, built around the model, used by everyone working inside the boundary. The three are defined together because each needs the others. A bounded context is where a ubiquitous language holds, and translation, which a ubiquitous language avoids inside a context, is what is needed between contexts (context map).

context

ddd:context

The setting in which a word or statement appears that determines its meaning. Statements about a model can only be understood in a context.

Source
verbatimEvans 2015, Definitions, p. vi
Avoid
contextbare: the agent part "context" (alignment-model/alignment.md) and an LLM's context window share the word
Invariants
  • It attaches to occurrences: it is "the setting in which a word or statement appears" (p. vi), so a word has a context each time it is used, not once.
  • Meaning is relative to it: "Model expressions, like any other phrase, only have meaning in context." (p. 2)
  • It may be implicit. Nothing in this definition requires a boundary to be drawn. That is what distinguishes it from bounded context. — our reading
Not to be confused with
  • The agent part context (alignment.md: "experience & agency; the real-time model") and an LLM's context window: what an agent has taken in during a run, not a setting that fixes meaning.
  • "Depending on the context" in IASR's alignment glossary (2025 and 2026): the ordinary sense, close to this entry.
  • Address theory deliberately avoids "context" for its locality unit ("too loose to carry the locality claim", def-scope). This entry is that loose notion; bounded context is Evans's bounded counterpart.

bounded context

ddd:bounded-context

A description of a boundary (typically a subsystem, or the work of a particular team) within which a particular model is defined and applicable.

Source
verbatimEvans 2015, Definitions, p. vi (repeated p. 28)
Relations
definesmodel"a particular model"1..1
speaksubiquitous language1..1
named-incontext map0..N
Invariants
  • One model per bounded context: "within which a particular model is defined and applicable" (p. vi).
  • Several are expected, not exceptional: "Multiple models are inevitable" (p. 2).
  • Boundaries are made by those who build a model: "Well-defined context boundaries only emerge as a result of intellectual choices and social forces" (p. 38). Where those are absent, there is no boundary to find (big ball of mud).
  • Someone mapping finds the boundaries, and draws them where the model is implicit. A context map covers "each model in play on the project … This includes the implicit models of non-object-oriented subsystems" (p. 29). — our reading that "implicit models" lets a mapper draw a boundary its team never drew
Practices
  • Keep the model consistent inside, and don't track issues outside: "keep model concepts and terms strictly consistent within these bounds, but don't be distracted or confused by issues outside" (p. 2).
  • Set the boundary by organization and artifacts as well as by concept: "Explicitly set boundaries in terms of team organization, usage within specific parts of the application, and physical manifestations such as code bases and database schemas." (p. 2)
Declared ambiguity
  • The description, or the region? The definition says a bounded context is "a description of a boundary". The pattern text uses it as the region itself: "Speak a ubiquitous language within an explicitly bounded context" (p. 1); "Declare a bounded context to have no connection to the others" (p. 37). Both readings are Evans's. This entry leaves the ambiguity declared rather than choosing. If one must be chosen, the region reading fits the relations above, and the description is then the map's record of it. — our reading

ubiquitous language

ddd:ubiquitous-language

A language structured around the domain model and used by all team members within a bounded context to connect all the activities of the team with the software.

Source
verbatimEvans 2015, Definitions, p. vi
Relations
structured-aroundmodel1..1
withinbounded context1..1
Invariants
  • It changes when the model changes, and the reverse: "Recognize that a change in the language is a change to the model." (p. 3)
  • It is what avoids translation inside a context: "Translation blunts communication and makes knowledge crunching anemic." (p. 3)
  • It is built from the model, not taken from an existing dialect: "none of these dialects can be a common language because none serves all needs" (p. 3).
Practices
  • Use it everywhere: "Within a bounded context, use the same language in diagrams, writing, and especially speech." (p. 3) And "in all communication within the team and in the code" (p. 3).
  • "Play with the model as you talk about the system." (p. 3)
  • "Iron out difficulties by experimenting with alternative expressions, which reflect alternative models." (p. 3)
Referenced by domain-model notes, bounded context
Notes on this group
Discussion
  • This project's sources as bounded contexts. CLAUDE.md treats every source as its own bounded context. Most sources' models are implicit in Evans's sense: they don't declare a boundary or a rule for reading their terms, though some do (the EU Code's interpretation clause; plan §1.3, finding 4). So this project draws a source's boundary when it maps the source. The source is not bound by it. — our reading
  • Bounded contexts here are nested. Evans's booklet doesn't nest bounded contexts. This project's sources have scopes inside scopes: SB 53 defines "catastrophic risk" differently in two code sections, and IASR 2026's glossary and body define loss of control differently (plan §1.3, finding 5). The plan proposes defining a bounded context in terms of address theory's scope: a scope whose bindings are one model's terms, kept by one maintainer, under a norm of consistency. Containment edges then give nesting. That is a widening of Evans's term, proposed here, not his. — our reading
  • Consistency is a norm, not an invariant. Evans prescribes strict consistency inside a boundary (see the practices above). Address theory keeps collisions representable because real systems have them (def-binding). The two fit together if a collision inside a bounded context is a recordable failure of that norm, not an impossibility. Self-inconsistent sources (IASR 2026; Anthropic's August 2026 Risk Report) are then contexts with recorded collisions, or several scopes mapped one at a time. — our reading
  • This project's lexicon is its ubiquitous language, scoped to this project's bounded context. It is a declared widening of Evans's definition: the "software" it connects to is the project's records and computed views, and the "team" is Joseph and the agents working with him. — our reading

ddd:def/def-ddd-context-map.udproposed

context map · upstream-downstream · mutually dependent · free

A context map is the view across bounded contexts: every model in play, each context named, and every point of contact between them described. That covers which translations are needed, what is shared, what is kept isolated, and who influences whom. It records the terrain as it is before proposing changes. Evans gives three terms for how much one context's success depends on another's. upstream-downstream is a one-way dependence: the upstream group's actions affect the downstream group's success, and not the reverse. mutually dependent projects succeed or fail together. A free context's delivery is little affected by work anywhere else. The relationship patterns (partnership through separate ways, and the translation patterns from anticorruption layer on) are Evans's catalogue of how teams in these situations actually relate. big ball of mud is in the same section of his booklet, but it describes one context, not a relation between two.

ddd:context-map

A description of the bounded contexts in play and of the points of contact between their models.

Source
modifiedEvans 2015, Context Map, p. 29the pattern's instructions ('Identify each model in play on the project and define its bounded context … Describe the points of contact between the models') restated as a definition
Avoid

Relations

Invariants

upstream-downstream

ddd:upstream-downstream

A relationship between two groups in which the "upstream" group's actions affect project success of the "downstream" group, but the actions of the downstream do not significantly affect projects upstream.

Source
verbatimEvans 2015, Context Mapping for Strategic Design, p. 28without its parenthetical example
Relations
upstreamteam"team" is not yet a lexicon term; Evans says "group" here and "team" in the patterns1..1
downstreamteam1..1
Invariants
  • It holds between groups, not between models or documents: what flows is influence on "project success".
  • It is asymmetric: "The upstream team may succeed independently of the fate of the downstream team." (p. 28)
  • It is defined by dependence of success. Supply is the usual way that dependence arises, but it is not required: Evans names customer/supplier for it (p. 32), and an open host "places the provider of the service in the upstream position" (p. 35), but his own example involves no supply: "If two cities are along the same river, the upstream city's pollution primarily affects the downstream city." (p. 28)
Not to be confused with
  • The AI supply chain's upstream and downstream. The EU AI Act's "downstream provider" (Art. 3(68)) and the GPAI guidelines' "downstream actors" who "may modify a general-purpose AI model", along with the supply-chain literature (Cobbe, Veale & Singh 2023; Hopkins et al. 2025), name positions in a flow of artifacts: who builds on whose model. Since supply is the usual way Evans's dependence arises, the two senses usually pick out the same pairs. They come apart where supply and dependence of success separate: an artifact passed to a party whose success doesn't turn on it, or dependence with no supply at all (the river). A translation records which one a source means. — our reading
  • Lineage. A definition copied from an earlier document (RAISE from SB 53) flows from an upstream text. The derivation is recorded in this project's lineage records. By itself it does not establish this relation, which is about dependence of success. The same copying may also be a context-map relationship (see conformist and its proposed widening). The two are recorded separately. — our reading

ddd:def/def-ddd-relationships.udproposed

partnership · shared kernel · customer/supplier · conformist · separate ways

These five are Evans's patterns for how the teams behind two bounded contexts actually relate, short of building a translation layer (anticorruption layer). They differ in four ways: whose success depends on whose, what is shared, whether anything is translated, and who chooses the arrangement. In a partnership, two mutually dependent teams coordinate their plans without needing to share models. A shared kernel is a small, explicitly bounded part of a model that both teams agree to hold in common and change only together. customer/supplier is an upstream-downstream relation in which the upstream plans for the downstream's needs. conformist is the same relation when it does not: the downstream gives up its own model and adopts the upstream's, so nothing needs translating. separate ways is the declared decision to have no connection at all.

conformist

ddd:conformist

A relationship in which a downstream context adopts the upstream context's model as its own, so that nothing between them needs translating.

Source
modifiedEvans 2015, Conformist, p. 33'Eliminate the complexity of translation between bounded contexts by slavishly adhering to the model of the upstream team.' restated as a definition
Relations
is-aupstream-downstreamalways, in Evans's scope; under the proposed widening below, the dependence of success may be absent0..1
Invariants
  • There is no translation: its point is to "eliminate the complexity of translation between bounded contexts" (p. 33).
  • The downstream inherits the upstream's language with its model: "you will share a ubiquitous language with your upstream team" (p. 33).
  • It is the downstream's choice: "choosing conformity enormously simplifies integration" (p. 33).
  • It costs the downstream its own model: it "cramps the style of the downstream designers and probably does not yield the ideal model for the application" (p. 33).
Conditions
  • "the upstream has no motivation to provide for the downstream team's needs" (p. 33).
Proposed widening, not adopted
  • Not yet part of the term. What makes a relation conformist is adoption without translation. Evans's condition (an unmotivated upstream) is the usual occasion for it, not part of what it is. On this reading, a downstream that conforms for other reasons, such as legal compatibility, is still conformist, and the two sides need not stand in upstream-downstream (dependence of success) at all. This is our widening of Evans's scope, for Joseph's decision; until it is adopted, the term means only what the invariants above say.

ddd:def/def-ddd-anticorruption-layer.udproposed

anticorruption layer · open-host service · published language

When two bounded contexts must communicate and neither will conform to the other, someone translates. Evans gives three shapes for where the translation lives. An anticorruption layer is built by the downstream for itself: it takes the upstream as it is and renders it in the downstream's own model, so the downstream model is not bent toward the upstream's. An open-host service is built by the upstream for everyone: one open protocol that any client can integrate with. A published language is a documented common medium that each side translates into and out of. Evans advises against making either side's own model that medium. The three combine. An open host often speaks a published language, and its clients either conform to it or build anticorruption layers against it.

anticorruption layer

ddd:anticorruption-layer

An isolating layer, built by a downstream context, that provides it with the upstream's functionality in terms of its own model, translating between the two models.

Source
modifiedEvans 2015, Anticorruption Layer, p. 34'As a downstream client, create an isolating layer to provide your system with functionality of the upstream system in terms of your own domain model … Internally, the layer translates in one or both directions as necessary between the two models.' restated as a definition
Avoid
adapterone implementation technique, not the pattern
Relations
built-bybounded contextthe downstream1..1
isolates-frombounded contextthe upstream1..1
Invariants
  • It belongs to the downstream: "As a downstream client, create an isolating layer" (p. 34).
  • It leaves the upstream as it is: it "talks to the other system through its existing interface, requiring little or no modification to the other system" (p. 34).
  • It translates in whichever directions are needed: "the layer translates in one or both directions as necessary between the two models" (p. 34).
  • What it protects against is the downstream model drifting toward the upstream's: "A large interface with an upstream system can eventually overwhelm the intent of the downstream model altogether, causing it to be modified to resemble the other system's model in an ad hoc fashion." (p. 34)
Conditions
  • Cooperation is not available: "when control or communication is not adequate to pull off a shared kernel, partner or customer/supplier relationship, translation becomes more complex. The translation layer takes on a more defensive tone." (p. 34)
  • The upstream's model does not fit: "The models of legacy systems are usually weak (if not big balls of mud), and even the exception that is clearly designed may not fit the needs of the current project, making it impractical to conform to the upstream model." (p. 34)

open-host service

ddd:open-host-service

A protocol, opened by a context to all who need to integrate with it, that gives access to that context as a set of services.

Source
modifiedEvans 2015, Open-host Service, p. 35'Define a protocol that gives access to your subsystem as a set of services. Open the protocol so that all who need to integrate with you can use it.' restated as a definition
Invariants
  • Its provider is upstream of every client: "This places the provider of the service in the upstream position. Each client is downstream, and typically some of them will be conformist and some will build anticorruption layers." (p. 35)
  • It is one protocol for all, not one per client. Idiosyncratic needs get a one-off translator outside it, "so that the shared protocol can stay simple and coherent" (p. 35).
Conditions
  • "When a subsystem has to be integrated with many others, customizing a translator for each can bog down the team." (p. 35)
Practices
  • "Enhance and expand the protocol to handle new integration requirements, except when a single team has idiosyncratic needs." (p. 35)

published language

ddd:published-language

A well-documented shared language that can express the necessary domain information as a common medium of communication, into and out of which each context translates.

Source
modifiedEvans 2015, Published Language, p. 36'Use a well-documented shared language that can express the necessary domain information as a common medium of communication, translating as necessary into and out of that language.' restated as a definition
Invariants
  • It is documented and shared: "a well-documented shared language" (p. 36).
  • It is a medium of translation: each context translates "as necessary into and out of that language" (p. 36).
Conditions
  • "The translation between the models of two bounded contexts requires a common language." (p. 36)
Practices
  • Don't make either side's own model the medium. Evans's advice is hedged: "Direct translation to and from the existing domain models may not be a good solution." His reasons: "Those models may be overly complex or poorly factored. They are probably undocumented. If one is used as a data interchange language, it essentially becomes frozen and cannot respond to new development needs." (p. 36)
Examples
  • "Many industries establish published languages in the form of data interchange standards. Project teams also develop their own for use within their organization." (p. 36)
  • "Published language is often combined with open-host service." (p. 36)

  • This project's translations are an anticorruption layer. Each source is upstream and this project is downstream. The sources are read through their "existing interface", their published text, and never modified, which matches "little or no modification to the other system" and the project's compilation principle. The danger it guards against is a downstream model "modified to resemble the other system's model in an ad hoc fashion". Model beta's adoption of IASR 2026's definitions was a different and deliberate move (conformist, plan §1.2), though it ended in the same place. The per-source translations are the inside of the layer (plan §3.2). It is this project's own ubiquitous language: a model with commitments, not a neutral medium (plan §2: "Neutrality lives in the assertions, not the vocabulary"). Evans's warning applies if it is ever offered as one: a domain model used for interchange "essentially becomes frozen". If others are to integrate with this project's records, a separate published language would be the cleaner route. — our reading

ddd:def/def-ddd-big-ball-of-mud.udproposed

big ball of mud

A big ball of mud is what forms where no one has drawn bounded context boundaries, or where the choices that drew them have lapsed: several models mixed together, definitions ambiguous or contradictory, held together by logic added piece by piece. In this lexicon the term names that condition. Evans's pattern then says what to do about it: draw one boundary around the whole mess, name it, do no careful modeling inside, and watch that it does not spread. Those are practices, kept apart from what the term means, because this project may need the name without the response.

big ball of mud

ddd:big-ball-of-mud

A part of a system where models are mixed and boundaries are inconsistent.

Source
modifiedEvans 2015, Big Ball of Mud, p. 38'parts of systems, often large ones, where models are mixed and boundaries are inconsistent' restated as a definition
Relations
bounded-bybounded contextpresent only once someone has designated it, per the practice below0..1
Invariants
  • Its boundaries are absent or inconsistent. Evans names both: "models are mixed and boundaries are inconsistent", and "systems where there simply are no boundaries" (p. 38).
  • It forms gradually: "The systems are made to work by contingent logic as features are added … Eventually the software congeals into a big ball of mud." (p. 38)
  • It tends to spread into other contexts: "Be alert to the tendency for such systems to sprawl into other contexts." (p. 38)
  • It can be practical: it "is actually quite practical for some situations (as described in the original article by Foote and Yoder), but it almost completely prevents the subtlety and precision needed for useful models" (p. 38).
Conditions
  • "When these factors are absent, or disappear, multiple conceptual systems and mix together, making definitions and rules ambiguous or contradictory." (p. 38; "and mix together" [sic], as in Evans's text) The factors are "intellectual choices and social forces".
Practices
  • "Draw a boundary around the entire mess and designate it a big ball of mud." (p. 38)
  • "Do not try to apply sophisticated modeling within this context." (p. 38)
Not to be confused with
  • Evans's designation sense. Evans also uses the name for the designated region ("designate it a big ball of mud", p. 38). In this lexicon that is a big ball of mud with its boundary drawn, per the practice above, not a second sense of the term.
