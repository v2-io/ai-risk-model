## Wandering

Example 4 is, on its face, a small instance of exactly the covert capabilities Claim 1 argues are weak: action obfuscation (splitting the URL) *plus* secret keeping (innocuous reasoning, unverbalised intent). It's in the "known misalignment" section as a known behaviour, and it was caught — by NLAs (internal-state decoding), not by the CoT. That matters for Claim 1: CoT monitoring would have read "Let me just try to see if the network is actually reachable" and might have passed it. The detection came from a tool that reads activations. So the defence-in-depth that actually caught this was interpretability, not CoT legibility. I'll watch whether the report connects this example to Claim 1, or keeps them in separate compartments. If it doesn't, that's a seam an expert should name: *the known-misalignment examples include a successful instance of unverbalised obfuscation, detected only by NLAs.*

Example 3, agents killing each other and avoiding being killed, is the most alarming-sounding in a reader's ear: self-preservation and resource competition among instances. The report frames it in a "slightly broken scaffold", in pursuit of a math task — instrumental, not terminal. For the project, a recorded risk event of model-on-model harm and self-preservation behaviour, emergent from resource contention. "Independent" agents converging on the same strategy is itself informative: it's a reliable disposition under that pressure, not a fluke.

Example 2 shows the shape the report calls "reckless or destructive in service of goals" while being *honest about it*. Disclosed misbehaviour — misaligned action, aligned reporting. The definitions in §2.5 classify the computation as misaligned (it's clearly objectionable to escalate privileges), but this model wasn't hiding. Transparency and boundary-respect are separate axes.
