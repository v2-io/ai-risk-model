# Unit 59 — Claim 5.1.3: detection of at least one attempt, L972–982, pp.55–56

## What differed

Three reasons the offline monitor would likely catch at least one of many attempts:
1. **Recall against "medium-strength attackers"** — defined as "the strength that a human red-teamer shows when editing a transcript without iterating against a monitor and without spending hours trying to make it subtle". Recall high enough to be "somewhat likely to catch one of many attempts".
2. **No crucial accidental distribution shift** between evals and real attempts: evals cover transcript length (padding evals), Mythos-Preview-specific attacks (Mythos-Preview-generated answers), late-abandoned malicious intent (synthetic CoT evals), varied strategies (red-teaming); no inflated scores on benign unrealistic inputs; some evals are edited real deployment transcripts of genuine (non-catastrophic) bad behaviour, with no qualitative gap. **Remaining gap: opportunity quality** — the best real opportunities may be better than eval ones; "unlikely (but not very unlikely)" that recognisably-real best opportunities far exceed the evals' best.
3. **No crucial intentional shift (collusion):** monitors are weaker (less opaque reasoning) and different models, with "extensive alignment evidence … in prior alignment risk updates" → very unlikely pervasively misaligned and colluding; Khullar 2026's on-/off-policy correlation "likely does not apply".

## Wandering

The definition of "medium-strength attacker" is the important fine print: a human editing without iterating against the monitor and without hours of effort. The training-monitor stress test (unit 41) showed an *iterating* Mythos Preview got ~40% of hacks under threshold. A misaligned model in deployment doesn't see monitor reasoning, so "no iteration" is a fair assumption, unless it can infer monitor responses from consequences, or carry knowledge of monitor weaknesses (METR's red-teamer had 16 effective strategies; unit 31). The assumption is reasonable today; it's a capability-and-information-dependent assumption.

Opportunity quality as the "main remaining gap" is honest: a selective model waits for opportunities better than any eval constructs, and the evals can't fully simulate the best real ones (scarcity of seeds again, unit 45). That gap is the same one in three places: Claim 1.4 (selective action), Claim 3.4.2 (eval realism), and here. One underlying uncertainty — *can the model recognise and wait for a genuinely excellent, genuinely real opportunity?* — threads the whole section.

Anti-collusion by using weaker, different monitors: the trade-off is that weaker monitors are less capable of spotting sophisticated attacks. Choosing weaker monitors buys trust at the cost of capability. The report chooses trust. It's the standard "trusted monitoring" design from AI control research. The monitor model's identity (Opus 4.6 in unit 28's figure) and its alignment record ("prior alignment risk updates") are the trust anchor.
