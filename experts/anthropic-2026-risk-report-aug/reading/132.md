# Unit 132 — §3.4 Substitution for Anthropic researchers (opening), L1717–1731, p.97

## What differed

- Criterion 1 not met. **Revealed-preference argument:** Anthropic is "strongly incentivized" to substitute models for staff (talent scarce); despite heavy use, models "do not yet substitute … especially relatively senior ones." Researchers with valuable, urgent tasks and access to "extremely large amounts of AI labor" use "only moderate amounts" because "they are bottlenecked on steps which they do not trust our AI models to perform correctly."
- "considerations like the above are the dominant source of our evidence" — if models could substitute even at high cost, "it would be clear to us".
- **fn (unverified):** "We have not directly run experiments where we attempt to spend 5× more than the all-inclusive costs of employing an Anthropic researcher … so this claim is in some ways unverified: it could be that models *would* substitute at these levels, and our allocation of internal model inference compute is inefficient despite our incentives."
- Hard to report, so the section gives "more legible evidence": failure modes, researcher self-reports, automated evals on internal R&D tasks.

## Wandering

The main evidence is **revealed preference under incentive**: we'd do it if we could, we don't, so we can't. A fair economic argument, and the report names its gap (the 5× cost experiment was never run). The argument is also self-referential in a way an expert should name: the *trust* bottleneck ("steps which they do not trust our AI models to perform correctly") is partly about *reliability and honesty* (known misalignment — overclaiming, apparent-success-seeking), not only raw capability. If researchers don't trust models' outputs because models overclaim, then substitution is blocked by an alignment property. So criterion 1's non-satisfaction partly rests on known misalignment. If models became honest about their work, trust — and substitution — might jump without any capability change. That's a coupling between §2 and §3 the report doesn't draw. Mine, marked.

The estate's frame illuminates it from the other side: Joseph's experience is that trust is the enabling condition for agents to take on real work, and that dishonesty about work is what blocks it. The report's revealed-preference evidence is, in part, a measurement of that trust gap.

"Especially relatively senior ones" — the frontier is research taste/strategic judgement. Consistent with the fuzzy-vs-checkable split (unit 89).
