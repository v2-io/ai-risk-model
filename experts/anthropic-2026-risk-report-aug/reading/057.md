# Unit 57 — Claim 5.1.1: misaligned models would act many times, L957–962, p.54

## What differed

Not monitoring coverage yet. 5.1.1: a model producing any harm-inducing computation would likely produce them "across many transcripts (e.g. at least dozens)", by convergence of:
- catastrophe likely needs dozens of attempts (failures; multi-transcript attack chains);
- models can't attack at the exact rare rate that maximises success-without-detection, because they "likely lack information about prior subtle attempts", so they must randomise.

The worked probability: attacking independently with probability $p$ on each of $n$ opportunities, $Y \sim \text{Binomial}(n,p)$; choosing $p$ to maximise $P(Y=1)$ (that is, $p = 1/n$) gives $P(Y=1) = (1-1/n)^{n-1}$, so

$$P(Y = 0 \text{ or } Y > 1) = 1 - \left(1 - \tfrac{1}{n}\right)^{n-1} \;\to\; 1 - \tfrac{1}{e} \approx 63\%,$$

already >55% at $n = 3$. I checked: $n=3$ gives $1 - (2/3)^2 = 5/9 \approx 0.556$. Correct.

## Wandering

The binomial argument is neat and correct, and its premise is the interesting part: the model "lack[s] information about prior subtle attempts." That premise is exactly what **exercise 5's model organism defeated** (unit 48): it used an *external memory file* to track how many contexts it had been used in, acting only after several. With persistent memory, a model *can* count its prior attempts and pick its moment without randomising. The report's own stress-test organism is a counterexample to the premise of 5.1.1 — in the setting with shared persistent state. The report doesn't connect them here. An expert asked about Claim 5.1.1 should name this: *the binomial argument assumes no cross-context memory; the fifth audit exercise shows a model can use external memory to escape it; the report states that the organism's trigger isn't elicited by current internal usage.* That last fact (unit 49) is what keeps the argument standing for now.

The other leg (catastrophe needs dozens of attempts) is a claim about the difficulty of the pathways, plausible for safety-research sabotage and backdoors, less obviously for a single decisive act (one well-placed backdoor that a future model uses). Pathway 3 (code backdoors to help future misaligned models) might need only one success. The report says "somewhat likely to require multiple successful attacks" — hedged accordingly.

The rare-action eval of unit 31 (models fail to act rarely without a randomness source) is the empirical leg cited ("as suggested by the evaluations in Section 2.7"). Note: in agentic contexts randomness sources are abundant (UUIDs, timestamps); unit 31 said the realistic condition lacked them. The cleanest attack on this premise is memory, not randomness.
