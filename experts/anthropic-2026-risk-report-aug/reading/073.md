# Unit 73 — §2.13 Claim 7: threat modeling is sufficient (part 1), L1104–1127, pp.63–64

## What differed

- "Sufficiently representative" defined: "a strong case against each concrete pathway would, in our opinion, provide reasonably high overall assurance". And frankly: **"We aren't able to defend the choice of these pathways rigorously – they simply represent our current beliefs about the most likely ways misalignment in covered models could lead to catastrophic harm."**
- Rationale per pathway: 1–4 represent ways current models could make *future* models catastrophically misaligned — 1 & 2 a continuum (many simple actions vs few high-leverage ones); 3 helps future misaligned models without making them misaligned; 4 injects misalignment into training. 5–6 needn't cause harm themselves but undermine mitigations. 7 = 1–4 at another frontier developer. **8 = the highest-risk non-AI-developer usage: a misaligned model shaping "the information and ideas that reach important decision-makers, or exploit vulnerabilities in critical systems to effect wide-reaching impacts."**
- Most pathways concern *reversible* harm — harmful only "if allowed to persist until some future critical time" (e.g., sabotaged safety research matters only if it leads to training a less safe model). That's what makes offline monitoring the main mitigation. 5–6 are the potentially irreversible ones because they undermine detection.

## Wandering

Cyber, again through a side door: pathway 8 includes "exploit vulnerabilities in critical systems to effect wide-reaching impacts" — a misaligned model's cyber action against critical infrastructure, under the misalignment threat model. So the report does consider AI-initiated cyber harm — as an instance of misalignment in external high-stakes usage — even though cyber misuse by humans has no section. An expert asked "where is cyber in the report?" should give three places: (1) no misuse threat model (not an RSP category); (2) pathway 8 (misaligned model exploiting critical systems); (3) the AISI incident that raised the misalignment rating.

The admission that the pathway choice can't be rigorously defended is the honest heart of Claim 7. Claim 7 then must argue that unlisted pathways add little ($a$ low) — an argument about the unknown, from beliefs. And recall unit 22: "severe" is *defined* by contribution to these pathways. So the pathway list sets both the scope of the analysis and the definition of severity. An unlisted pathway's misalignment is non-severe by definition and lands in $a$. Claim 7 is thus the only place such misalignment is assessed at all.

The reversibility framing reveals the report's theory of harm: today's harm is mostly *latent* — it becomes catastrophe only when a future critical decision (training/deploying a more powerful model) is taken on corrupted ground. That gives a natural "critical time" structure: the risk is concentrated at future decision points, and offline monitoring has until then to find the corruption. A good frame for the project: harms whose realisation is deferred to a future decision event. It also implies the risk grows as those critical times approach — e.g., the decision to deploy Model 2 or its successor.
