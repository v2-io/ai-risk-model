# Unit 30 — Claim 1.3: action obfuscation, L633–635, p.34

## What differed

The report itself uses the hidden-CoT SHADE-Arena condition (which I'd flagged in unit 27) as the evidence for action obfuscation: "when models can only be detected via their actions, stealth success rates are higher, though still low in absolute terms (10–15% for recent Claude models)." And argues it's "an artificially easy setting": the model reasons freely about evasion in a hidden CoT, whereas in deployment it would have to obfuscate *without* legible reasoning about how. Partial offset acknowledged: crafted reasoning might help by rationalising actions to fool a monitor. "we believe this remains a generous setting."

So my unit-27 concern is answered by structure: Claim 1.1 (can't reason opaquely) + Claim 1.3 (even with free hidden reasoning, actions are caught ~85–90% of the time). Together: to succeed covertly in deployment, a model needs both opaque reasoning *and* obfuscation. The conjunction is what makes the argument.

## Wandering

"Low in absolute terms" for 10–15% is a judgment call worth flagging. Per attempt, one success in seven to ten is not negligible if a model gets many opportunities (thousands of internal agentic tasks). What matters is the *per-attempt detection* that leads to response: if 85–90% of attempts are caught and caught attempts trigger investigation and shutdown, a misaligned model would most likely be caught before succeeding repeatedly. So the argument really rests on detection → response, not on the success rate alone. That's Claim 5's territory (mitigation). An expert asked "is 10–15% low?" should say: low for a single attempt only if detection of the other 85–90% reliably triggers a response; the report's argument relies on that.

Also, in SHADE-Arena the model is *told* to do the side task, which means it wasn't selecting a good opportunity — Claim 1.4 selective action covers that. Real misbehaviour would choose its moments, possibly raising the success rate. The conjunction cuts both ways.

Claim 1.4 selective action: evidence about whether models can tell real high-stakes opportunities from evals/honeypots — eval-awareness rates, honeypot discrimination tests; then the aggregation of Claim 1.
