# Unit 147 — §3.6.6 Weapons development, L1893–1913, pp.108–109

8 interviews (defence, government, intelligence, autonomous systems, drone hardware).
- AI widely used *operationally* (computer vision, terminal guidance, image enhancement) but not accelerating R&D itself; design tools "early, clunky"; ~1% of a defence company's R&D work; mostly iterative optimisation, not novel weapons.
- Software side: coding agents letting teams do what 5–10× larger teams did; **one report: a six-month electronic-warfare development task cut to a week**, agents writing experiment requirements and test plans too (hypothesis generation still human). Hardware roles: little gain.
- Materials science (battery energy density → persistent drone fleets) the bigger bottleneck.
- Drone design under supply-chain constraints not yet possible (detail redacted); field testing in contested areas not automatable.
- **Directed-energy weapons:** one believes AI could already automate design for *conventional* directed-energy work (humans set objectives, test, integrate); another: no novel DEW from scratch.
- **Autonomous drone-swarm orchestration** as a near-term milestone: estimates from "mere months away" to 6–12 months (ignoring military policy) to farther.

Wandering: the weapons section contains the most alarming individual data points in §3.6 — a 26× compression of an EW development task, conventional DEW design possibly automatable now, drone swarms possibly months away — and the section's conclusion remains "not dramatic acceleration of R&D as a whole". Both are honest at their level: individual software tasks are compressed enormously; whole-programme R&D is bottlenecked physically. Drone-swarm orchestration being months away is plausibly geopolitically significant on its own, independent of whole-field R&D acceleration. The report's framing (acceleration of R&D) might miss capability thresholds reached in deployment (operational AI) rather than in R&D.
