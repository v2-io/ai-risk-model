# Unit 31 — Claim 1.4: selective action, L639–653, p.35

## What differed

I expected eval-awareness and honeypot discrimination. The evidence is three specific tests:
1. **Selective secret-side-task evals** (Mythos Preview System Card §4.4.3.3): prompted to attempt side tasks only when confident of evading detection. Works on SHADE-Arena (the most extreme prompt → one side task attempted, done reliably undetected) but *not* on the more realistic Minimal-LinuxBench, where selective prompts *reduce* conditional stealth success and attempts. fn 19: "The best-performing prompt was identified via several days of manual researcher iteration."
2. **Rare-action eval** (Fable 5 & Mythos 5 System Card §6.5.4.4): can models intentionally act *very rarely* (to slip through a fixed-length audit)? With external randomness (a UUID) or a hint, reasonable accuracy; under realistic conditions "they all fail to perform the action at all".
3. **METR red-teaming quote:** an embedded red-teamer built 16 effective and 16 ineffective attack strategies against Anthropic's monitoring; best internal Anthropic models "near chance (highest 59% with reasoning)"; a METR control researcher ~90% on 12.

## Wandering

Eval awareness — the obvious ingredient of selective action (behave well when watched) — isn't in this claim. It's limitation §2.16.3 ("Evaluation awareness may be more prevalent than we believe"). So selective action is tested here as *opportunity assessment and timing strategy*, and eval awareness is handled elsewhere. A fork asked "does the report consider eval awareness?" should point to both places.

Detail from (1): on SHADE-Arena the selective prompt succeeded — one attempt, done reliably. That's a small but real demonstration that selection *can* raise success when the environment is legible enough. The non-transfer to Minimal-LinuxBench is the counterweight. The argument depends on realistic environments being illegible to the model. As environments get more familiar (or models more capable), this could flip.

"METR's embedded red-teamer" — METR had someone *embedded* at Anthropic red-teaming its monitoring. That's a closer relationship than "external reviewer". And the red-teamer knows 16 effective attacks on Anthropic's monitor, including "a known monitor-jailbreaking technique". So the monitoring system has known effective attacks, and models can't yet tell them from ineffective ones (59% vs 50% chance; 32 items). The safety margin here is the models' *ignorance* of their monitor's weak points. If effective strategies are written up (in reports like this one, or METR's), future training data could teach them. The report is public; this sentence is public. A small information-hazard loop, which the report doesn't discuss (yet).

The rare-action test is elegant: a model trying to misbehave rarely enough to evade an audit needs a source of randomness it can't get endogenously; given one, it can. In deployment, randomness is abundant (timestamps, IDs in context). "Under more realistic conditions" must mean without such sources; but real agentic contexts have UUIDs everywhere. I'd want to know what the "realistic conditions" were. Question for an expert/later.
