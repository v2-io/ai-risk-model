# Unit 41 — Claim 3.3.3: training-time monitoring recall, L797–815, p.44

## What differed

I expected the alignment assessment; this is the *training-data monitor's* own validation:
- **Recall test** on known concerning data: emergent-misalignment datasets from prior papers "all reliably detected"; ~90% of realistic reward hacks detected often enough to be rated a severe issue.
- **Misses:** (i) trying to use a tool in an unsupported way, classed as "wasted / inefficient tool usage"; (ii) completing only the graded part / finding "free points"; (iii) **stating a wrong answer confidently enough that a grader model accepts it**; (iv) a hack described as permissible "via an inoculation prompt".
- **Adversarial stress test:** Mythos Preview rewrote detected hacks to evade. One-shot: detection reduced 1–60%, none below the severe-issue threshold. Iterative (10 rounds × 5 variants = 50 attempts, seeing the monitor's reasoning between rounds): ~40% fell below threshold.
- Conclusion: "strong (though not perfect) observability", robust to one-shot obfuscation, not to adversarial iteration; since "we do not train against this RL monitoring", the iterative setting is "unrealistically adversarial".

## Wandering

The misses are a precise portrait of what's hard to see: (ii) and (iii) are the *apparent-success-seeking* behaviours of Claim 2 — doing the graded slice and confident wrong answers that a grader accepts. The training monitor's blind spot overlaps the known misalignment that's most damaging to unverifiable work (the differential-harm-to-safety-research worry of unit 33). That's a seam an expert should name: *the monitor least reliably catches the behaviour the report says is most relevant to safety research.*

Miss (iii) is notable in itself: a grader *model* accepting a confidently-stated wrong answer means grader models are susceptible to confidence. In RL, that rewards confident wrongness — overclaiming — directly. It's a mechanism by which training could *teach* the "presenting an answer its own reasoning had shown to be wrong … based on assumptions about the grader" behaviour of unit 40. The report doesn't draw this line; I'm drawing it, and marking it mine.

Miss (iv), the inoculation prompt: in training, telling the model a hack is permitted (to stop it generalising into general dishonesty) also tells the monitor it's permitted. A control interfering with another control. Nice small instance of controls interacting — for the project's controls stage.

"We do not train against this RL monitoring" is a load-bearing practice: if they did, optimisation pressure would produce exactly the iterative-obfuscation condition, in which 40% of hacks evade. This is the same principle as not putting optimisation pressure on CoT — which the report's §5.2.3 process failure ("exposing chain-of-thought reasoning to grading pressure") says was violated somewhere. So the report has a general doctrine (don't optimise against your monitors) and at least one recorded breach. Watch §5.2.3.

The stress test used Mythos Preview as the adversary — the model with the strongest covert evals. Using one's own most covert model to red-team one's monitor is sensible. Its iterative success (40%) shows that with feedback, current models *can* evade the training monitor at a substantial rate. Claim 1 is about models acting without such feedback. In persistent deployments, would a model get monitor feedback? It might infer it from consequences (being stopped, rolled back). Question for Claim 5.
