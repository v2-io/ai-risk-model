# Unit 72 — §2.12 Claim 6: mitigation under pervasive misalignment, L1096–1102, p.63

Conditioning on pervasive misalignment undermines the mitigation arguments, claim by claim: 5.1 partly holds (multiple attempts likely, "many" less so); 5.1.2 and 5.1.3 "significantly weaker"; **5.2 "likely wouldn't apply at all"** (sandbagging could be quite harmful if pervasive unknown misalignment exists, since that would signal worse models imminent); 5.3 weaker (a model that hides pervasive misalignment could do more with exfiltration/rogue deployment); 5.4.1 holds, 5.4.2 weaker. Conclusion: "our belief that risk from pervasive misalignment is very low comes primarily from a belief that it is very unlikely, rather than that we are well positioned to mitigate any harm it might cause."

Differed: a more systematic conditional walk-through than I expected — each mitigation subclaim re-assessed under the condition. This is the cleanest sensitivity analysis in the report.

Wandering: The last sentence is the one to quote when asked "what would happen if Anthropic is wrong about pervasive misalignment?": *the defence would be weak; the low risk rating rests on the probability being very low.* So $R_P$ is essentially $P_P$ times a large number: Claim 3 is doing nearly all the work for this row, and Claim 3 converges on Claim 1. Hence the whole misalignment conclusion is very sensitive to Claim 1, as the report's own "Looking forward" (Table 1.2.A) says: "Our current arguments rely on models' limited covert capabilities."

Claim 5.2's reasoning under the condition is subtle: sandbagging becomes harmful not because of what it slows *now*, but because pervasive hidden misalignment would mean dangerous successors are near, making safety research urgent — so the stakes argument (5.2.1: no project is time-critical) collapses exactly when it matters. The stakes premise is conditional on the world being safe. Good catch by the authors.
