# Unit 205 — §4.5.8.2.2 Vendor traffic without blocking bio classifiers, L2622–2636, pp.148–149

- **May 2025 (first deployment of CB safeguards) to April 2026**: all traffic through the human-feedback data-collection systems ran **without blocking biological classifiers**.
- Pool: **~50,000 people**, vetted only by vendors (many of whose screening "did not have screening processes capable of stopping even CB-1 threat actors" before the strengthened requirements); **~133M exchanges**; most with open-ended conversation access.
- Cause: a flag meant for internal use only, which disabled **both blocking and the logging of flags** — so would-be flags were never recorded or reviewed.
- Transcripts retained for almost all (except ~1% of one platform where users didn't click "submit").
- Review: **Claude Sonnet 5** as a prompted classifier over all human turns against a CB-1 constitution (fn 74): **1,197** transcripts flagged "high"; **757** from internal Anthropic teams on the same infrastructure; all but **62** of the rest from deliberate red-teaming exercises. Manual review of all 62 plus 30 random red-teaming transcripts: no clearly concerning misuse giving meaningful uplift (a handful of dual-use or academic-level red-teaming conversations). Longest flagged external transcript 240k tokens; 75th percentile 2,102 (fn 76).
- Judged "very unlikely" to have meaningfully raised CB risk: (1) a would-be weaponeer couldn't send more than a few dozen detailed high-harm queries without human review very likely catching one (sentence continues); (2) conversations mostly short; (3) §4.2.3's sustained-access requirement.

## Wandering

This is the incident behind CB-1's raised rating, and the numbers are large: **~11 months, ~50,000 vendor-vetted people, ~133M exchanges, no blocking and no logging of flags**. The report's review is substantial and its conclusion careful. But the structure of the failure is what an expert must convey: a single internal configuration flag silently disabled a core safeguard *and its own telemetry*. That's why no monitor noticed — the monitoring's input was switched off by the same flag. A control that can disable its own observability is a single point of failure for detection. For the project: a canonical risk-event pattern (control and its monitoring share a failure mode).

The reasoning in (1) is retrospective and counterfactual: "could not have sent more than a few dozen detailed high-harm queries without our human review being very likely to examine at least one" — but during the gap, nothing was logged, so there was no real-time human review; the claim must mean the *retrospective* review would have caught them (it reviewed all flagged non-red-team transcripts). That's sound for detection after the fact, not for prevention at the time. And the review's flagger is a model (Sonnet 5) applying a CB-1 constitution — CB-2-style queries indistinguishable from research biology (unit 177) wouldn't be flagged by a CB-1 constitution. The report's conclusion is reasonable for CB-1-style misuse; for CB-2-style research-looking queries, the review's sensitivity is lower by the report's own dual-use admission. An expert should note that limitation, which the report doesn't state here.

The pool size (50,000) alone makes this a serious exposure: the vetting was the vendors', and many vendors' screening couldn't stop CB-1 actors. The report states that plainly.

Predict next: the rest of the reasoning, remediation (how flags are now governed), then §4.6 Overall assessment.
