## Wandering

- This section has a different author from the statute that follows. The Legislative Counsel's Digest is written by the Office of Legislative Counsel. It's a nonpartisan summary, and it isn't law. California courts do sometimes cite it as evidence of legislative intent, but it has no operative force. For this project the digest is a **source within the source**, with its own role: a summary by a careful drafter's office of what existing law is and what the bill changes. It will say "Existing law …" and then "This bill would …". The "existing law" paragraphs describe a baseline the statute itself never states. Those are claims about California law outside SB 53, such as the existing whistleblower statutes and the existing Department of Technology mandates. Assertions extracted from this document need a role marker: **digest (Legislative Counsel's summary)** versus **enacted text**. A search hit in the digest is not "what SB 53 says". This is probably the most important thing for a fork to get right on retrieval tasks. Digest text will rank high for many queries because it's plain-language and keyword-dense.
- The digest also tends to compress. Where it paraphrases a definition, it may drop qualifiers. A comparison between the digest's version of "catastrophic risk" and the statute's would be a nice small instance of the project's "precision that a summary loses" thesis, inside one document, with an authoritative summarizer.
- The digest runs in the conditional ("would"), because it was written before passage and not revised. So a chaptered statute's digest says "This bill would require…" even though the bill is now law. Grammatical mood isn't evidence of anything here.
