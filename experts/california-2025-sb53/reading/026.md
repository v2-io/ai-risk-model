# Unit 26 · L74 · p.3

> (f) As industry actors conduct internal research on their technologies' impacts, public trust in these technologies would significantly benefit from access to information regarding, and increased awareness of, frontier AI capabilities.

## What differed

None of my predictions. This finding is about **information asymmetry**: industry researches internally, and the public would trust more if it knew more. The beneficiary named is *public trust in these technologies*. Not public safety.

## Wandering

- **The purpose stated is trust, which tilts toward adoption.** "Public trust in these technologies would significantly benefit" means more information, so more trust, so more uptake. That's a pro-industry rationale for transparency: transparency serves *the technology's* legitimacy. The safety rationale (information lets the public and government spot and respond to dangers) hasn't been stated yet. If the findings never state it plainly, then the liberal-construction purposes lean toward trust and innovation. Strictly speaking, findings (a)–(f) haven't yet named a single harm. Six findings in, the statute that regulates "catastrophic risk" hasn't said what it fears.
- "**Frontier AI capabilities**" is the first time "frontier" appears in the enacted text, and it's attached to *capabilities*. So the public should know about capabilities (what models can do), not only risks. That frames transparency as being about the *frontier* itself, the state of the art. It connects to transparency reports at deployment: release-time capability disclosure.
- "Internal research on their technologies' impacts" foreshadows the internal-use assessments (digest, u8). The asymmetry is that labs know things about their models that nobody else knows. This is the closest the findings have come to the actual safety logic. The JCPWG made this point more sharply, from memory: that a window for evidence-informed policy closes as capabilities advance, and that the industry has information the public doesn't.
- From a reader's chair, I'm starting to feel the drafters' care in how they word the purpose. Every finding so far could be signed by an AI company's policy team. That was probably the point: SB 53 passed with Anthropic's endorsement, and with weaker opposition than SB 1047. Findings this conciliatory are what you write when you need the industry not to fight you. For anyone using SB 53 as evidence of "what California thinks about AI risk", the findings are a *negotiated* text. Its own risk theory may sit in the definitions and duties more than in the findings.
