# Unit 9 · L38 · p.1

> The TFAIA would exempt from the California Public Records Act a report of a critical safety incident submitted to the Office of Emergency Services, a report of assessments of catastrophic risk from internet use, and a covered employee report made pursuant to the whistleblower protections described below.

## What differed

- None of my three predictions: the next topic is confidentiality (a Public Records Act exemption), not incident timing or penalties.
- **"from internet use"**. In the previous unit, the same digest twice said "internal use". This is almost certainly a typo for "internal use" in the digest itself. OCR wouldn't turn "internal" into "internet": this is web text, not OCR, and the substitution is a word-level slip, not a character-level one. So the digest has an error that is a real word, plausible, and wrong. A keyword search for "internal use" would miss this sentence; a search for "internet use" would find a phantom concept. **A small specimen for the project's fidelity principle:** quote it verbatim, and record our reading ("internal" intended) beside it, not in place of it.

## Wandering

- **The PRA exemption implies a constitutional findings section.** Under the California Constitution (Art. I, §3(b)(7), from Prop 59 and Prop 42, if I recall), a statute that limits public access to government records must carry legislative findings that justify the limitation. So I predict [inference, fairly confident] a section near the end of the bill: "The Legislature finds and declares that Section ___ of the Business and Professions Code, as added by this act, imposes a limitation on the public's right of access … In order to protect …, it is necessary that …". That section is another non-operative but interpretively weighty part, the Legislature's own stated reasons for secrecy. It's also one of the few places a statute says *why* in its own voice. Along with the SEC. 1 findings, if there are any.
- Three confidential channels: incident reports, internal-use assessments, and whistleblower reports. Taken together, the confidential layer carries most of the *actual risk information* SB 53 creates. Published frameworks and transparency reports are the public layer, and they're likely to be redacted for trade secrets and security. So **what the public learns under SB 53 is mainly the developer's self-description, and what the government learns is the incident and assessment stream**. Which raises a question someone should ask: does OES publish anything aggregated? I vaguely recall an annual anonymized report starting in 2027. A question for later units.
- "Covered employee" is a new defined term, and it belongs to the whistleblower part (the Labor Code). Its scope will matter: employees only, or contractors too? Employees of a frontier developer, or of a large frontier developer? Responsible for assessing risk, or anyone? I recall "covered employee" means an employee responsible for assessing, managing or addressing risk of critical safety incidents, a narrower class than "all employees". If so, whistleblower protection here is role-gated. **Watch.**
- The digest's order follows the bill's sections, not importance: framework, then internal-use summaries, the OES mechanism, the PRA exemption, and only then the whistleblower part. So reading order is drafting order.
