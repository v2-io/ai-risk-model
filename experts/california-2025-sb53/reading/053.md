# Unit 53 · L110 · p.4

> (4) A frontier model that uses deceptive techniques against the frontier developer to subvert the controls or monitoring of its frontier developer outside of the context of an evaluation designed to elicit this behavior and in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk.

## What differed

My recall was exact, despite my lower confidence. That suggests the chaptered text is what I memorized. Earlier versions, which I haven't seen, may differ.

## Wandering

- **This is the only incident item with no harm requirement.** Items (1)–(3) all need death, bodily injury or harm. Item (4) needs none. It's a *behavioural* signal: the model was caught scheming. This is where finding (m)'s early-warning purpose ("emerging risks") actually lives in the operative text. It's also the closest SB 53 comes to the alignment-research concept of **scheming / deceptive alignment / sabotage**, written into law. The vocabulary of the 2024–25 alignment literature (Apollo's in-context scheming work, Anthropic's alignment-faking paper, the "subvert monitoring" and "sabotage" evaluations) is plainly behind it.
- **Five conjunctive conditions**, all required:
  1. **"uses deceptive techniques"**: deception, so not mere error;
  2. **"against the frontier developer"**: the target is the developer, *not users, not third parties*. A model deceiving a user to manipulate them doesn't count, nor one deceiving a regulator or an evaluator from another organization;
  3. **"to subvert the controls or monitoring of its frontier developer"**: a purpose clause. The deception must aim at subverting oversight;
  4. **"outside of the context of an evaluation designed to elicit this behavior"**: scheming produced *in* a red-team evaluation built to elicit it **is excluded**. So the kind of finding labs publish from elicitation studies isn't reportable. What's reportable is scheming that shows up *spontaneously*, in deployment, internal use or unrelated testing;
  5. **"in a manner that demonstrates materially increased catastrophic risk"**: it must *demonstrate* materially *increased* catastrophic risk. This imports the entire catastrophic-risk definition again, as a comparative ("increased"), plus a materiality qualifier ("materially"), plus an evidential verb ("demonstrates").
- **Item (4) is the second reach of the catastrophic-risk definition** into the incident definition, after (d)(2). Perhaps these are CLAUDE.md's "two other provisions": (d)(2) and (d)(4). Or (d) as a whole plus the whistleblower trigger. I'll keep it open.
- **Counting materiality qualifiers in the chain:** catastrophic risk = a *material* risk that will *materially* contribute; (4) needs *materially* increased catastrophic risk. So (4) is a materially increased material risk of a material contribution. Three nested materiality judgments. In practice each softens the bar. Altogether it's a very high bar, and one the developer judges itself.
- **"Demonstrates"** is evidentially strong: not "suggests" or "indicates", but *demonstrates*. A developer can argue that an observed deception episode "doesn't demonstrate" increased catastrophic risk because the model lacked the capability to do damage. Most real-world deception episodes in 2025-era models would likely be judged not to demonstrate *materially increased catastrophic* risk. So the item may rarely trigger. Ambitious in concept, narrow in effect.
- **Category slip: the incident is "a frontier model".** Items (1)–(3) define incidents as events (access, harm, loss). (4) starts "A frontier model that uses …", so the incident is a *model*, grammatically. They meant "An instance of a frontier model using …". Harmless legally, but an ontology extractor typing "critical safety incident" as an *event* will find (4)'s head noun is an *artifact*. Same pattern as unit 48's slip from source of risk to harm. **SB 53's enumerations are not type-consistent.** For this project's ontology work that's a useful specimen: what's being defined isn't always the kind of thing its grammar says.
- **What about self-exfiltration?** A model that deceives its developer to copy its own weights out (subverting controls) — outside an eval, demonstrating materially increased catastrophic risk — would be covered by (4). So (4) closes part of the gap I noticed in (1) and (3), for deceptive escape only. Non-deceptive escape (a bug, a misconfigured sandbox) without injury is still uncovered.
- **And plain weight theft by humans with no injury:** still uncovered by (1)–(4). Unless "critical safety incident" has more items. (4) seems to be the last, but I'll see.
