# Unit 224 · L312 · p.12 · the last

> Information in critical safety incident reports, assessments of risks from internal use, and reports from covered employees may contain information that could threaten public safety or compromise the response to an incident if disclosed to the public.

## What differed

The stated interests are **public safety** and **incident response**. My prediction led with trade secrets, cybersecurity and national security (the value set in u100, u115, u118, u141). None of those appears here.

## Wandering

- **The constitutional justification leaves out the industry reasons.** Everywhere else, the bill's confidentiality value set is trade secrets, cybersecurity, public safety and national security. In the one place the Constitution requires the Legislature to *justify* secrecy, it names only public safety and the integrity of the incident response. The reasons that protect the developer (trade secrets, its own cybersecurity) aren't offered as constitutional grounds. That fits: "protecting a company's trade secrets" is a weaker justification for limiting public access to government records than "protecting the public". So **the statute's stated reason for confidentiality is public protection, while the operative redaction and transmission rules also protect commercial interests.** A source expert asked "why are SB 53 reports confidential?" should give both: the *constitutional* rationale (public safety, incident response, here) and the *operative* considerations (the wider value set, in the redaction and transmission rules).
- **"Assessments of risks from internal use"** gives the internal-use phrasing a fifth form: "risks" here, not "catastrophic risk". That's the last instance of the drift that ran from the digest (u8) to here.
- **"May contain … could threaten"**, the hedge again, a double modal. The findings justify secrecy by what *might* be in the reports. The bill ends where it began, in the modal register: risk spoken of at one or two removes, never asserted.
- **"Compromise the response to an incident"** is a rationale I hadn't anticipated. Disclosure could tip off attackers (in a weight-theft case) or cause panic or interference while authorities respond. It's the OES emergency-management frame (u8) returning at the end: the confidential channel exists so the state can respond.

## Closing thought, as a reader

SB 53 turned out to be two bills and a half:
- **the TFAIA**: a disclosure-and-self-commitment regime with narrow, layered definitions and penalties for large developers only;
- **the Labor Code chapter**: a whistleblower regime with *broader* definitions (foundation models; property harm in weight theft), real private remedies, and arguably the strongest enforcement in the bill;
- **CalCompute**: a dormant study, waiting on an appropriation;
- **uncodified closing sections**: liberal construction, severability, cumulation, two federal carve-outs, local preemption, and the PRA findings. None of them appears in the codified code text.

The things I'd most want a fork of me to remember are the ones I didn't know from recall, even though I could recite most of the sentences beforehand:
- "comply with", which the digest leaves out;
- weight theft being reportable only with injury (TFAIA) but with property loss too (Labor Code);
- penalties for large developers only;
- the equity exclusions, in sections apart from the definitions;
- two catastrophic-risk and two critical-safety-incident definitions;
- federal contracts displacing the act;
- seven or so epistemic standards;
- the developer-set parameters;
- the uncodified sections a code reader never sees.

Two of my own structural claims were wrong and later corrected (u191/193 on the Labor Code equity exclusion, u215 on cumulation). Both errors had the same shape: concluding "X lacks Y" before the bill's general provisions had arrived. That's the lesson I'd pass to whoever reads the next source.
