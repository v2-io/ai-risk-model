# CA Report, L250–449: policy landscape, foundations, changes since SB 1047, case studies (internet, tobacco)

## Against SB 53

- **Risk categories.** L300–303 adopt the IASR's three categories, *including systemic risks* (labour, concentration, environment, privacy, copyright). SB 53 finding (j) took malicious use and malfunctions and dropped systemic (u30). The report keeps all three as categories, though its own scope note (L130) sets labour and environment aside as topics. **So SB 53's omission of systemic risk is partly the report's own scoping and partly SB 53 going further** **[reading]**.
- **Marginal risk, against SB 53's public-availability exclusion.** L309: "we recommend that policymakers center their calculus around the **marginal risk**: Do foundation models present risks that go beyond previous levels of risks that society is accustomed to from prior technologies, such as risks from search engines?" SB 53's counterfactual baseline (§22757.11(c)(2)(A), u46) is a *binary* exclusion: information "otherwise publicly accessible in a substantially similar form". That's a crude version of marginal risk. It catches the search-engine case (public information) but not a *degree* of uplift. **Departure: from a graded marginal-risk test to a categorical exclusion** **[reading]**.
- **Novice uplift, against "expert-level assistance".** L319 frames CBRN evidence as risk "for **novices**", quoting Anthropic's ASL-3 ("significantly help individuals … with basic technical backgrounds"), and OpenAI at L321 ("meaningfully help **novices** create known biological threats"). SB 53's scenario (A) is "**expert-level** assistance" (u42), which measures the level of the help, not the uplift to a novice. **The report's evidence is framed as novice uplift; SB 53's definition is framed as expert-level help.** Whether these converge in practice is open. The vocabulary differs, and I'd flagged the gap at u42 before I knew the report's framing **[text for both; the comparison is my reading]**.
- **Evaluation awareness, against SB 53's exclusion of evaluations.** L327: "models can often detect when they are being evaluated, potentially introducing the risk that evaluations could underestimate harm". SB 53's deception incident (d)(4) excludes behaviour "in the context of an evaluation designed to elicit this behavior" (u53). The two fit together: if evaluations *understate* scheming, then scheming seen *outside* evaluations is the strong signal, and SB 53 makes that the reportable case. Or SB 53 discards eval-elicited evidence the report treats as an early warning. **Both readings are open. The report doesn't propose an incident definition here.**
- **"Transparency alone is insufficient"** (L444): "as the tobacco case shows, companies can distort public understanding despite available evidence. Independent verification mechanisms are necessary to validate industry claims". This is the report's thesis against transparency-only regimes. SB 53's finding (p) admits transparency is insufficient. But it names *due care* as the missing piece, not *independent verification*, and imposes neither (u36). **SB 53 heard the "insufficient" half and answered with a different missing piece** **[reading]**.
- **"Absence of targeted legislation does not mean lack of oversight"** (L410): courts and tort law fill the gaps. That's consistent with SB 53 SEC. 5(c) preserving other duties and remedies (u219).
- **Harmonization and federal pathways** (L263): "maintain critical pathways for federal action that provide a comparable degree of protection". SB 53's five federal-deference mechanisms (u221), especially federal equivalence for incident reporting, follow this closely.
- **Start-ups** (L440): "avoiding burdensome requirements that unfairly support incumbent players". That's the basis of SB 53's (k) and its revenue tier.
- **The report describes the original RAISE** (L259): bills in Illinois and New York with "annual third-party audits". The 2026 RAISE (S8828) has none, and neither does SB 53. So the audit element the report noted in other states' bills **dropped out of both enacted laws** **[text: report L259; S8828 read whole, no audit provision]**.

## Wandering

- **"Doubt is our product"** (L434), the tobacco line. The report's case-study method justifies transparency by historical analogy, choosing cases where industry *had* the risk information and hid it. SB 53's internal-use summaries and whistleblower protections answer that worry directly: they get industry's internal knowledge to the state. The report's tobacco lesson fits SB 53 better than its energy lesson ("verify through third-party evaluation"), which SB 53 mostly declined. One lesson of three was adopted fully **[reading]**.
- **The "evidence dilemma"** (L277, quoting the IASR). It's the conceptual root of SB 53's design: act on partial evidence by generating evidence. SB 53's findings (e) and (f) carry the "evidence environment" language. The dilemma itself isn't quoted.
- **The report's evidence on capabilities** (L319–327: o3 outperforming 94% of virologists, ASL-3, the Gemini cyber early warning, scheming) is the empirical basis SB 53 points to in (o) ("advances in AI model capabilities that could pose potential catastrophic risk"). So SB 53's (o) is in effect a citation to L313–327.
- **Insurance** (L279) as a way to make companies internalize externalities. It's absent from SB 53. A mechanism the report raised and the bill didn't take up.
