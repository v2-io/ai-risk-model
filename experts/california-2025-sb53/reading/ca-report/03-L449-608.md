# CA Report, L449–608: energy case, positive examples, §3 Transparency, §3.1, §3.2 third-party, §3.3 whistleblowers (start)

## Where SB 53 follows the report (lineage it doesn't cite)

- **"Comply with" has its root here.** L468: "developing robust policy incentives ensures that developers create **and follow through on** stated safety practices, such as those articulated in safety frameworks already published by many leading companies." SB 53's "write, implement, **comply with**, and … publish" (u67) puts that sentence into law. **The bill's main conduct lever is the report's recommendation** **[reading: close fit of purpose; the wording differs]**.
- **Internal deployment.** L472–478: "oversight is critical for external outputs as well as internal testing and safety controls. Policies that govern internal deployment are common for high-risk emerging technologies … including during training, pre-deployment, and deployment inside frontier AI companies". That's the source of SB 53's internal-use regime: framework item (a)(10), quarterly summaries to OES (u77, u96).
- **"Circumventing oversight mechanisms"** (L472: "including **circumventing oversight mechanisms** designed to ensure their safety") is SB 53 §22757.12(a)(10)'s exact phrase, "risks resulting from a frontier model **circumventing oversight mechanisms**" (u77). **A verbatim phrase lineage** **[text, both]**. It also explains why SB 53 has four different control phrasings (u77): this one came from the report, and the others came from elsewhere.
- **Security practices** (L556): "secure unreleased model weights from both company-internal and company-external exfiltration". SB 53 (a)(7): "secure unreleased model weights from unauthorized modification or transfer by internal or external parties" (u74). A close paraphrase.
- **Safety practices and frameworks** (L554): this matches the framework's purpose.
- **Whistleblower topics beyond violations of law** (L600): "actions that may clearly pose a risk and violate company policies (e.g., releasing a model without following the protocol laid out in a company's safety policy) may not violate any existing laws. Therefore, policymakers may consider protections that cover a broader range of activities, which may draw upon notions of '**good faith**' reporting". SB 53 does both: the danger prong (u195), and turning policy breaches into legal violations through "comply with" (u196). "Good faith" appears in SB 53's internal-channel standard (u202).
- **The Whistleblower Protection Act as the model** (L596). This confirms my u195 lineage guess. SB 53's "specific and substantial danger" comes through the report's framing of the WPA design space.
- **Anonymous channels and board reporting** (L608: "directed at different parties (e.g., a company's board …) … secure (and potentially anonymous) communication"). SB 53 has an anonymous internal channel and quarterly board visibility (u202–203).

## Where SB 53 departs

1. **Third-party assessment is the biggest departure** (L566–590). The report calls third-party risk assessment "**essential**" (L570), for its scale, diversity and independence. It recommends:
   - a **safe harbour for independent researchers**, "analogous to those afforded to third-party cybersecurity testers" (L580);
   - norms and **infrastructure for responsible disclosure**, routing vulnerabilities across the supply chain (L582–590);
   - states leveraging "federal-level AI institutions to conduct standardized risk evaluations" (L576).

   **SB 53 enacts none of these.** Developers describe their approach to third parties and disclose the extent of their involvement (u72, u92). That's all **[text: confirmed across my whole SB 53 reading]**. The deleted "third-party evaluations" in finding (h) (01 note) is the same choice made in the findings.
2. **What gets disclosed** (L552–562). The report lists five areas of transparency:

   | Report area | SB 53 |
   |---|---|
   | Data acquisition | **absent** (left to AB 2013, which the report says isn't enough, L552) |
   | Safety practices | framework (a); summaries in the transparency report (c)(2) |
   | Security practices | framework topic (a)(7) |
   | Pre-deployment testing, with **rigor and independence** (depth and time of access, whether the evaluator is paid, disclosure constraints, L558) | only "the extent to which third-party evaluators were involved" (u92), with no rigor or independence fields |
   | **Downstream impact** (usage reports by hosts and distributors, L562) | **absent**: nothing on usage, adoption or distribution |

   So SB 53 implements two of the five areas fully, one weakly and two not at all **[reading on "weakly"]**.
3. **Who discloses.** The report aims downstream-impact transparency at **distribution channels** (model hosts, inference providers), not developers (L562). SB 53 regulates developers only. Hosts, deployers and distributors aren't actors in SB 53 at all.
4. **Openness.** The report discusses the transparency benefits of open weights and the EU's open-model exemption (L564). SB 53 treats open release as deployment, with no special treatment (u54). It isn't so much a departure from a recommendation, since the report doesn't recommend an exemption. But SB 53 doesn't engage with the question.
5. **Whistleblower coverage.** The report notes most protections "apply to all employees" and asks about contractors (L598). SB 53 narrows protection to role-gated "covered employees" and leaves out contractors (u185, u214). That's narrower than the report's baseline **[reading]**.
6. **Where SB 53 goes further than the report:** the report "deliberately avoid[s] exploring … non-disclosure agreements" (footnote 6, L604). SB 53 bars contracts that prevent protected disclosures (u194, u197). **SB 53 is stronger than the report here.**

## Wandering

- **The report argues that transparency plus verification protects developers from litigation** (L452, L576): companies with verified good practices "would be able to reduce their exposure to lawsuits". SB 53 gives no such shield. SEC. 5(c) preserves every other remedy (u219), and finding (p) points at due care. So **the report's carrot was a liability benefit for verified good actors, and SB 53 kept the stick (disclosure duties) without the carrot** **[reading]**.
- "Race to the top" (L578) assumes *verified* disclosure. SB 53's unverified self-description weakens the race-to-the-top mechanism the report described. Comparisons between labs' frameworks can still happen through journalists and researchers, but nothing official checks them.
- The report's preference for **public-facing** transparency (L550: "citizens, journalists, academics, and civil society are often the first to identify technological harms … policy should often prioritize public-facing transparency") contrasts with SB 53's main risk channels (incident reports, internal-use summaries), which are **confidential and PRA-exempt** (u116). The report also allows disclosure to "a carefully scoped set of actors, such as a government agency" where appropriate (L540). So SB 53's confidential channels fit the report's exception, not its default.
