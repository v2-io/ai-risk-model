## SB 53's definitions-review criteria come from here

L760–766 give three threshold-design considerations:
- **determination time**;
- **measurability**;
- **external verifiability**. Here the report cites New York's earlier AI policy and the "widespread null compliance" seen when "the regulated entity is the one to unilaterally determine if they are in scope" (Wright et al., 2024).

SB 53 §22757.14(b)(3)–(5) puts these into law (u136–138):
- "able to determine, **before** beginning to train or deploy", which is determination time;
- "**complexity** of determining", which is measurability or simplicity;
- "**external verifiability** … by parties other than the frontier developer".

**This is lineage that goes beyond wording: SB 53's whole theory of threshold criteria is the report's §5.** That explains why u138 read as the most methodologically interesting passage in SB 53: it was the report's analysis, made into statute **[text in both; the lineage is my reading, though a strong one]**.

## Where SB 53 departs (§5.2, the report's own guiding principles)

1. **"Generic developer-level thresholds seem to be generally undesirable"** (L774). The report warns that such thresholds "may inadvertently ignore key players" (DeepSeek; Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI are "relatively small" by some measures). It also warns that they "may bring into scope massive, established companies in other industries … since thresholds based on properties of companies may not distinguish between the entire business and the AI-specific subset." SB 53's "large frontier developer" is **annual gross revenue of the whole group, over $500M, from any source** (u64). That's exactly the "customary developer-level metric" the report "caution[s] against". **This is a direct departure from a stated guiding principle** **[text, both]**. The report's earlier, more permissive line (L754: revenue thresholds can shield startups) doesn't undo this. §5.2 is the report's considered principle, and SB 53's tier is a generic one. Unit 64's worry (a pre-revenue, well-funded lab escapes the "large" tier) is the "ignore key players" failure the report predicted.
2. **"Cost-level thresholds such as training compute should not be used alone"** (L776). If they're used at all, they work "best … as an **initial filter** to cheaply screen for entities that may warrant greater scrutiny" (L782). In SB 53, compute (>10^26) is the **sole** test for frontier-model status, and it **decides** coverage; it isn't a screen. SB 53 doesn't combine it with capability evaluations or impact measures. The revenue tier sits on top for "large" only. **This departs from the report's recommended role for compute** **[text, both]**.
3. **There's no flexibility like the EU's Annex XIII and no scientific-panel alert** (L768). The report points to the EU's alternative metrics and its scientific panel, which can flag models outside the thresholds. SB 53 has neither. Only the Legislature can change scope, after Department of Technology recommendations (u129).
4. **"Mechanisms … not only to update specific quantitative values … but also to change the metrics altogether"** (L770). SB 53 has none. The Department of Technology recommends, and the Legislature legislates.
5. **Risk-evaluation thresholds through industry frameworks and harmonized third-party auditing** (L786). SB 53 lets framework thresholds govern *mitigation* inside each lab ((a)(2), u69). It doesn't use evaluation results to define *scope*, and it has no third-party auditing.
6. **§6.4: the final report added "whistleblower protections for non-employees"** (L852). SB 53 protects employees only, and only "covered employees" for the danger prong (u185, u214). **That's a departure from a point the authors deliberately strengthened in the final version.**
7. **§6.4: "immunity provisions" like the ASRS's** (L858). Absent from SB 53.
8. **§6.4: "complexities of proportionately addressing model derivatives"** (L860). SB 53 leaves this unresolved (u63).

## Wandering

- **The feedback summary (§6.2) previews SB 53's choices.** Commenters noted the "immature third-party evaluation ecosystem … lack of evaluation standards" (L820). That's the strongest practical argument for SB 53 *not* mandating third-party assessment, and it was in the record. So SB 53's biggest departure has a reason the report itself recorded: the ecosystem wasn't ready. The report kept its recommendation anyway. The Legislature, under negotiation, didn't **[reading]**.
- **"Adaptive thresholds" had consensus** (L824: "consensus that thresholds cannot be static"). SB 53's thresholds are static in statute, with an advisory review, so it departs from the consensus the report recorded.
- **Regulatory capacity** (L842): commenters doubted California's ability to regulate. That may explain SB 53's light-touch, self-description design, and its routing to an existing agency (OES) instead of a new regulator. New York instead built a new office and funds it from industry assessments (raise/05).
