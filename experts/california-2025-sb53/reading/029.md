# Unit 29 · L77 · p.3

> (i) Incident reporting systems enable monitoring of the post-deployment impacts of artificial intelligence.

## What differed

My first prediction: the incident-reporting finding. What I didn't expect is that it's tied to **post-deployment** specifically.

## Wandering

- **There's a tension between finding (i) and the operative design.** The finding frames incident reporting as monitoring *post-deployment* impacts. But the digest (u8) shows the bill also cares about *internal use*, which is pre-deployment, or non-deployment altogether, with internal-use catastrophic-risk assessments going to OES. And I'd guess a "critical safety incident" will include things that happen *inside* a lab: unauthorized access to model weights (exfiltration), loss of control of a model, deceptive behaviour discovered in testing [recall, fuzzy]. If so, the incident definition reaches well beyond "post-deployment impacts", and this finding understates the scope. Not an inconsistency in the law, since findings don't limit operative text, but a reader who took the findings as the scope would underestimate it. **A question someone should ask: do critical safety incidents include pre-deployment or internal events?** Hold it for the definition.
- "Impacts of artificial intelligence" is general, not "frontier models". Findings speak about AI at large; operative text will be specific.
- The findings so far, as a list of instruments: frameworks (implicitly, in "leading safety practices"), whistleblowers, public-facing information, incident reporting. The order of findings (e)–(i) roughly matches the order of the digest's mechanisms. The findings section justifies each mechanism in turn, so it's a **rationale map**: for each instrument, one finding saying why. Useful for tagging: the finding for each instrument is the "why" retrieval target for that instrument.
