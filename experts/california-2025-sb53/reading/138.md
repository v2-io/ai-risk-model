# Unit 138 · L208 · p.8

> (5) The external verifiability of determining whether a person or foundation model is covered, with an aim toward definitions that are verifiable by parties other than the frontier developer.

## What differed

A fifth consideration, **external verifiability**. I didn't recall this one.

## Wandering

- **A counterweight to self-definition.** All through the TFAIA I've been listing parameters the developer sets for itself. Here the statute names the opposite value for *coverage*: definitions should be "verifiable by parties other than the frontier developer". So the Legislature recognized self-determination as a weakness, at least for who is covered. Applied to today's tests:
  - **training compute** (10^26) is essentially self-reported. Outsiders can estimate it from hardware and time (Epoch AI does this), but can't verify it. So compute fails (5), partly;
  - **revenue** is verifiable for public companies (filings) and less so for private ones;
  - "**intends to use**" compute (u61) is an intent, and the least verifiable of all.

  So (5) arguably invites the Department to recommend more verifiable tests. A source expert asked "how verifiable is SB 53's coverage?" can quote this criterion as the statute's own acknowledgement that verifiability matters, and note that the current tests depend largely on developer self-reports.
- The five considerations, collected:
  1. alignment with federal and international thresholds;
  2. stakeholder input;
  3. ex-ante predictability;
  4. simplicity;
  5. external verifiability.

  Together with (a)'s targets (at the frontier, well-resourced), they're a compact **theory of good regulatory thresholds**, and probably the most methodologically interesting passage so far. They're worth extracting whole for any comparison of threshold-setting approaches across sources (the EU's Commission designation criteria, Annex XIII, would be the natural comparison).
