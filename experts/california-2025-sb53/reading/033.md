# Unit 33 · L81 · p.3

> (m) Timely reporting of critical safety incidents to the government is essential to ensure that public authorities are promptly informed of ongoing and emerging risks to public safety. This reporting enables the government to monitor, assess, and respond effectively in the event that advanced capabilities emerge in frontier artificial intelligence models that may pose a threat to the public.

## What differed

This is incident reporting again, a second finding on it after (i). I hadn't expected a second. It partly resolves the tension I noticed in unit 29.

## Wandering

- **This finding gives incident reporting a second purpose.** Finding (i) said incident reporting monitors *post-deployment impacts*. Finding (m) says it informs authorities of "ongoing and **emerging** risks" and lets them respond "in the event that advanced capabilities **emerge**". So incidents are also an **early-warning channel for capability emergence**, not just a harm log. That fits a critical-safety-incident definition reaching into labs: evidence of dangerous capabilities or deceptive behaviour, possibly before deployment. The two findings sit uneasily together: (i) is post-deployment monitoring, and (m) is emergence early warning. A reader quoting only (i) would say SB 53's incident regime is about deployment; one quoting (m) would say it's about emergence. **The truthful answer is both, and the definition decides.**
- "Monitor, assess, and respond" sets the government's role in three verbs, and *respond* is OES's natural mode. But respond how? OES can't regulate models. Respond with what powers? Perhaps none in SB 53 itself, beyond reporting onward. That leaves the question: **after OES receives a report that dangerous capabilities have emerged, what can California actually do?** Possibly nothing statutory except inform the Legislature and the Governor, and share with federal partners.
- "Frontier artificial intelligence models" is a longer form of "frontier model". It's probably not the defined term, which I guess is "frontier model", unprefixed.
- "Risks to public safety" and "threat to the public". There are now five or six protected-party framings. The findings settle into *the public* and *public safety* as the main ones, with *consumers* (e) as the outlier.
- The rhythm of the findings changed at (j): before (j), purely positive; from (j), risk-aware and mechanism-justifying. The structure reads as if negotiated in layers: the boosterish (a)–(h), then the risk-acknowledging (j)–(m).
