# Unit 30 · L78 · p.3

> (j) Unless they are developed with careful diligence and reasonable precaution, there is concern that advanced artificial intelligence systems could have capabilities that pose catastrophic risks from both malicious uses and malfunctions, including artificial intelligence-enabled hacking, biological attacks, and loss of control.

## What differed

The harm finding arrives at last, as my second option. It's more concrete than I expected: three named harms (hacking, biological attacks, loss of control), and two named *causal categories* (malicious uses, malfunctions).

## Wandering

- **"Malicious uses and malfunctions"** is the vocabulary of the International AI Safety Report (Bengio et al., January 2025), which sorts general-purpose AI risks into three categories: *malicious use*, *malfunctions* and *systemic risks*. SB 53 takes two of the three and **drops systemic risks**. That's a **lineage edge: SB 53 (j) → IASR 2025 taxonomy**, implicit, with no citation. The omission is informative: systemic risks (labour-market effects, concentration of power, environmental harms, privacy) are outside SB 53's catastrophic frame. It fits a statute about mass-casualty and billion-dollar events. This gives a clean answer to "Does SB 53 address systemic risks?": by its own findings, its catastrophic concern covers malicious use and malfunction, and not systemic risk. Whether the *definition* agrees is to be checked.
  - "Loss of control" filed under *malfunctions* matches the IASR's own placement, if I remember right: the IASR puts loss of control under malfunctions. So the borrowing may be structural, not only lexical.
  - The repo's CLAUDE.md said the EU Code's loss-of-control formula ("reliably direct, modify, or shut down") recurs in nine later documents. SB 53 is from September 2025, after the Code (July 2025). Does SB 53's operative text define "loss of control"? If it uses or paraphrases the Code's formula, that's another copy edge. If it leaves "loss of control" undefined, then "loss of control" in SB 53 is an undefined finding term. **Watch.**
- **"Careful diligence and reasonable precaution"** is duty-of-care vocabulary, the ghost of SB 1047 and its "reasonable care" standard. But it sits in a *finding*, conditionally ("unless they are developed with …"). SB 53 isn't going to impose a duty of care, I think. So the finding gestures at a standard of conduct the operative text won't enforce. A careful reader shouldn't infer a negligence standard from this finding. **A question someone should ask: does SB 53 impose a duty of care?** (I expect no, only disclosure, reporting and perhaps self-compliance. To be confirmed.)
- **"There is concern that"** is a strongly distanced hedge. Who is concerned? Not "the Legislature finds that AI could …", but "there is concern that". Every other finding asserts in the Legislature's own voice: "is leading", "has the potential", "has recommended", "should balance", "can align", "would benefit", "can advance", "are key", "enable". This one alone is agentless and attitudinal. The Legislature declines to own the risk claim; it reports that a concern exists. For this project's recording of the source's own hedge, that's exactly the kind of thing to mark: **SB 53 asserts benefits in its own voice and reports risks as others' concern.** It's the most telling sentence in the findings so far.
- "Advanced artificial intelligence systems" is yet another noun: not "foundation models" (b), not "frontier models". The findings use three nouns for the thing (AI, foundation models, advanced AI systems). Probably no meaning in the variation, but a definitions extractor shouldn't treat "advanced AI system" as an SB 53 defined term.
- "Capabilities that pose catastrophic risks": risks arise from capabilities. That's a capability-centred risk model (dangerous capabilities → catastrophic risk), the same model frontier safety frameworks use (capability thresholds). It fits codifying those frameworks.
- AI-enabled hacking and biological attacks are the canonical CBRN+cyber pair. There's no chemical, nuclear or radiological here, but the definition may list "chemical, biological, radiological, or nuclear weapon" [recall]. So the finding's examples are narrower than the definition's. Check.
