# Unit 50 · L107 · p.4

> (1) Unauthorized access to, modification of, or exfiltration of, the model weights of a frontier model that results in death or bodily injury.

## What differed

My recall was exact. Recalling it didn't prepare me for what it implies, which I see only now, reading it slowly.

## Wandering

- **Weight theft alone is not a critical safety incident.** The clause requires that the unauthorized access, modification or exfiltration "**results in death or bodily injury**". So if a frontier model's weights are stolen by a state actor and nobody is (yet) hurt, item (1) doesn't apply. The most consequential *security* event in frontier-AI risk thinking (the RAND weight-security work, the labs' own security levels) doesn't by itself trigger SB 53's incident reporting, unless another item covers it. I'll check items (2)–(4). If none does, then **SB 53 doesn't require reporting of weight exfiltration that hasn't yet caused injury.** That's a striking gap, and the opposite of what a casual reader would assume from "critical safety incident … exfiltration of model weights". A question someone should ask, with a surprising answer: "Must a frontier developer report stolen weights?" Answer: only if the theft results in death or bodily injury. Unless another item reaches it.
- **The epistemics of "results in".** The developer typically won't know whether stolen weights "result in" injury, perhaps for years. So the incident's *existence* depends on downstream consequences the reporting party may never learn. When does the 15-day clock (recall) start? On discovery of the incident, presumably. But the incident isn't complete until injury occurs, so the clock would start on discovery of the injury link. In practice this item will rarely be reportable in time to matter. **As a design, a security-breach item conditioned on downstream bodily harm is close to unusable as an early warning.** It conflicts with finding (m)'s early-warning purpose ("promptly informed of ongoing and emerging risks").
- **"Bodily injury", not "serious injury", and no count.** One person's bodily injury suffices. So incidents have a far lower casualty bar than catastrophic risk: one injured person against 51 killed-or-seriously-injured. A clear tiering: an incident is an event with any bodily harm (for this item), and a catastrophe is the risk of mass harm. Lexicon: *bodily injury* (incident threshold) ≠ *serious injury* (catastrophic threshold). Two different injury concepts in adjacent definitions. Neither is defined, as far as I've seen.
- **Grammatical attachment.** "the model weights of a frontier model that results in death or bodily injury". "That results" is singular ("results"), so its antecedent is singular. "Model weights" is plural ("weights … that result" would be plural). So the singular antecedent is either "a frontier model" or the whole act ("unauthorized access …, modification …, or exfiltration …", treated as singular). Read as "a frontier model that results in death" it would be nonsense (a model doesn't "result in"), so the act is the subject. That's fine, but the agreement is what tells you. An extractor shouldn't attach the condition to "model weights".
- **"Unauthorized"**: by whom? By the frontier developer, presumably. An insider with authorized access who copies weights for an unauthorized purpose: is that "unauthorized access"? Or authorized access followed by unauthorized exfiltration? "Exfiltration" covers it either way.
- **"Modification of" the weights**: tampering, poisoning, backdooring. It's included, which is thoughtful. A backdoored model that later hurts someone counts.
- "Model weight" will presumably be defined later (alphabetically under M).
