# Unit 108 · L172 · p.6

> (2) The Office of Emergency Services shall take all necessary precautions to limit access to any reports related to internal use of frontier models to only personnel with a specific need to know the information and to protect the reports from unauthorized access.

## What differed

A security duty on OES itself. I hadn't predicted it.

## Wandering

- **The state takes on a security duty.** OES must protect internal-use reports on a need-to-know basis. The logic: internal-use summaries may reveal what a lab's unreleased models can do, which is itself sensitive (proliferation risk, competitive value). So the state becomes a custodian of dangerous information, and its own security matters. A state emergency agency now holds summaries of frontier labs' most sensitive risk assessments. **A question someone should ask: who inside California's government can see internal-use assessments?** "Personnel with a specific need to know". Not the Legislature, not the public, perhaps not even the AG, unless need-to-know is established.
- Note the scope: "**any reports related to internal use**". That includes incident reports flagged as internal-use (u106) as well as the quarterly summaries. So an incident report with the internal-use flag set gets the stricter handling.
- "**All necessary precautions**" is an absolute-sounding standard ("all necessary"), but unenforceable by anyone in particular. Who sues OES for weak security? No one.
- This, (f)(2)'s unredacted retention, and the PRA exemption together form a **confidential information layer**: sealed copies held by developers, need-to-know reports held by OES. The public layer is the redacted framework and transparency report.
