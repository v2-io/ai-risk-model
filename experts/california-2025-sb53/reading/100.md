# Unit 100 · L164 · p.6

> (f) (1) When a frontier developer publishes documents to comply with this section, the frontier developer may make redactions to those documents that are necessary to protect the frontier developer's trade secrets, the frontier developer's cybersecurity, public safety, or the national security of the United States or to comply with any federal or state law.

## What differed

As recalled; it just comes at (f), not (d).

## Wandering

- **Five redaction grounds**: trade secrets, the developer's cybersecurity, public safety, US national security, compliance with law. "**Necessary to protect**" is a necessity standard, stricter than "relevant to". But the developer decides what's necessary, at least in the first instance. Self-assessment again.
- **"Public safety" as a ground for withholding** is the infohazard rationale. A transparency report that would reveal how to elicit a dangerous capability can redact that for safety. So **transparency and safety pull against each other, and the statute lets the developer resolve the tension.** The public sees what the developer judges safe and non-proprietary to show.
- **A tension with the false-statement rule.** A redaction that leaves the remainder misleading ("we found no concerning capabilities [REDACTED: except in bio]")? The false-statement rule (e) covers "misleading", so a misleading-by-redaction document could still violate (e). The two provisions check each other, in principle.
- **"Documents to comply with this section"**: frameworks, transparency reports, modification justifications. Not OES submissions, which are confidential anyway.
- Combined with (c)(3), system cards as compliance vehicles, labs can redact system cards under these grounds, which they already do informally.
