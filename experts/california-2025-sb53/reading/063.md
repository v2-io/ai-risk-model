# Unit 63 · L120 · p.4

> (2) The quantity of computing power described in paragraph (1) shall include computing for the original training run and for any subsequent fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or other material modifications the developer applies to a preceding foundation model.

## What differed

My recall was exact, but recall doesn't answer the hinge, so here it is worked through.

## Wandering

- **Who is "the developer"?** It's lowercase and undefined, not "frontier developer". The paragraph adds together "the original training run" and the "modifications **the developer applies** to a preceding foundation model". Two readings:
  1. **Same developer throughout.** A developer's count includes its own original run plus its own later fine-tuning, RL and modifications. This is cumulative within one developer's lineage of work: pretraining, then post-training, so a 9×10^25 pretrain plus a 2×10^25 RL phase is a frontier model. That reading closes the obvious loophole of splitting pretraining from post-training.
  2. **Across developers.** "The original training run" (by whoever) plus "modifications the developer applies". So when B fine-tunes A's 10^26+ model, the result counts A's original run, and B's derivative *is* a frontier model.
- **Under reading 2, a strange consequence follows.** B's fine-tuned derivative is a *frontier model*, but is B a *frontier developer*? Definition (h) requires that "**the person** has used, or intends to use, at least as much computing power to train the frontier model as would meet the technical specifications". B used only fine-tuning compute. If "the person has used" counts only B's own compute, B isn't a frontier developer. So **a frontier model could exist with no frontier developer responsible for it**: A trained the base, not the derivative, and B didn't use enough compute. If instead (h) is read cumulatively as well ("the person has used" = compute embodied in the model the person produced), then every fine-tuner of an open-weight frontier model becomes a frontier developer, even a hobbyist doing a $50 LoRA. That would extend incident reporting and whistleblower duties to them, and if they're large, the framework duty too. Both readings are uncomfortable. The drafters probably meant reading 1, with "the developer" referring back to the frontier developer in (h). **A source expert should flag this as a genuine open question: does fine-tuning someone else's frontier model make you a frontier developer?** The text doesn't settle it cleanly.
  - This is exactly the open-weight derivative question that made SB 1047 controversial; that bill had a "covered model derivative" concept, I recall. SB 53 seems to have no such term, and this paragraph is where the derivative question gets absorbed, ambiguously.
- **"Material modifications"**: materiality again, now for modifications. A non-material modification (a system prompt change? a quantization?) doesn't count. Quantization isn't training compute anyway.
- "**A preceding foundation model**": the modifications are applied to a *foundation* model (not necessarily a frontier one), so the base could be sub-threshold. 9×10^25 base + 2×10^25 fine-tune → frontier. Under reading 2, B fine-tuning A's 9×10^25 open model with 2×10^25 of its own compute creates a frontier model, and B *has* used 2×10^25. Is that "at least as much computing power … as would meet the specifications"? Only cumulatively. The ambiguity goes all the way down.
- The plain list "fine-tuning, reinforcement learning, or other material modifications" names the post-training methods. "Reinforcement learning" being named in a 2025 statute reflects the reasoning-model era (o1, R1), when RL compute became a large share of training.
