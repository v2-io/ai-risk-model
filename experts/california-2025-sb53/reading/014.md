# Unit 14 · L50 · p.2

> This bill would, among other things related to protecting whistleblowers working with foundation models, prohibit a frontier developer from making, adopting, enforcing, or entering into a rule, regulation, policy, or contract that prevents a covered employee, as defined, from disclosing, or retaliates against a covered employee for disclosing, information to the Attorney General, a federal authority, a person with authority over the covered employee, or another covered employee who has authority to investigate, discover, or correct the reported issue, if the covered employee has reasonable cause to believe that the information discloses that the frontier developer's activities pose a specific and substantial danger to the public health or safety resulting from a catastrophic risk or that the frontier developer has violated the TFAIA.

## What differed

- My recall was close to verbatim again. The one addition is "or retaliates against a covered employee for disclosing". The sentence's grammar is odd: the frontier developer is prohibited from making a rule that "prevents … or retaliates". A *rule* doesn't retaliate. The digest is compressing two prohibitions into one: rules that prevent disclosure, and acts of retaliation.
- "Whistleblowers **working with foundation models**". The digest frames scope by the artifact, while the operative trigger is the covered-employee definition.
- The anonymous internal process isn't here. It may come next, or it may sit only in the statute.

## Wandering

- **A correction to my unit-13 worry.** The protected disclosure has two alternative prongs: (a) "a specific and substantial danger … resulting from a catastrophic risk", or (b) "the frontier developer has violated the TFAIA". So my 30-deaths employee is protected if the danger also involves a TFAIA violation. An example: the developer didn't follow its framework, if following it is required, or failed to report a critical safety incident. That makes the "comply with framework" question from unit 8 matter even more. If noncompliance with one's own framework is a TFAIA violation, prong (b) protects a broad class of disclosures ("we're not doing what our framework says"), whatever the casualty count. If it isn't, prong (b) is narrow. **The open question has turned into a hinge for the whistleblower scope.**
- "Reasonable cause to believe" is an objective-ish belief standard, the same as 1102.5's. The employee doesn't have to be right, only reasonable. Combined with "catastrophic risk", which itself carries knowledge and materiality standards, I'll have nested epistemic standards: an employee's reasonable belief that a developer's activities pose a danger from a risk that is itself defined by foreseeability and materiality. Belief about a risk of a risk. Anyone formalizing this will need to keep the layers apart.
- The recipients form a list: the AG; a "federal authority" (which? undefined in the digest, maybe defined in the statute); a person with authority over the employee; another covered employee with authority to investigate. **OES is not on the list**, though OES receives incident reports, and the press is not either. So an employee can't go public under this protection. It's a channel to regulators and to internal management, not to the public. That's a whistleblowing model built on official channels. It also means the information these disclosures carry tends to go to the AG and federal bodies, confidentially, under the PRA exemption from unit 9.
- "A federal authority" brings in federal bodies like CAISI (formerly the US AI Safety Institute) without naming them. By late 2025 the federal landscape was shifting under the new administration's AI Action Plan, so pointing at "a federal authority" without naming one ages well.
