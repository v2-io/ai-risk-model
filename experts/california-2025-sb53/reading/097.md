## Wandering

- **Not limited to the required documents.** "Shall not make a materially false or misleading statement about catastrophic risk …". It covers *any* statement: press releases, blog posts, CEO interviews, congressional testimony, tweets, investor decks? If a lab's CEO says publicly "our models pose no catastrophic risk" and that's materially misleading, it's a TFAIA violation, enforceable by the AG. That's a significant speech-regulating provision, and (with "comply with") one of the bill's two real teeth. **A question someone should ask: does SB 53 cover what a lab says outside its required filings?** On its face, yes. Possible First Amendment questions (compelled or restricted commercial speech) are a matter for later, and for courts.
  - Then again, a statute generally reaches only statements a court would treat as commercial or factual. Opinion ("we believe…") may be protected, which is where "misleading" does the work.
- **All frontier developers, not only large.** Even small frontier developers can't make materially false or misleading statements about catastrophic risk.
- **"Its management of catastrophic risk"** is exactly the preemption phrase (digest u16: "management of catastrophic risk"). So "management of catastrophic risk" is a quasi-term used in at least two places (the false-statement rule and preemption) and undefined. The framework (g) "manage, assess, and mitigate" is its natural content.
- **"Materially"**, number seven: materially false or misleading. Here materiality has its familiar securities-law sense (a reasonable person would consider it important). This is the one place in SB 53 where "material" has a well-established legal meaning to borrow.
- "**False or misleading**": misleading covers true-but-deceptive statements, a half-truth standard. Broader than falsity alone.
- This connects to the system-card point (u94). System cards are now legally required statements, and their content about catastrophic risk is under a false-or-misleading standard. A system card that understates a model's bio capability could be a TFAIA violation. That raises the stakes of every risk claim labs make, and the corpus's lab documents from 2026 on are documents written under this liability. **That bears on how the project should weigh lab self-assessments**: after SB 53, labs have both an incentive to be careful (liability for misleading statements) and an incentive to say less (fewer statements, fewer risks). Their hedging may get thicker.
