# Unit 143 · L213 · p.8

> 22757.15. (a) A large frontier developer that fails to publish or transmit a compliant document required to be published or transmitted under this chapter, makes a statement in violation of subdivision (e) of Section 22757.12, fails to report an incident as required by Section 22757.13, or fails to comply with its own frontier AI framework shall be subject to a civil penalty in an amount dependent upon the severity of the violation that does not exceed one million dollars ($1,000,000) per violation.

## What differed

My recall was exact. The tier word answers my watch point: **"A large frontier developer"**.

## Wandering

- **The civil penalty reaches only large frontier developers.** Every listed violation is penalized only when a *large* frontier developer commits it. So for a frontier developer that isn't large, these duties carry **no civil penalty under this section**:
  - publishing a transparency report (§22757.12(c)(1));
  - not making materially false statements about catastrophic risk (§22757.12(e)(1)(A));
  - reporting critical safety incidents within 15 days or 24 hours (§22757.13(c)).

  Unless (b) or later text adds one, small frontier developers have **duties without a penalty**. A source expert should flag this prominently, because nearly any summary ("SB 53 imposes civil penalties up to $1M for violations") will get it wrong. **The digest itself got it wrong:** u10 said "civil penalty for noncompliance with the TFAIA", unqualified. That's a second digest-vs-statute divergence after "comply with", and like the first it falls on enforcement. A question with a surprising answer: "What happens if a small frontier developer fails to report a critical safety incident?" Under §22757.15(a), nothing. Other remedies may exist (injunctions under general law? unfair competition under B&P §17200, since violating a statute is "unlawful" business practice?). That's my speculation on general California law, not this text.
  - The proportionality logic stretched to its end: small developers get duties as norms, large developers get them as enforceable obligations. Or it's a drafting gap. The findings ("avoiding burdening smaller companies") support the first reading as intended.
- **The four violation types**, as enumerated:
  1. failing to publish or transmit a *compliant* document (framework, modification justification, transparency report, internal-use summary);
  2. a false or misleading statement under (e);
  3. failing to report an incident under §22757.13;
  4. failing to comply with its own framework.

  So "comply with" (u67) is explicitly a penalized violation. That's where the conduct lever lands.
  - **Not listed:** failing to describe redactions (f)(2), failing to retain unredacted documents for five years, failing to review the framework annually (b)(1)? The annual review produces no document if nothing changes, so "fails to publish … a compliant document" doesn't reach it. The retention duty isn't a document to publish or transmit. So **the five-year retention duty has no penalty in this list.** The sealed record that makes enforcement possible (u101) isn't itself enforced here.
- **"A compliant document"**: a published but deficient framework (missing a required topic) is a failure to publish a *compliant* document. So content gaps are penalized, not only non-publication. Good.
- **"Up to $1M per violation, dependent upon the severity"**: for the largest labs, with tens of billions in revenue, $1M is trivial as a fine. Its force comes from reputation and the per-violation count. What counts as one violation? Each day of noncompliance? Each document? Unstated. And the statutory cap was lowered from SB 1047's (which had penalties up to 10% of compute cost, I recall).
