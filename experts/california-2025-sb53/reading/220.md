# Unit 220 · L307 · p.12

> (d) This act shall not apply to the extent that it strictly conflicts with the terms of a contract between a federal government entity and a frontier developer.

## What differed

I didn't predict this at all. **A federal contract can displace the act.** It's a second federal carve-out, after "lawful activity of the federal government" (u47).

## Wandering

- **The whole act, including the whistleblower chapter, gives way to federal contract terms.** "This act shall not apply to the extent that it strictly conflicts with the terms of a contract between a federal government entity and a frontier developer". Any duty in SB 53 is suspended where a federal contract's terms strictly conflict with it. That includes:
  - transparency reports;
  - framework publication;
  - incident reporting to OES;
  - internal-use summaries;
  - **whistleblower protections in the Labor Code chapter.**

  A DoD or intelligence-community contract that requires secrecy about a model's capabilities, or restricts employees' disclosures to non-federal recipients, would override SB 53 to the extent of the strict conflict. For labs with 2025–26 federal contracts (the four big frontier labs all had DoD deals, I recall), **SB 53's application depends partly on contract terms the public can't see.** A question someone should ask, with a surprising answer: **"Can a federal contract exempt a lab from SB 53?"** To the extent of strict conflict, yes, by the act's own terms.
- **"Strictly conflicts"** is a high bar. Compliance with both must be impossible, not merely inconvenient. A contract requiring confidentiality about a classified deployment strictly conflicts with publishing a transparency report *about that deployment*. It doesn't conflict with incident reporting to OES about unrelated models. So the carve-out is narrow in principle, and it applies only where the contract speaks.
- **A private agreement overriding state law.** Normally a contract can't displace a statute. A party can't contract out of public law. Here the statute *chooses* to yield to contracts with federal entities. That's partly a federal-supremacy courtesy (federal procurement law and classified-information rules would often pre-empt anyway), and partly a political concession to national-security uses. Combined with u47, **SB 53 steps back from national-security AI in two ways**: lawful federal activity isn't catastrophic risk, and federal contract terms prevail over conflicting duties.
- For the whistleblower case: a covered employee on a classified project who reports a catastrophic danger to the California AG, against contract terms requiring disclosure only through federal channels, may lose SB 53 protection to the extent of the conflict. They'd have federal whistleblower protections instead, which have their own channels (Inspectors General, the Intelligence Community Whistleblower Protection Act).
- Note "**a frontier developer**", not "large". It applies at every tier.
