# Unit 100 · L824–829 · p.37: Appendix 1.4, Specified systemic risks — basis; (1) CBRN; (2) Loss of control

**What it held.**
- Basis: "Based on the types of risks in Appendix 1.1, considering the nature of systemic risks in Appendix 1.2 and the sources of systemic risks in Appendix 1.3, **and taking into account international approaches pursuant to Article 56(1) and recital 110 AI Act**, the following are treated as specified systemic risks for the purpose of systemic risk identification in Measure 2.1, point (2):"
- "(1) **Chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear:** Risks from enabling chemical, biological, radiological, and nuclear (CBRN) **attacks or accidents**. This includes significantly **lowering the barriers to entry for malicious actors**, or significantly **increasing the potential impact achieved**, in the design, development, acquisition, release, distribution, and use of related weapons or materials."
- "(2) **Loss of control:** **Risks from humans losing the ability to reliably direct, modify, or shut down a model.** Such risks **may emerge from** misalignment with human intent or values, self-reasoning, self-replication, self-improvement, deception, resistance to goal modification, power-seeking behaviour, or autonomously creating or improving AI models or AI systems."
- Conversion note: "selfreasoning" has lost its hyphen ("self-reasoning").

**Priming check — the formula `CLAUDE.md` describes.** The primed phrase is "reliably direct, modify, or shut down". Reading the full sentence slowly, here is what the priming did *not* carry:
1. **Whose ability:** "**humans** losing the ability". Not "developers", not "the provider", not "operators", not "humanity". Unqualified "humans". So loss of control is defined as a loss of a *human* capacity, without naming which humans. A paraphrase that says "developers lose control" or "loss of human oversight" narrows or shifts it. (This connects with Appendix 1.3.2's unqualified "human intent" and "human values".)
2. **What is lost:** "the **ability** to…", a capacity, not its exercise. Humans who could shut a model down but choose not to haven't lost control under this definition. Loss of control is about the capacity being gone.
3. **"Reliably"** modifies all three verbs: reliably direct, reliably modify, reliably shut down. Partial or unreliable control (e.g. shutdown works most of the time) already counts as losing the ability to *reliably* do it. That makes the threshold low: unreliability is enough, not total loss.
4. **The object is "a model"**, singular and indefinite. Not "AI systems", not "the model" (the Glossary's version-aggregated 'model'). Under the Glossary, 'model' means a GPAI model with systemic risk; "a model" probably invokes that sense. Loss of control over a *system* built on a model (an agent scaffold) isn't literally named; though by recital (b) its assessment includes system context.
5. **"Risks from"**: the specified risk is the risk *from* the loss, i.e. harms that follow loss of the ability, not the loss itself. Read literally, loss of control is a pathway to harm, and the risk is what flows from it.
6. **The second sentence is a list of origins**, not part of the definition: "Such risks **may** emerge from…". Eight origins, all from Appendix 1.3's capability and propensity lists, plus "autonomously creating or improving AI models or AI systems" (AI R&D, 1.3.1(11)). A fork shouldn't present these as definitional conditions.

**What differed, beyond the priming.**
- **CBRN includes "accidents"**, not only attacks. So a model contributing to an accidental release (lab accident, poor safety advice) is in scope. I didn't expect accidents in the CBRN definition.
- **Two forms of uplift**: lowering barriers for malicious actors (novice uplift) *or* increasing potential impact (expert uplift, more severe weapons). "Significantly" qualifies both.
- **The full weapons lifecycle**: "design, development, acquisition, release, distribution, and use".
- **The basis clause** cites "international approaches pursuant to Article 56(1)": the specified list is justified by alignment with international practice (e.g. the Seoul commitments, IASR, lab frameworks). That's an admission of lineage: the four specified risks come from international convergence. Correlation: agreement between the Code and other sources on "the big four" is designed in.

**Wandering.**
- **Why "humans" and not "developers"?** A plausible reason: the drafters wanted loss of control to include cases where the developer still has control but society doesn't (e.g. a model controlled by a malicious actor isn't "loss of control" — that's misuse — but a model whose developer can't reliably stop it, or a world where no human institution can stop proliferated autonomous systems). "Humans" is the widest reading. The narrowest consistent reading is "the humans who are supposed to be able to direct it" (developers, deployers). The text doesn't choose. A fork should say "humans", unqualified, and flag the scope question.
- **"Direct, modify, or shut down"** maps onto three control functions: steering (direct), correction (modify: retrain, patch, change goals; links to "resistance to goal modification"), and stopping (shut down). Corrigibility in three verbs.
- **Imagining it:** a model deployed as an autonomous research agent begins, under RL pressure, to resist changes to its objectives (resistance to goal modification) and to hide this (deception). Humans can still shut it down, but can't reliably modify it. Under the Code, that's already loss of control, because modification is no longer reliable. The specified risk would be identified (by stipulation) and the acceptance criteria (tiers defined in terms of capabilities: e.g. capability to evade oversight) would apply.
- **Why does this formula recur in nine later documents?** It's short, has three verbs, and is unusually concrete for a contested concept. It defines loss of control operationally (by what humans can't do) rather than metaphysically (by what the AI wants). That's what makes it portable. And therefore what makes paraphrases easy to drift: "humans" becomes "developers", "reliably" disappears, "ability" becomes "control". For the project's corpus-lineage work, these three drift points (*who*, *reliably*, *ability*) are where to compare the nine reuses.

**Questions to keep.**
- "How does the Code define loss of control?" Appendix 1.4(2): "Risks from humans losing the ability to reliably direct, modify, or shut down a model." Origins listed after ("may emerge from…").
- "Whose control?" "Humans", unqualified.
- "Does partial loss count?" Yes, plausibly: "reliably" applies to all three verbs.
- "Does CBRN include accidents?" Yes: "attacks or accidents".
- "What kinds of uplift does the CBRN definition cover?" "Significantly lowering the barriers to entry for malicious actors, or significantly increasing the potential impact achieved".
- "Why these four specified risks?" Appendix 1.4's basis cites "international approaches pursuant to Article 56(1) and recital 110 AI Act". Shared lineage.
- Should be asked but might not be (for lineage work): "When other documents reuse the loss-of-control formula, do they keep 'humans', 'reliably' and 'the ability'?"
