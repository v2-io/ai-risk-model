# Unit 74 · L600–603 · p.27: M9.3 (3)–(4); intermediate reports every four weeks

**What it held.**
- "(3) **a death of a person**, or if… suspect with reasonable likelihood such a causal relationship…, not later than **10 days**…; and"
- "(4) serious harm to a person's health (**mental and/or physical**), an infringement of obligations under Union law intended to protect fundamental rights, and/or serious harm to property or the environment, or if… suspect…, not later than **15 days**…."
- "For unresolved serious incidents, Signatories will update the information… in an **intermediate report**… **at least every four weeks** after the initial report."

**What differed.** The 10- and 15-day tiers match memory; the four Art. 3(49)-style categories too. Not predicted: **"mental and/or physical"** health explicitly; the four-week intermediate cadence (I'd half-guessed).

**Wandering.**
- **The threshold is one death.** A serious incident here needs no mass-casualty scale: "a death of a person". So "systemic risk" (the Chapter's subject) and "serious incident" (C9's trigger) are on very different scales. Systemic risks are about Union-level, large-scale harms (Art. 3(65)), but a single death involving the model is a serious incident to report. This matters for cross-source comparison: my priming says SB 53's catastrophic risk is ">50 people or $1B" and that two later documents relabel that bar as "systemic risk". The Code's *incident* bar is one death; its *risk* concept is defined by Art. 3(65), without a number (so far). A fork should never equate the Code's "serious incident" with "catastrophe".
- **Mental health.** A model's involvement in a suicide (the cases discussed in 2025 around chatbots and teenagers) is a "death of a person" (10 days); serious mental-health harm is a 15-day incident. So the Code's incident regime reaches individual-harm cases that its systemic-risk framing might seem not to. This is how individual harms enter a systemic-risk instrument: as incidents, feeding risk assessment.
- **Fundamental rights infringements** as incidents: e.g. a model used in discriminatory decisions? These are harms of a different kind (legal, not physical); reporting them as incidents within 15 days requires noticing them, which is harder.
- **"Serious harm to… the environment"**: the first operational appearance of environmental harm (cf. unit 2's prediction). It enters as an incident category (from Art. 3(49)), not as a systemic risk to assess. My unit-2 hypothesis holds, refined: environmental protection is operationalised only in incident reporting (and as a forecasting variable in M3.1).
- **Four-weekly updates until resolved**: incidents stay open, with regular reporting. A long-running incident (e.g. a persistent misuse campaign) gives the AI Office a monthly stream.
- The severity order of deadlines is interesting: critical infrastructure (2 days) < cyber (5) < death (10) < other serious harm (15). Death is less urgent than cyber? Probably because infrastructure and cyber incidents can cascade and need urgent coordination, while a death is past and the investigation is slower. The timelines track *urgency of response*, not severity of harm. A fork should be ready to explain that ordering, as my inference.

**Questions to keep.**
- "What counts as a serious incident?" M9.3's categories: serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure; serious cybersecurity breach incl. (self-)exfiltration of weights and cyberattacks; death of a person; serious harm to health (mental and/or physical), infringement of fundamental-rights obligations under Union law, serious harm to property or the environment. (Check the Glossary.)
- "Is one death enough to be a serious incident?" Yes: "a death of a person".
- "How often must unresolved incidents be updated?" "At least every four weeks after the initial report."
- Should be asked but might not be: "Why is a death reported more slowly than a cyber breach?" Timelines seem to track response urgency (inference).
