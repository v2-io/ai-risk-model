**What differed.** My unit-9 prediction was nearly verbatim ("adopting a state-of-the-art Safety and Security Framework ("Framework")"). That's the label memory once more, and a reason for care, not confidence. What I hadn't predicted:
- the **"LEGAL TEXT:"** line: each Commitment cites the AI Act provisions it implements. That's a structural feature a fork can use to map Commitments to Articles.
- The Framework's purpose is to ensure systemic risks are "**acceptable**", not minimised, not eliminated, not "reduced as far as possible". The goal word is acceptability. That's the central concept of the whole Chapter's risk model, stated in its first operative sentence.
- A **precedence rule for figures**: text over diagrams. A small thing, but it means the Code anticipates readers relying on diagrams and rules them subordinate.

**Wandering.**

- **"Acceptable" is the pivot.** The Code doesn't promise safety; it promises a process that ensures risks are acceptable. Acceptable to whom, by what standard? I expect a later Commitment (risk acceptance determination) with "systemic risk acceptance criteria" set by the signatory, maybe tied to "systemic risk tiers". If the signatory sets its own acceptance criteria, the Code's core standard is partly self-defined, constrained by the state-of-the-art rule and by AI Office review. That's the most important question for any comparison with SB 53 or company frameworks: *who sets the threshold of acceptability?* Hold it open until the text answers.
- **The Framework is a document about processes, not about models.** It "outline[s] the systemic risk management processes and measures that Signatories implement". So it's firm-level, and there'll presumably be a separate model-level document (the Model Report, if memory is right). Two levels: firm (Framework) and model (Report). This matches company frameworks (RSPs, Preparedness Frameworks), which are firm-level policies. The Code effectively requires each signatory to have an RSP-like document. Lineage: the Framework concept descends from the "frontier safety frameworks" that labs published in 2023–24 (Anthropic RSP, OpenAI Preparedness, Google DeepMind FSF) and from the Seoul Frontier AI Safety Commitments. A fork asked "where does the Safety and Security Framework come from?" should give that lineage as likely, not as stated by the Code.
- **"State-of-the-art Framework"**: the state-of-the-art rule of (a) applies to the Framework document itself, not only to evaluations and mitigations. A Framework that is behind current practice wouldn't meet Commitment 1.
- **"Implement" sits in the purpose clause**: the processes "that Signatories implement". The Framework describes what's actually done. Measure 1.2 (implementing) is where a Framework stops being a paper commitment. I'll read 1.2 for how implementation is checked.
- **Notify, not approve.** Measure 1.4 is notification to the AI Office. There's no approval step in the Commitment. The AI Office gets the Framework; it doesn't sign it off. (Unless 1.4 says more.)
- **The diagram's absence.** Figure 1 is lost in the canonical text (only the caption survived). A fork asked about the process should note the figure exists on p.6 and isn't in the canonical text; but the precedence rule means the text alone is authoritative, so nothing binding is lost.

**Questions to keep.**
- "What is the Safety and Security Framework for?" C1: "to outline the systemic risk management processes and measures that Signatories implement to ensure the systemic risks stemming from their models are acceptable."
- "Does the AI Office approve Frameworks?" C1: Signatories commit to *notifying* it (Measure 1.4). Check 1.4.
- "What's the Code's standard: minimise risk, or make it acceptable?" Acceptable. A question someone should ask, since many readers assume "reduce as far as possible".
- Conversion note to keep: the "LEGAL TEXT" lines may have lost their article numbers in conversion.

**Prediction for unit 12.** Measure 1.1, creating the Framework: Signatories create a Framework containing (probably lettered) (1) a description of the systemic-risk process (identification, analysis, acceptance determination); (2) systemic risk acceptance criteria / tiers; (3) "trigger points" for when assessments happen during development; (4) the high-level mitigations; (5) responsibility allocation; (6) a procedure for updating. And a deadline: created within some weeks of notification that a model has systemic risk, or before placing on the market.
