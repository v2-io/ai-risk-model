# Unit 8 · L55 · p.4: recital (f), Principle of Innovation in AI Safety and Security

**What it held.**
- "Determining the most effective methods for understanding and ensuring the safety and security of [these models] remains an evolving challenge."
- The Chapter "should encourage providers… to advance the state of the art in AI safety and security and related processes and measures."
- Advancing the state of the art "also includes developing targeted methods that specifically address risks while maintaining beneficial capabilities (e.g. mitigating biosecurity risks without unduly reducing beneficial biomedical capabilities), acknowledging that such precision demands greater technical effort and innovation than less targeted methods."
- "If providers… can demonstrate equal or superior safety or security outcomes through alternative means that achieve greater efficiency, such innovations should be recognised as advancing the state of the art… and meriting consideration for wider adoption."

**What differed.** I predicted the name and the "alternative means" clause (the label memory again). I didn't predict the **targeted-methods** sentence, the most interesting thing here: precision mitigation (remove the bio risk, keep the biomedical benefit) is named as itself an advance of the state of the art, and as *harder*. And the alternative-means clause is about **efficiency**, not only equivalence: "equal or superior outcomes… that achieve greater efficiency".

**Wandering.**

- **The state of the art is a moving target the signatories help move.** (a) says adopt at least the state of the art; (f) says the Chapter should encourage providers to *advance* it. Taken together: the bar a provider must meet is set partly by what providers themselves (and others) achieve. The leading provider's practice becomes everyone's baseline. That's a ratchet. It gives a leader an incentive to publish improvements (to be "recognised as advancing the state of the art"), and a laggard a reason to fear them. Who decides what the state of the art *is* at a given time? Not said here. Probably the AI Office in practice, informed by what's shared under (e). A fork should hold this as an open question: the Code has no stated procedure, so far, for determining the state of the art.
- **"Beneficial capabilities" enter the risk calculus.** The bio example says a mitigation that removes risk at the cost of beneficial biomedical capability is less good than one that keeps the benefit. So the Code doesn't treat capability reduction as costless. That's a position on the dual-use problem: precision is valued, not just risk reduction. In bow-tie terms, mitigations have side effects on benefits, and the Code wants them measured. For the lexicon: "beneficial capabilities" and "targeted methods" are concepts here.
- **"Unduly"** carries a balance judgement: some reduction of beneficial capability may be due. Who weighs biosecurity against biomedicine? Not said. It's the most contestable word in the unit.
- **"Demonstrate equal or superior safety or security outcomes through alternative means."** How would a provider demonstrate an *outcome* rather than a *process*? Safety outcomes are mostly counterfactual (incidents that didn't happen). So in practice "outcomes" will be evaluated through proxies: evaluation results, red-team success rates. This clause is the Code's flexibility valve: you needn't follow the named method if you can show your method works at least as well. Compare (a)'s "conclusively ruled out" for *less advanced* methods. The two together: below the state of the art requires conclusive proof that the risk is absent; a *different* method at the state of the art requires demonstration of equal or better outcomes. Different burdens for "worse" and "different".
- **"Meriting consideration for wider adoption"**: innovations by one provider may become recommendations for all. Combined with (e)'s sharing, the Code imagines itself as a mechanism for diffusing best practice across providers. Again, this predicts correlation between providers' methods over time.
- The tone in (f) is the most optimistic so far: safety as a field of innovation, not only of compliance. This is likely the "support innovation" leg of Art. 1(1) given a safety-specific form.
- One more thing: the bio example is the first time a specific risk domain is named in the Chapter. Biosecurity. I expect the specified systemic risks to include CBRN, and (f) uses bio as the paradigm case of a dual-use capability where targeting matters.

**Questions to keep.**
- "Can a provider use methods other than those in the Code?" (f): yes, if it "can demonstrate equal or superior safety or security outcomes through alternative means that achieve greater efficiency"; contrast (a)'s stricter rule for *less advanced* methods.
- "Who decides what the state of the art is?" Not stated so far. A question someone should ask.
- "Does the Code care about losing beneficial capabilities?" (f): "without unduly reducing beneficial biomedical capabilities".
