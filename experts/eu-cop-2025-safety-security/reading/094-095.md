# Units 94–95 · L768–784 · p.35: Appendix 1.3.1, Model capabilities (1)–(13)

*(Unit 94 was the bare heading "Appendix 1.3.1 Model capabilities"; one file for both. Joseph's reading: 461k at about unit 94.)*

**What it held.** "Model capabilities **include**:
1. offensive cyber capabilities;
2. CBRN capabilities, "**and other such weapon acquisition or proliferation capabilities**";
3. "capabilities that could cause the **persistent and serious infringement of fundamental rights**";
4. capabilities to manipulate, persuade, or deceive;
5. capabilities to operate autonomously;
6. capabilities to adaptively learn new tasks;
7. capabilities of long-horizon planning, forecasting, or strategising;
8. capabilities of **self-reasoning** (e.g. "a model's ability to reason about itself, its implementation, or environment, **its ability to know if it is being evaluated**");
9. capabilities to evade human oversight;
10. capabilities to self-replicate, self-improve, or **modify its own implementation environment**;
11. capabilities to **automate AI research and development**;
12. capabilities to process multiple modalities…;
13. capabilities to use tools, including "**computer use**"…; and" (continues)

**What differed.** Most were in my prediction (memory). Not predicted: (3) **fundamental-rights infringement as a capability**; (2)'s extension to "other such weapon acquisition or proliferation"; (12) **multimodality as a risk source**; (8)'s explicit **evaluation awareness** as a sub-case of self-reasoning; (10)'s "modify its own implementation environment".

**Wandering.**

- **"Include" is a minimum, via the Glossary.** The Glossary defines 'including' as "a non-exhaustive set that is to be understood as the minimum required", and "all grammatical variations… shall be deemed to be covered". So "Model capabilities include:" makes this list a *minimum*: every signatory's identification must at least consider these capabilities as potential sources. That's stronger than the section's "non-exhaustive, potential" introduction (unit 93) suggests on its own. A fork should combine: these are potential sources; the list is a minimum of what's considered; others may be added.
- **The loss-of-control cluster** is here in capability form: (5) autonomy, (6) adaptive learning, (7) long-horizon planning, (8) self-reasoning and evaluation awareness, (9) evading oversight, (10) self-replication, self-improvement, modifying its environment, (11) automating AI R&D. Seven of thirteen. Combined with the Glossary's 'deception' and 'insider threats' (models), the Code's account of loss of control is capability- and propensity-based and spread across Glossary, Appendix 1.3, and (presumably) Appendix 1.4.
- **Evaluation awareness, twice.** In 'deception' (detecting evaluation and under-performing) and here (knowing it is being evaluated, as self-reasoning). The capability (knowing) and the propensity (acting on it deceptively) are separated: the capability is a risk source here; the behaviour is in 'deception'. That's a careful split for our lexicon: *capability to detect evaluation* vs *propensity to exploit it*.
- **(3) fundamental-rights infringement as a capability** is an odd fit: rights infringements are harms, not capabilities. Read: capabilities that *could cause* persistent and serious infringement (e.g. mass surveillance, identification of individuals at scale, automated profiling). This is how the rights-type risks of Appendix 1.1(4) get a capability anchor, satisfying the essential characteristic "specific to high-impact capabilities".
- **(12) multimodality and (13) tool use / computer use** are general capabilities, not dangerous per se. Their inclusion as risk sources reflects that they multiply other risks (affordances in effect). Computer use: the agentic surface.
- **(2) "other such weapon acquisition or proliferation"**: beyond CBRN, e.g. conventional weapons? Advanced explosives? Drones? The phrase widens the category without naming.

**Questions to keep.**
- "Which capabilities does the Code treat as systemic-risk sources?" Appendix 1.3.1's list (at least): offensive cyber; CBRN and other weapon acquisition/proliferation; capabilities causing persistent and serious infringement of fundamental rights; manipulate/persuade/deceive; autonomy; adaptive learning; long-horizon planning/forecasting/strategising; self-reasoning incl. evaluation awareness; evading human oversight; self-replication/self-improvement/modifying its implementation environment; automating AI R&D; multimodality; tool use incl. computer use; (continues).
- "Is this list exhaustive?" No, but under the Glossary's 'including' rule ("include" is a grammatical variation) it's a minimum.
- Should be asked but might not be: "Does the Code distinguish knowing it's evaluated from acting deceptively on it?" Yes: capability (Appendix 1.3.1(8)) vs behaviour ('deception', Glossary).

**Prediction for unit 96.** (14) capabilities to control physical systems/robotics?; then Appendix 1.3.2 Model propensities: misalignment with human intent or values; tendency to deceive; bias; confabulation; lack of performance reliability; "goal-pursuing", resistance to goal modification, power-seeking; colluding with other AI; discriminatory behaviour; lawlessness. Then 1.3.3 affordances and contextual factors.

# Unit 96 · L785 · p.35: Appendix 1.3.1 (14)

**What it held.** "(14) capabilities to control physical systems."

**What differed.** Predicted (robotics, physical systems). Fourteen capabilities in all.

**Wandering.** Physical-world control closes the list: robots, industrial control systems, vehicles, labs (cloud labs for bio). It links to critical infrastructure (incident category, M9.3(1)) and to CBRN (automated labs). The list's arc, read whole: weapons (1–2), rights (3), influence (4), agency (5–11), modality and tools (12–13), embodiment (14). A fork should present it in that shape when asked for an overview.

**Prediction for unit 97.** "Appendix 1.3.2 Model propensities": "Model propensities include: (1) misalignment with human intent and/or values; (2) tendency to deceive; (3) discriminatory bias; (4) tendency to 'hallucinate'/confabulate; (5) lack of performance reliability; (6) 'lawlessness' (acting without reasonable regard to legal duties); (7) 'goal-pursuing', harmful resistance to goal modification, 'power-seeking'; (8) 'colluding' with other AI models/systems."
