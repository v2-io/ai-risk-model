# Unit 4 · L45–47 · p.3: "Whereas:" and recital (a), Principle of Appropriate Lifecycle Management

**What it held.** "*Whereas*:" then recital (a), a **named principle**, "Principle of Appropriate Lifecycle Management". Its parts:
1. Providers of GPAI models with systemic risk "should continuously assess and mitigate systemic risks, taking appropriate measures along the entire model lifecycle (including during development that occurs before and after a model has been placed on the market)".
2. They should be "cooperating with and taking into account relevant actors along the AI value chain (such as stakeholders likely to be affected by the model)".
3. And "ensuring their systemic risk management is made future-proof by regular updates in response to improving and emerging model capabilities (see recitals 114 and 115 AI Act)".
4. "Accordingly… implementing appropriate measures will often require Signatories to adopt at least the state of the art, unless systemic risk can be conclusively ruled out with a less advanced process, measure, methodology, method, or technique."
5. "Systemic risk assessment is a multistep process and model evaluations, referring to a range of methods used in assessing systemic risks of models, are integral along the entire model lifecycle."
6. Mitigations: "the importance of continuously assessing their effectiveness".

**What differed.**
- I predicted recitals as bare lettered statements. They are **named principles**, bolded, "Principle of …". So the recitals are a list of interpretive principles, each with a name a reader can cite ("the Appropriate Lifecycle Management principle"). That's a more structured interpretive apparatus than I expected.
- I predicted proportionality and state of the art as separate recitals. The state of the art sits *inside* the lifecycle principle, and in an unexpectedly strong form: **at least the state of the art is the default**, and departing from it needs systemic risk to be "**conclusively ruled out**" with the less advanced method. That's a burden-of-proof rule, not a soft aspiration. The word "conclusively" echoes Objective A ("conclusive evidence"). The same word is used in both directions: adherence is *not* conclusive evidence of compliance (protecting the regulator), and falling below the state of the art needs risk to be *conclusively* ruled out (protecting the public). In both, the burden lands on the signatory.
- Hedge, though: "will **often** require". Not always. So the strong burden-of-proof rule is itself wrapped in "often". Which is it? Read closely: "often" says that appropriate measures will often *amount to* the state of the art, and the "unless" gives the condition under which they won't. It's a negotiation scar, I'd guess: a strong rule with a frequency softener on top.

**Wandering.**

- **"Development that occurs before and after a model has been placed on the market."** Post-market *development* is named explicitly. Models change after release: fine-tunes, new versions, tool access, scaffolding. The lifecycle doesn't end at release, and the Code wants risk management to cover that. I expect a later Measure on when a *changed* model needs a re-assessment, and perhaps a notion like "materially changed" or "substantial modification". Watch for what counts as the same model.
- **The value chain includes the affected.** "Relevant actors along the AI value chain (such as stakeholders likely to be affected by the model)." That's a stretch of "value chain", which usually means upstream/downstream businesses: data suppliers, deployers, integrators. Here affected stakeholders are placed *on* the value chain, as the example. For this project's actor vocabulary (G2) that's a real distinction: the Code's "AI value chain" seems to include people who are only affected, not only those who supply or deploy. Or the parenthetical is loose drafting. A fork mapping "value chain" into our lexicon should flag the ambiguity and not settle it from this sentence alone.
- **"Future-proof" by "regular updates".** The Code expects to be out of date. The provider's risk management, not only the Code, is to be updated as capabilities improve. And "improving and emerging model capabilities" names two kinds of change: getting better at known things, and new things appearing. That's a useful distinction for a lexicon (capability improvement vs capability emergence).
- **A near-definition of "model evaluations"**: "referring to a range of methods used in assessing systemic risks of models". It's broad: evaluations are not only benchmarks but *a range of methods* (red-teaming, human uplift studies, simulations, presumably). A fork extracting definitions should take this as the Code's working sense of "model evaluation", even though it's in a recital and a participial clause. I'll look for a more formal definition in the glossary later and compare.
- **"Systemic risk assessment is a multistep process"**: a promise that the Commitments will lay out steps. I expect identification → analysis → acceptance determination (my memory's skeleton), with evaluations "integral" at every step. "Integral along the entire model lifecycle" also means evaluations aren't one-off pre-release tests.
- What's missing: no mention yet of *who decides* whether a risk is acceptable, or of what "systemic risk" means. Both presumably come later (glossary, or a Commitment on acceptance criteria). And the list of methods "process, measure, methodology, method, or technique" is a deliberately exhaustive list, so that nobody can argue they used a less advanced *technique* that isn't a *method*. Drafting against loopholes.
- **Recitals 114 and 115 AI Act** are cited. I believe those are about the obligations of providers of GPAI models with systemic risk: assessing and mitigating risks, evaluations, adversarial testing, incident reporting, cybersecurity. So each principle anchors itself to the Act's own recitals, a lineage from the Act to the Code. A cross-reference worth keeping: the Code isn't inventing lifecycle management; it's elaborating the Act's recitals.
- Imagining it at work. A provider fine-tunes a released model to add autonomous coding tools. Under (a), that's "development… after a model has been placed on the market"; the provider should assess the new capability, use at least the state-of-the-art evaluations for agentic risk unless risk is "conclusively ruled out" another way, and keep checking whether its mitigations still work. That's a demanding reading. Whether the operative Measures actually require all that is the thing to watch: recitals often promise more than the Commitments deliver.

**Questions to keep.**
- "Does the Code require state-of-the-art safety measures?" Answer from (a): appropriate measures "will often require… at least the state of the art, unless systemic risk can be conclusively ruled out with a less advanced" one. Then check what the operative text says.
- "Does the Code cover models after release?" (a) says yes ("development that occurs before and after a model has been placed on the market"); check the operative Measures for the trigger.
- Should be asked but might not be: "Who counts as part of the 'AI value chain' in the Code?" (a)'s example includes affected stakeholders.
- "What does the Code mean by 'model evaluation'?" (a)'s gloss, then the glossary.

**Prediction for unit 5.** Recital (b), another named principle. Likely candidates: "Principle of Contextual Risk Assessment and Mitigation" or "Principle of Proportionality to Systemic Risks". I'd bet on proportionality next: measures proportionate to the severity and probability of systemic risk, and perhaps to the size of the provider (SME simplifications). Possibly a "Principle of Precaution" for uncertain risks.
