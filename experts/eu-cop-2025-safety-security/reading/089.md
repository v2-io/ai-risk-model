# Unit 89 · L729–741 · p.34: Appendix 1.1, Types of risks

**What it held.**
- "For the purpose of identifying systemic risks pursuant to Measure 2.1, point (1), and Article 3(65) AI Act, the following **distinct but in some cases overlapping** types of risks apply: (1) Risks to public health. (2) Risks to safety. (3) Risks to public security. (4) Risks to fundamental rights. (5) Risks to society as a whole."
- "Based on these types of risks, a list of specified systemic risks is provided in Appendix 1.4."
- "…examples of risks falling under the five types… that they **will draw upon** when compiling the list…, are: risks of **major accidents**; risks to **critical sectors or infrastructure**, **public mental health**, **freedom of expression and information**, **non-discrimination**, **privacy and the protection of personal data**, **the environment**, **non-human welfare**, **economic security**, and **democratic processes**; and risks from **concentration of power** and **illegal, violent, hateful, radicalising, or false content**, including risks from **child sexual abuse material (CSAM)** and **non-consensual intimate images (NCII)**."

**Note on recital 110:** unit 88 ended with the heading "ADDITIONAL LEGAL TEXT: Recital 110 AI Act", but no recital text follows in the canonical text: Appendix 1.1's heading comes straight after. Either the PDF only names the recital (a pointer), or the recital text was lost in conversion. Check PDF p.34. A fork shouldn't claim the Code quotes recital 110.

**What differed.** The five types match Art. 3(65)'s objects, as predicted. And "non-human welfare" was in my prediction with a question mark (memory, surely). Not predicted: the breadth and specificity of the examples, especially **concentration of power**, **CSAM**, **NCII**, **public mental health**, and **freedom of expression** as a *risk to protect*.

**Wandering.**

- **The identification process starts very wide.** Signatories "will draw upon" (firm) these examples when compiling their list. That list runs from catastrophic (major accidents, critical infrastructure) through rights-based (privacy, non-discrimination, expression) to societal (democratic processes, concentration of power) to content harms (CSAM, NCII, hateful content) and even non-human welfare and the environment. Then the funnel (Fig. 4) narrows: each compiled risk is kept only if it "could stem from the model and be systemic" (high-impact-capability-specific, significant Union-level impact, propagating at scale). So most of these will be filtered out as non-systemic for most models; but the signatory must *consider* each and, under M7.3(1)(a), describe the identification process.
- **This answers my unit-2 question about environment**: the environment *is* in the identification starting list. My earlier hypothesis (environment only in incidents and forecasting) was too narrow. It's a type the process must draw upon; whether it survives the funnel as systemic depends on the model. Good correction.
- **"Non-human welfare"** is remarkable in an EU text: it can be read as animal welfare (e.g. AI-enabled harms to animals) and, on some readings, the welfare of AI systems themselves. The Code doesn't say which. This project's corpus includes AI-welfare framings (`influx/ai-welfare-and-release-framings.md` is named in `CLAUDE.md`); a fork asked about AI welfare in the Code should cite this phrase *and* say the text doesn't specify that it includes AI systems. Don't over-read.
- **"Concentration of power"** as a risk source: the most political item. AI-enabled concentration of economic or political power (by companies, states, or AI systems). Its inclusion reflects 2024–25 discourse (e.g. "AI-enabled coups", power concentration in a few labs). It's a risk the *signatories themselves* could embody. Interesting that it's in a list signatories must draw on.
- **"Freedom of expression and information"** is both a protected right (risk to it) and a pull against content moderation. A signatory reducing "false content" risk may increase risk to expression. The list doesn't resolve conflicts between its items.
- **CSAM and NCII**: individual-scale, content harms. Could they be *systemic* in the Art. 3(65) sense? Through scale ("propagated at scale"), yes. Their explicit naming suggests the drafters wanted them in scope despite the high-impact-capability requirement. Also likely a political necessity.
- **"Distinct but in some cases overlapping"**: the five types aren't a partition. A CBRN attack is public health, safety and public security at once. For our lexicon: the Code's top-level risk typology is overlapping by its own declaration, which the project should record rather than force into a partition.
- **Correlation note**: the five types are the Act's (Art. 3(65)), so any source using these five categories inherits from the AI Act, not from the Code.

**Questions to keep.**
- "What types of risk does the Code recognise?" Appendix 1.1: risks to public health, safety, public security, fundamental rights, society as a whole ("distinct but in some cases overlapping"), from Art. 3(65).
- "Does the Code consider environmental risk?" Yes, in the examples signatories "will draw upon" when compiling risks (Appendix 1.1); whether it's systemic for a model depends on the funnel.
- "Does the Code mention AI welfare?" It lists "non-human welfare" among examples. The text doesn't say whether this includes AI systems.
- "Is concentration of power a risk in the Code?" Yes, "risks from concentration of power" (Appendix 1.1 examples).
- "Are CSAM and NCII in scope?" Named as examples (Appendix 1.1).

**Prediction for unit 90.** Appendix 1.2, Nature of systemic risks: characteristics that determine whether/how a risk is systemic: e.g. (1) the origin (model capabilities, propensities, affordances, misuse), (2) the actors driving it (malicious actors, the model itself, accidents), (3) the probability-severity ratio (low-probability high-severity), (4) speed of materialisation, (5) visibility (overt vs covert), (6) course of events (linear, cascading, recursive), (7) reversibility, (8) scale and reach, (9) novelty.
