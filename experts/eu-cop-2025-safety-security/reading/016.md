# Unit 16 · L124–133 · p.8: Figure 2 caption; M1.2's full systemic risk assessment and mitigation process

**What it held.**
- "Figure 2. Illustrative timeline of systemic risk assessment and mitigation along the model lifecycle. The text of the Commitments and Measures takes precedence." (Figure not in the canonical text.)
- "In addition, Signatories will implement a full systemic risk assessment and mitigation process that involves four steps, without needing to duplicate parts of the model's previous systemic risk assessments that are still appropriate:
  - (1) identifying the systemic risks… as specified in Commitment 2;
  - (2) analysing each identified systemic risk as specified in Commitment 3;
  - (3) determining whether the systemic risks… are acceptable as specified in Measure 4.1; and
  - (4) **if the systemic risks… are not determined to be acceptable**, implementing safety and/or security mitigations as specified in Commitments 5 and 6, and **re-assessing the systemic risks… starting from point (1)**, as specified in Measure 4.2."
- "Signatories will conduct such a full systemic risk assessment and mitigation process **at least before placing the model on the market** and whenever the conditions specified in **Measure 7.6, first and third paragraph**, are met. Signatories will report… to the AI Office as specified in Commitment 7."

**What differed.** My prediction was close: four steps, C2 identification, C3 analysis, C4 acceptance, C5 safety and C6 security mitigations, C7 reporting. Two things I missed:
- **Mitigations are conditional on non-acceptance.** Step (4) applies "if the systemic risks… are not determined to be acceptable". The structure is: assess → if acceptable, done; if not, mitigate → re-assess from step 1. It's a loop that terminates on acceptability.
- **The re-assessment restarts at identification**, not at analysis. Mitigations can change *what* risks exist (e.g. a new safeguard creates a new attack surface), so identification runs again. That's a careful design choice.
- The re-run triggers point to **Measure 7.6** (first and third paragraphs), so Commitment 7 (reporting) contains the conditions for re-running the full process: probably the conditions for updating the Model Report (material change, new information, etc.).

**Wandering.**

- **Where are the mitigations a model already has?** Step (4) mitigations are added only if risk isn't acceptable. But a real model is assessed *with* its existing safeguards. So the risk being determined acceptable at step (3) must already be the risk *given* current mitigations. That means steps (1)–(3) assess the model as it would be deployed, with safeguards, and step (4) adds more if needed. That reading matters: a fork asked "are mitigations required?" should say: the Code requires mitigations sufficient for acceptability (step 4 loop), and the baseline mitigations are part of what's assessed. Watch Commitment 3 or 4 for language like "with mitigations in place" or "residual risk".
- **The loop has no exit other than acceptance, or…?** If mitigations never make the risk acceptable, the loop never terminates. M4.2 ("proceeding") presumably closes it: if risk can't be made acceptable, don't proceed (don't develop further, don't market, don't use). That's the Code's go/no-go. I'll read M4.2 for the strength of that "don't".
- **"At least before placing the model on the market."** Not before *using* it internally. The full process isn't required before internal deployment, only the continuous light layer (M1.2(1)) is. Unless 7.6's conditions catch internal use. So the Code's coverage of internal deployment is: Framework covers it (M1.1), lighter-touch evaluations and incidents apply (M1.2(1)), go/no-go input description mentions "use" (M1.1(2)(d)), but the **full four-step process is anchored to market placement**. That's the most consequential structural fact about internal deployment so far. A fork should give it with that precision.
- **Non-duplication returns** ("without needing to duplicate parts of the model's previous systemic risk assessments that are still appropriate"), echoing recital (c). Re-runs can reuse what's still valid. Sensible, and also the place where a lazy re-assessment hides.
- **Two figures lost.** Figures 1 and 2 both survive only as captions. Figure 2 is an "illustrative timeline". Both say text takes precedence, so they're explanatory. But for a human reader they probably carry the clearest picture of the lifecycle loop. A fork explaining the process would benefit from the figure (PDF p.8).
- Imagining a release: a frontier model reaches end of pre-training. Lighter-touch evals at checkpoints raised a flag on cyber, so (1)(d) widened the cyber assessment. Before release, the full process: identify risks (C2), analyse each (C3), determine acceptability (M4.1) — cyber at a tier needing extra safeguards — implement (C5/C6), re-identify from step 1, determine acceptable, then report (C7) and place on market. Post-market monitoring (M3.5) and incidents (C9) feed back into the light layer. The design is coherent. The judgement points are all inside "acceptable", which the signatory's own criteria define.

**Questions to keep.**
- "What are the steps of the Code's systemic risk process?" M1.2: identification (C2), analysis (C3), acceptance determination (M4.1), and if not acceptable, mitigations (C5, C6) and re-assessment from identification (M4.2).
- "When is a full assessment required?" "At least before placing the model on the market" and when M7.6's first and third paragraph conditions are met.
- "Is a full assessment required before internal deployment?" Not by M1.2's own terms. Check M7.6.
- "Are mitigations always required?" Only (additional ones) if risk isn't determined acceptable; but baseline safeguards are presumably part of what's assessed. Check C3/C4 for "residual".

**Prediction for unit 17.** Measure 1.3, Updating the Framework: Signatories will update the Framework as appropriate, at least annually (12 months?) after confirmation, and when there are material changes (to their processes, or to the state of the art, or after serious incidents). They'll include a changelog, and "confirm" the updated Framework per their process. Possibly a rule about "Framework assessment": a periodic review of whether the Framework is adequate.
