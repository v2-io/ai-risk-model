# Unit 5 · L48–49 · p.3: recitals (b) Contextual Risk Assessment and Mitigation, (c) Proportionality to Systemic Risks

**What it held.**
- (b) "**Principle of Contextual Risk Assessment and Mitigation.**" The Chapter "is only relevant for providers of general-purpose AI models with systemic risk and not AI systems". "However", assessment and mitigation "should include, as reasonably foreseeable, the system architecture, other software into which the model may be integrated, and the computing resources available at inference time because of their importance to the model's effects, for example by affecting the effectiveness of safety and security mitigations."
- (c) "**Principle of Proportionality to Systemic Risks.**" Assessment and mitigation "should be proportionate to the risks (Article 56(2), point (d) AI Act)". So "the degree of scrutiny…, in particular the level of detail in documentation and reporting, should be proportionate to the systemic risks at the relevant points along the entire model lifecycle." And, though assessment is "iterative and continuous, they need not duplicate assessments that are still appropriate to the systemic risks stemming from the model."
- Also: "this Safety and Security Chapter ("Chapter")" is a defined term.

**What differed.**
- The name "Contextual Risk Assessment and Mitigation" was one I wrote in my prediction. That's suspicious in a good way: it means my training holds the recitals' names, at least partly. It's exactly the priming the brief warns about. The *content* still surprised me, so the memory is of labels, not substance. I'll treat label-hits as evidence of priming, not of understanding.
- Proportionality is **to the risks only**. I predicted a provider-size element (SMEs). There's none here. Proportionality governs *scrutiny* and especially *documentation and reporting detail*, not whether to assess at all. A small provider with a high-risk model gets no discount from (c). (If SME relief exists, it must be elsewhere, probably in a separate recital or Measure.)
- (b) wasn't what I'd have guessed from its name. I'd have guessed "context of deployment / use cases". It's narrower and more technical: the *model's technical surroundings*: system architecture, integrating software, and **inference-time compute**. That last one is striking.

**Wandering.**

- **The model/system boundary.** The AI Act regulates GPAI *models* (Chapter V) separately from AI *systems* (high-risk, etc.). (b) holds that line ("only relevant for… models… and not AI systems") and in the next breath crosses it: you must assess the system architecture and the software the model goes into, "as reasonably foreseeable". So the unit of *obligation* is the model, but the unit of *assessment* is the model-in-reasonably-foreseeable-systems. That's a careful and important move. A fork asked "does the Code cover agents?" should answer: the Code governs models, not systems, but requires assessing the reasonably foreseeable system architecture and integrating software (b), which is where agent scaffolds live. Keep this in the expert's answer to every "model vs system" question.
- **Inference-time compute** is named as part of a model's context because it matters to "the model's effects", e.g. by "affecting the effectiveness of safety and security mitigations". That's a 2025-shaped sentence: reasoning models and test-time scaling, where more inference compute raises capability. Training-compute thresholds (the AI Act's 10^25 FLOP presumption) don't see this. So the Code acknowledges, in a recital, that the risk a model poses isn't fixed at training: what it can do depends on how much it's allowed to think. For lexicon work: "computing resources available at inference time" is a risk factor in its own right here.
- **Why "effectiveness of safety and security mitigations"?** Presumably because a mitigation tested at one inference budget may fail at a larger one (more attempts, more reasoning to work around a refusal), or because a security measure (rate limits) depends on the compute available. So mitigations have to be evaluated against the context they'll actually run in.
- **"As reasonably foreseeable"** is the limiting standard. A provider isn't required to assess every possible integration, only reasonably foreseeable ones. Who decides what's foreseeable? It isn't said. In tort law, foreseeability is judged objectively (what a reasonable provider would foresee), not by what the provider says it foresaw. Open-weight release would make almost any integration foreseeable. That's a point a fork should be ready for: how does (b) bear on open-weight models?
- **(c) cites Art. 56(2)(d) AI Act.** If I remember the Act, Art. 56(2) lists what codes of practice should cover, and point (d) concerns measures, procedures and modalities for assessing and managing systemic risks at Union level, "including the documentation thereof, which shall be proportionate to the risks, take into consideration their severity and probability". So proportionality to *severity and probability* would be the Act's phrasing. The Code here says only "proportionate to the risks". Watch for whether severity and probability appear later as the Code's components of risk. That would tell us its risk model: risk = f(severity, probability)?
- **"Need not duplicate assessments that are still appropriate."** A relief valve against the "continuous" burden of (a). A model update doesn't automatically trigger a full re-assessment if the old one is "still appropriate to the systemic risks stemming from the model". Who judges "still appropriate"? Presumably the signatory, documented. This will matter for model families and minor versions: a fork asked "does every new version need a new assessment?" should cite (c)'s non-duplication clause and then whatever Measure defines the triggers.
- Imagining the drafting room: (a) pushes hard (continuous, at least state of the art), and (c) is where industry's concerns get their answer: proportionate scrutiny, less documentation for lower risk, no duplication. The recitals are balancing each other. A reader taking (a) alone overstates the Code's demands; one taking (c) alone understates them.

**Questions to keep.**
- "Does the Code apply to AI systems or only to models?" (b): only models ("and not AI systems"), but assessment includes "system architecture, other software into which the model may be integrated, and the computing resources available at inference time" as reasonably foreseeable.
- "Does the Code take inference-time compute into account?" Yes, (b), as part of the model's context. A question someone *should* ask about compute thresholds and might not.
- "Is there relief for small providers?" Not in (c); its proportionality is to the risks. Look elsewhere.
- "Must every model update be re-assessed?" (c): not if the existing assessment is "still appropriate"; see later triggers.
