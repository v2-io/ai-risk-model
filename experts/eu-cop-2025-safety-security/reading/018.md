# Unit 18 · L141–147 · p.9: Measure 1.3, Updating the Framework (first part)

**What it held.**
- "Signatories will update the Framework **as appropriate**, including **without undue delay after a Framework assessment**…, to ensure the information in Measure 1.1 is kept up-to-date and the Framework is **at least state-of-the-art**."
- "For any update…, Signatories will include a **changelog**, describing how and why the Framework has been updated, along with a version number and the date of change."
- "Signatories will conduct an appropriate **Framework assessment**, if they have **reasonable grounds to believe** that the adequacy of their Framework and/or their adherence thereto **has been or will be materially undermined**, or every 12 months starting from their placing of the model on the market, whichever is sooner."
- Examples of grounds: "(1) how the Signatories develop models will change materially, which can be reasonably foreseen to lead to the systemic risks stemming from at least one of their models not being acceptable;" (list continues)

**What differed.**
- I predicted 12 months, material changes, changelog and a "Framework assessment" (memory again for the label). What I didn't predict:
  - The assessment covers **two** things: the Framework's *adequacy* **and** the signatory's *adherence* to it. So a Framework assessment is partly a self-audit of compliance with one's own Framework. That's stronger than "is our document still good?".
  - "**Has been or will be** materially undermined": forward-looking again. A foreseeable future undermining is grounds now.
  - The 12-month clock starts from "**their placing of the model on the market**", not from the Framework's confirmation. And "the model" singular again (cf. unit 14's wrinkle).
- The update is "as appropriate", not on a fixed schedule; the *assessment* has the fixed schedule.

**Wandering.**

- **Adherence as a trigger.** If a signatory has reason to believe it isn't following its own Framework (or soon won't), it must run a Framework assessment. That's a self-reporting loop for slippage: e.g. a race to release makes the team skip evaluations. The Code makes that recognisable as a formal ground. Whether anyone inside will invoke it is a culture question (8.3). Nice coupling between M1.3 and the risk-culture Measure.
- **"Reasonable grounds to believe"** is an objective-ish standard: not "if they believe", but if grounds exist that would make belief reasonable. A regulator can argue the grounds existed even if the signatory didn't act. That's the sort of standard enforcement can bite on.
- **Example (1) is subtle**: a material change in *how the signatory develops models* (new training paradigm, e.g. large-scale RL, or a move to agentic training) that can be reasonably foreseen to make risk from at least one model unacceptable. So a change in method, foreseen to break acceptability, triggers reassessment of the Framework itself, not just of the model. That means the Framework is expected to be method-specific enough that a paradigm shift can make it inadequate. A Framework written for pre-training-scale thinking would need reassessing when RL-heavy development arrives. That's precisely the 2024–25 transition.
- **The changelog requirement** is a small transparency gift to the regulator (and to us, if Frameworks are ever published): version, date, how and why. It makes Framework drift visible. A fork comparing a signatory's Framework versions has a hook in the Code itself.
- **"At least state-of-the-art"** recurs: the Framework must keep up with the state of the art, so an advance by another provider (recital (f)) can make a Framework out of date. Which is presumably another ground for assessment (I'd bet one of the examples).
- **12 months from market placement.** For a firm that never places a model on the market (internal only), the periodic clock never starts, and only the "reasonable grounds" trigger applies. Internal deployment again sits on the weaker leg. I keep finding this; it's a pattern, not an accident: the AI Act's jurisdictional hook is market placement, and the Code inherits it.

**Questions to keep.**
- "How often must a Framework be reviewed?" M1.3: a Framework assessment at least every 12 months from placing the model on the market, or sooner on reasonable grounds that adequacy or adherence "has been or will be materially undermined".
- "Does the Code require version control of Frameworks?" Yes, a changelog with how/why, version number, date (M1.3).
- Should be asked but might not be: "What if a provider isn't following its own Framework?" That's a ground for a Framework assessment (adherence "materially undermined").

**Prediction for unit 19.** More example grounds: (2) serious incidents or near misses suggesting the Framework is inadequate; (3) the state of the art changes materially; (4) the systemic risks change (new risks identified, models reach a new tier); (5) results of a Framework assessment by the AI Office? Then what a Framework assessment contains: a review of adequacy and adherence, perhaps with input from independent experts, and an update without undue delay.
