# Unit 2 · L32–39 · p.2: Objectives (overarching; A, B)

**What it held.** An "overarching objective" that restates AI Act Art. 1(1): improve the internal market, promote "human-centric and trustworthy" AI, ensure "a high level of protection of health, safety, and fundamental rights enshrined in the Charter, including democracy, the rule of law, and environmental protection, against harmful effects of AI in the Union", and support innovation. Then lettered "specific objectives":
- A: "To serve as a guiding document for demonstrating compliance with the obligations provided for in Articles 53 and 55 AI Act, while recognising that adherence to the Code does not constitute conclusive evidence of compliance".
- B: "To ensure providers… comply… and to enable the AI Office to assess compliance of providers… who choose to rely on the Code".

**What differed.**
- I predicted the "not conclusive evidence" disclaimer, and it's there (a hit, from memory or from genre). But I predicted it'd be scoped to Art. 55. It names **Articles 53 and 55**. Art. 53 is the obligation of *all* GPAI providers (documentation, copyright policy, training-data summary); Art. 55 is the extra one for systemic-risk models. So this Objectives section is **shared across the whole Code**, and has been repeated into the Safety & Security chapter unchanged. That's a strong hint that each chapter carries the same front matter. A fork asked "what does the S&S chapter say its purpose is?" should know that the Objectives are the Code's and not this chapter's own. The chapter-specific purpose (Art. 55 only) will presumably come later.
- The unit didn't open with Art. 56 (codes of practice). It opens with Art. 1(1), the Act's own purpose clause. So the Code positions itself as serving the Act's purpose, and not merely as an Art. 56 instrument.

**Wandering.**

- **"Conclusive evidence" is a careful legal word.** Adherence isn't conclusive evidence of compliance. It is still *some* evidence, and the AI Act itself (as I recall, Art. 55(2)) says providers "may rely on codes of practice… to demonstrate compliance… until a harmonised standard is published". So there's a gap between "may rely on to demonstrate compliance" and "not conclusive evidence". A signatory who follows the Code to the letter can still be found non-compliant. What would that look like? Presumably where the Code's "as appropriate" leaves room, and the AI Office reads "appropriate" more strictly than the signatory did. This is where AI Office guidance (my priming) will bite: interpretation authority sits with the regulator, not with the signatory.
- Objective B is oddly two-sided. "To ensure providers… comply" is an aim no voluntary code can have on its own; it's the Act's job. Read charitably, it means the Code is the mechanism by which compliance gets made concrete and checkable. "To enable the AI Office to assess compliance" makes the AI Office a second addressee: the Code is designed for the assessor as much as for the assessed. I expect later units to be shaped for auditability: documents the AI Office can read (Framework, Model Report), deadlines, records. The Code is in part a *reporting interface* between provider and regulator.
- "Who choose to rely on the Code": choice is preserved. Non-signatories demonstrate compliance some other way (the Act says "alternative adequate means"). So the population this text governs is self-selected. That matters for any claim like "the EU requires X of frontier developers": the Act requires; the Code requires only of signatories, and only as a means of demonstrating what the Act requires.
- The overarching objective lists **environmental protection** among the protected values. I doubt the Safety & Security chapter will say anything about environmental risk (compute, energy). If it doesn't, that's an inherited aim the chapter doesn't operationalise, a gap between stated objectives and specified risks worth noting for coverage views.
- "Harmful effects of AI in the Union": the geographic scope of harm is the Union. A systemic risk realised outside the EU (say, an AI-enabled bioweapon released elsewhere) falls under the Act's protection only via its effects in the Union. I'll watch whether the definition of systemic risk carries this "Union level" qualifier.
- Tone: the capitalised "Code" is a defined term ("Code"), and the Act is cited as "AI Act" without its regulation number. So a definitions/abbreviations apparatus exists somewhere, and the chapter assumes a reader who knows the Act.

**Questions to keep.**
- "Does following the Code mean a provider complies with the AI Act?" The answer is no, and in the Code's own words: "does not constitute conclusive evidence of compliance" (Objective A). Then point to whatever the chapter says about the AI Office's role.
- "Which articles does the Code serve?" Arts. 53 and 55 for the Code as a whole. Check where the S&S chapter narrows this to 55.
- A question someone should ask but might not: "Who is the Code written *for*?" Signatories, and the AI Office as assessor (Objective B). That changes how to read its detail.

**Prediction for unit 3.** Objective C, probably about the Safety & Security chapter itself, or about other chapters (Transparency, Copyright). Or the list ends and a new heading arrives. My best guess: a C stating that the Code is to be updated or reviewed (living document) and, possibly, an aim about supporting "the uptake of" the state of the art. Then perhaps a heading for this chapter's own preamble/recitals ("Preamble" or "Principles").
