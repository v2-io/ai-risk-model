# Unit 28 · L227–235 · p.12: Measure 3.2, Model evaluations

**What it held.**
- "Signatories will conduct **at least state-of-the-art** model evaluations **in the modalities relevant to the systemic risk** to assess the model's **capabilities, propensities, affordances, and/or effects**, as specified in Appendix 3."
- "Signatories will ensure that such model evaluations are designed and conducted using methods that are appropriate for the model and the systemic risk, and **include open-ended testing** of the model to improve the understanding of the systemic risk, with a view to identifying **unexpected behaviours, capability boundaries, or emergent properties**."
- "Examples of model evaluation methods are: Q&A sets, task-based evaluations, benchmarks, red-teaming and other methods of adversarial testing, human uplift studies, model organisms, simulations, and/or proxy evaluations for classified materials."
- "Further, the design of the model evaluations will be informed by the model-independent information gathered pursuant to Measure 3.1."
- **Conversion note:** the canonical text breaks a sentence across two lines ("unexpected behaviours," / "capability boundaries…"), at L231–233, probably a page or column break in the PDF. The sentence is one sentence. Quote it whole.

**What differed.** My prediction matched closely: the four objects (capabilities, propensities, affordances, effects), the example methods (including model organisms and proxy evaluations for classified materials), Appendix 3. That's memory, not insight. What I didn't predict:
- **Open-ended testing is required**, not just one example: evaluations "include open-ended testing". Every evaluation programme must have an exploratory component aimed at the unexpected.
- The aim "**capability boundaries**": finding where capabilities stop, i.e. maximal elicitation.
- Evaluation **design informed by** M3.1's information: a dependency from the model-independent step into evaluation design.

**Wandering.**

- **Four evaluation objects, and what they say about the Code's ontology of risk sources.** Capabilities (what it can do), propensities (what it tends to do), affordances (what it has access to / can act with), effects (what happens when it's used, e.g. on people). That's a four-part model of a model's risk-relevant properties. It maps well onto our project's needs: an agent's *capabilities*, *dispositions*, *access/tools*, and *impacts*. I'd expect Appendix 1.3 (sources of risk) to use the same four, or a superset. "And/or": not every evaluation addresses all four.
- **Effects as an evaluation object** is unusual: effects normally need deployment data (post-market monitoring). Evaluating effects pre-deployment means human studies (uplift studies, persuasion studies). It fits "harmful manipulation" as a risk.
- **"At least state-of-the-art" evaluations**: the recital (a) burden applied to evaluation specifically. Which means a signatory's evaluation methods are judged against the field's best, which (recital (e)) providers share and (recital (f)) providers advance. So evaluation science in public (papers, AISI reports) sets the floor for every signatory.
- **"In the modalities relevant to the systemic risk."** If the risk involves images (e.g. lab protocols in images, or visual manipulation), evaluate in that modality. Multimodal evaluation isn't optional where the risk is multimodal. A text-only eval suite on a multimodal model would fail M3.2 for risks where other modalities matter.
- **Open-ended testing for "unexpected behaviours… emergent properties"** is the Code's answer to unknown risks (the "unknown" in our causal chain's "risk events [recorded, ideated, unknown]"). The Code doesn't only test known threat models; it requires looking for the unforeseen. That's the evaluation counterpart of M2.1's process-identified risks. A fork asked "how does the Code handle unknown risks?" should cite open-ended testing here and process identification in M2.1.
- **"Proxy evaluations for classified materials"**: some knowledge (e.g. nuclear weapon design) can't be tested directly without handling classified information; proxies stand in. This implies government involvement in some evaluations, or at least a recognition that providers can't evaluate certain risks fully. It ties to M1.1(2)(d) (government input).
- **"Model organisms"** in a regulatory text: models deliberately trained to show a misaligned behaviour, to study it. Its presence is an alignment-research fingerprint (Anthropic coined the term in this sense, I believe). Correlation note: the Code's evaluation vocabulary draws on one lab's research programme; agreement between the Code and that lab's documents on evaluation methods isn't independent.
- Appendix 3 now carries most of the rigour. M3.2 points to it for *how* evaluations must be done. I'll read Appendix 3 hardest of all the appendices.

**Questions to keep.**
- "What must model evaluations assess?" M3.2: "capabilities, propensities, affordances, and/or effects", "in the modalities relevant", "at least state-of-the-art", "as specified in Appendix 3".
- "Does the Code require testing for unexpected behaviour?" Yes: evaluations "include open-ended testing… with a view to identifying unexpected behaviours, capability boundaries, or emergent properties".
- "Which evaluation methods does the Code name?" M3.2's list (examples, not mandatory).
- Should be asked but might not be: "Must a multimodal model be evaluated in all modalities?" In the modalities relevant to each risk.
