# Unit 1 · L12–28 · p.1: title page, Chairs and Vice-Chairs

**What it held.** Only the title ("Code of Practice for General-Purpose AI Models", "Safety and Security Chapter") and nine names: the Chair and two Vice-Chairs of each of Working Groups 2, 3 and 4. WG2: Matthias Samwald (Chair), Marta Ziosi, Alexander Zacherl. WG3: Yoshua Bengio (Chair), Daniel Privitera, Nitarshan Rajkumar. WG4: Marietje Schaake (Chair), Anka Reuel, Markus Anderljung.

**What differed.** I predicted the framing would start here. It didn't: the unit is purely authorial. That's itself information: the chapter presents itself first as *authored*, by named people, not as an institutional EU text. Three working groups put their names on one chapter, so this chapter is a merge of three groups' work. If I remember right, the drafting process had four working groups (1: transparency & copyright; 2: risk identification & assessment; 3: technical risk mitigation; 4: internal risk management & governance). The names fit that, and they'd predict the chapter's skeleton: assessment (WG2), mitigation (WG3), governance (WG4). Hypothesis to check: can I see the seams between the three groups' contributions in the prose, as differences of register or of vocabulary?

**Wandering.**

- **Correlation, not corroboration, starts on page 1.** Yoshua Bengio chairs WG3 here and also chairs the International AI Safety Report (IASR 2025/2026), which is another Au5 source. Daniel Privitera was, I think, IASR's lead writer. So two of the nine names on this chapter are central to IASR. Any agreement between the Code and IASR on what mitigations exist, or on risk categories, has a shared-authorship path. That's a fact a fork should raise unprompted whenever someone counts "the Code and IASR both say X" as two votes. Markus Anderljung (GovAI) has written a great deal on frontier AI regulation and safety cases. Nitarshan Rajkumar was at the UK AISI (I think). So UK-AISI-adjacent and GovAI-adjacent thinking is in here, and that's another lineage path, to AISI's documents. Hold these affiliations as memory, not as fact; the document itself names only roles.
- What the title page *doesn't* say: no date, no version, no "final" mark, no mention of the Commission or the AI Office as author. A reader holding only this page can't tell whether it's a draft or the published Code. (Our relata key says 2025; I believe the final was published 10 July 2025.) A fork asked "which version is this?" should answer from the text's own internal evidence, not from the key.
- The chairs are mostly academics and civil-society people, not lab staff. That fits the drafting process's design: independent chairs, stakeholders consulted. So the voice will be the chairs' voice, shaped by provider feedback. Places where the text is strangely hedged are likely to be negotiation scars.
- I notice an urge to read the names as credentials and so to raise my trust in the text. That's the wrong move for this project: it's an authority signal, not evidence. The right use of the names is lineage.

**Questions to keep (for the expert file).**
- "Who wrote this chapter, and who else did they write?" Should be asked whenever corroboration is counted across the Code, IASR, and UK AISI reports.
- "Is this the final text or a draft?" Answer only from the text's internal evidence.

**Prediction for unit 2.** Now the framing: probably a short preamble or "Objectives" heading, possibly lettered recitals. I expect a reference to the AI Act, Art. 55 and Art. 56, and an indication that the chapter is for providers of GPAI models with systemic risk only. Possibly an early statement that signing the Code is a means of demonstrating compliance but not conclusive evidence of it.
