# Unit 19 · L148–149 · p.9: M1.3 grounds (2) and (3)

**What it held.**
- "(2) serious incidents and/or **near misses** involving their models **or similar models** that are likely to indicate that the systemic risks stemming from at least one of their models are not acceptable have occurred; and/or"
- "(3) the systemic risks stemming from at least one of their models have changed or are likely to change materially, e.g. safety and/or security mitigations have become or are likely to become materially less effective, or at least one of their models has developed or is likely to develop materially changed **capabilities and/or propensities**."

**What differed.**
- Incidents as a ground: predicted. But two things widen it beyond my guess: **near misses** count, and incidents involving **similar models** (not the signatory's own) count. A serious incident with a competitor's comparable model can be grounds to reassess *my* Framework.
- **Propensities** appear for the first time, beside capabilities. That's a substantive concept: what a model *tends* to do (e.g. deceive, seek power, refuse, comply with misuse), not only what it *can* do. Its presence here means the Code's risk model isn't purely capability-based.
- **Mitigation decay** is named: mitigations becoming "materially less effective" (e.g. new jailbreaks, new attack techniques, or a model getting better at evading monitoring).
- No ground about the state of the art changing. I predicted one. The Framework must be "at least state-of-the-art" (first paragraph), but a change in the state of the art isn't listed as an example ground. It's covered only by the general "as appropriate" duty to update, plus "adequacy… materially undermined", which a state-of-the-art shift arguably causes. A gap in the examples, not in the duty.

**Wandering.**

- **"Similar models."** That's the cross-provider learning channel. If another lab's model with similar capabilities is used in a cyberattack, every signatory with similar models has grounds to reassess. It makes the industry a shared sensor network for incidents. But who tells the signatory about another provider's incident? Serious incident reports go to the AI Office (C9), confidentially. Unless incidents are public, or the AI Office relays them, a signatory may not know. So this ground works best for public incidents. A question worth asking: does the AI Office share incident information among signatories? I haven't seen it yet.
- **Near misses** are a safety-engineering concept (aviation's near-miss reporting). Including them lowers the threshold from harm to almost-harm. But near misses aren't (yet) defined. What's a near miss for an AI model? A red-team success that would have enabled harm? A blocked attempt to misuse? A model attempting self-exfiltration in a test? The Glossary may define it. A fork should note the term's presence and check for a definition.
- **Propensities**, and what they imply for loss of control. A model "has developed or is likely to develop materially changed… propensities". Models don't *develop* propensities after training unless they're trained further, or unless context changes reveal them. So "developed" here probably covers post-training changes and newly discovered propensities. This pairs with the loss-of-control risk: if a model shows a new propensity to resist shutdown in evaluations, that's grounds to reassess the Framework. The Code has the vocabulary to cover misalignment, not only misuse.
- **"Have become or are likely to become"**, "has developed or is likely to develop", "have changed or are likely to change": every ground is written in both actual and foreseeable form. Recital (g)'s precaution (extrapolate trajectories) shows up concretely: the Framework must be reassessed on foresight, not only on events.
- **"And/or"** between grounds: any one suffices. And the examples are "examples of such grounds"; the operative standard is the general one (reasonable grounds that adequacy/adherence has been or will be materially undermined). So a fork shouldn't treat (1)–(3) as exhaustive.

**Questions to keep.**
- "Does an incident with a competitor's model affect a provider's obligations?" M1.3(2): incidents or near misses "involving their models or similar models" are example grounds for a Framework assessment.
- "Does the Code consider model propensities or only capabilities?" Both; first at M1.3(3). Follow through.
- "What counts as a near miss?" Not yet defined. Check Glossary.
- Should be asked but might not be: "How would a signatory learn of incidents with similar models?" C9 reports are confidential; unclear.

**Prediction for unit 20.** The rest of M1.3: what a Framework assessment involves. Probably: assess whether the Framework's processes and measures are appropriate for the systemic risks; check adherence; consider the state of the art; "where appropriate" involve independent experts; and the result informs an update without undue delay. Then Measure 1.4 (notification to the AI Office): Signatories will provide the AI Office with (unredacted) access to the Framework and its updates within N weeks of confirmation.
