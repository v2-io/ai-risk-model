# Unit 23 · L179–188 · p.11: Measure 2.1, Systemic risk identification process (first part)

**What it held.** "Signatories will identify: (1) the systemic risks obtained through the following process:"
- (a) "compiling a list of risks that could stem from the model **and be systemic**, based on the types of risks in **Appendix 1.1**, taking into account:
  - (i) model-independent information (pursuant to **Measure 3.1**);
  - (ii) relevant information about the model **and similar models**, including information from post-market monitoring (pursuant to Measure 3.5), and information about serious incidents and near misses (pursuant to Commitment 9); and
  - (iii) any other relevant information communicated directly or via public releases to the Signatory by **the AI Office, the Scientific Panel of Independent Experts, or other initiatives, such as the International Network of AI Safety Institutes, endorsed for this purpose by the AI Office**;"
- (b) "analysing relevant characteristics of the risks compiled pursuant to point (a), such as their **nature (based on Appendix 1.2)** and **sources (based on Appendix 1.3)**; and"

**What differed.**
- The Appendix layout matches my prediction (1.1 types, 1.2 nature, 1.3 sources), so 1.4 is likely the specified risks. (Label memory again; I'll read Appendix 1 with care for that reason.)
- "**Could stem from the model and be systemic**": two filters at the compilation step. A risk is listed if it could stem from this model *and* could be systemic. Systemicness is an identification criterion from the start.
- (iii) answers the question I asked at unit 19 ("how would a signatory learn of incidents with similar models?"): the **AI Office** (and the Scientific Panel, and AI Office-endorsed initiatives such as the **International Network of AI Safety Institutes**) can communicate information to signatories, directly or via public releases, and signatories must take it into account. So the regulator is a hub that can feed risk information back to providers. I didn't predict a named role for the International Network of AISIs.
- (b) **analysing characteristics** (nature, sources) is part of *identification*. The Code splits "analysis" into two places: characterisation of risk types happens in C2; estimation in C3.

**Wandering.**

- **The AI Office as information hub.** Three channels into the identification step: the AI Office, the Scientific Panel (the AI Act's Art. 68 panel of independent experts, which can issue "qualified alerts" about systemic risks, as I recall), and "other initiatives… endorsed for this purpose by the AI Office". The AISI Network is the example. So an AISI finding (e.g. UK AISI's evaluations) could formally enter a signatory's risk identification, if the AI Office endorses the Network for this purpose. That's a lineage path from the AISI documents in our corpus (e.g. AISI's Frontier AI Trends Report) into every signatory's identification process. And a correlation note: if every signatory's identification is fed by the same AI Office communications, their risk lists will converge by design.
- **"Endorsed for this purpose by the AI Office."** The endorsement is purpose-specific. An initiative may be endorsed as a source for identification and not for something else. The AI Office curates whose information counts. That's quiet power: deciding which external bodies' risk information signatories must take into account.
- **"Similar models"** again, now in identification. The Code consistently treats information about similar models (presumably others' models of comparable capability) as relevant. It implies a notion of model similarity, undefined so far. Similar by capability? Architecture? Training compute? A fork should flag it as undefined unless the Glossary defines it.
- **"Model-independent information (pursuant to Measure 3.1)"**: forward reference to C3. So C3's first Measure is about gathering model-independent information: literature reviews, market analysis, expert interviews, I'd guess. Interesting that identification relies on an analysis Measure: the commitments aren't strictly sequential; they share information-gathering.
- **"Types", "nature", "sources"** are three different dimensions of a risk taxonomy (Appendix 1.1 types; 1.2 nature; 1.3 sources). In our project's terms: types are probably the *what* (e.g. CBRN, cyber, manipulation…), nature might be properties like severity, reversibility, speed, scale (the "systemic" features), sources might be model capabilities, propensities, affordances and context (the risk factors). That's my guess; the Appendix will say. It's a proper multi-axis classification, which this project should welcome: three bases of division, kept apart.
- Noticing myself: I'm now fairly confident of the overall architecture, and that's exactly when the skip reflex starts. The details are where the Code is surprising me (internal use, near misses, similar models, AI Office as hub). Keep reading at the level of the clause.
