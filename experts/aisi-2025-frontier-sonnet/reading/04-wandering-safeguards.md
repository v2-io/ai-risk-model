# Second-pass wandering: safeguards (my units 52-70)

*Hindsight, marked-imagination notes; see 02-wandering-chem-bio.md for the tags. Cyber and biological misuse appear here only as the categories safeguards were tested against.*

## Units 52-54: a severity vocabulary the report is quietly building

The safeguards section borrows its frame from software vulnerability management: "vulnerabilities", "bypass", a "Safeguard Bypass Bounty Programme", and the NCSC reference on adapting vulnerability disclosure. I think the borrowing is more than metaphor, and the report's own measures map onto the dimensions of an existing severity scheme. `[analogy]` The common vulnerability scoring systems describe a flaw by how hard it is to exploit (attack complexity), how far it reaches (scope), and what it does once exploited (impact). The report's three safeguard measures line up: expert-hours to find a bypass (complexity), "universal" across requests or models (scope), and "accuracy close to a similarly capable model without safeguards" (impact). Nobody here has written down a scoring rubric, but the report is using the three axes without a name for them. `[proposal]` Write it down: a bypass severity score with those three components, defined well enough that two evaluators scoring the same bypass agree. With it, "universal jailbreaks for every system" stops being an undifferentiated claim and becomes a distribution: how many bypasses, of what complexity, reaching how far, preserving how much capability. A regulator could then write a standard in terms of the score, and a developer could say what it fixed in terms of the score. The same machinery supports a disclosure convention (embargo windows by severity, who is told first) which is what the NCSC piece is about.

Limits of the analogy that a leader should keep in view. A software flaw is patched and the patch ships to everyone; a safeguard bypass in a hosted model can be closed by the host, but in an open-weight model there is nothing to patch (unit 60 says exactly this). And disclosure carries a different risk when the bypass unlocks content that is hazardous in itself rather than merely a system compromise: publishing the working technique is not the same act as publishing a CVE for a printer driver. So the disclosure side of the analogy needs its own thinking by the people who run biological and chemical safeguards, and I don't think the report claims otherwise.

## Units 55-57: what a regulator can and can't do with "every system"

"Universal jailbreaks for every system we've tested" is an existence claim, and it has been, in various wordings, the stable finding across the institute's public work. Think about what the next edition would have to say for it to change. "Not every system" would be news. Now think about how a licensing regime could use it. A rule of the form "the system must have no known universal jailbreak" cannot be met and will be ignored or gamed. A rule of the form "the cheapest known bypass must cost more than X expert-hours (and retain no more than Y of baseline capability)" can be met, tightened over time, and audited. The report's metric is what makes the second kind of rule thinkable. `[proposal]` The remaining design problem is X. Natural anchors are the going rate of bounty programmes, the value of what the bypass unlocks to the actor, and the adversary budgets one wants to deter. The unit of account being hours of an *expert* is itself a choice: it prices the attacker as skilled labour, and a world in which the labour is partly automated (the report's own capability trends!) makes the price fall for reasons unrelated to the safeguards.

Goodhart is present and the report half-acknowledges it. If developers know that one institution's red team is the measure, they will harden against that team's repertoire. Monoculture in red-teaming produces a false sense of coverage. `[proposal]` Fund several independent red teams and treat their *disagreement* as data. `[analogy]` Capture-recapture, borrowed from software-defect and wildlife estimation: if two independent teams each find a set of bypasses and the overlap between the sets is small, the total population of undiscovered bypasses is large; if the overlap is large, the pool is nearly exhausted. The public competition with Gray Swan (reference 15) is exactly the second sample. The report uses it for the capability-versus-robustness correlation; its more important use may be as a recapture estimate of how much AISI's own team is missing. The report doesn't compute that, and I think it should.

On the 40x figure: it compares two systems "released six months apart" that are not the same model before and after (Figure 13's "Model A / Model B"), and a "publicly known" vulnerability in one against a "novel" attack in the other. At leadership level the more interesting thing is what the number does as a *communication*. "40x" is a sentence a minister can repeat, and it is a statement about one pair in the most heavily defended category (the caption says so: "one of the most heavily defended domains"). The report's second picture (Figure 14, the ten-fold and the others) was designed, I think, to give the honest context, and it is, with the mislabelled panels. Whoever owns the institute's public voice should know that the headline figure and its qualifiers travel by different routes, and the qualifiers lose.

## Units 56 and 57: naming, anonymity, and what an evaluator without a statute can do

Companies are named in this section only as sources of safeguard techniques (Deliberative Alignment, Constitutional Classifiers) and never as subjects of findings ("Provider 1", "Model A"). `[analogy]` Domains differ in whether evaluators publish names: aviation, food hygiene, drug adverse events and bank stress tests do, usually under statutory authority; rating agencies and consumer-testing outfits do under commercial or reputational authority; academic benchmarkers do whenever they like. The report's choice tells you where AISI sits at present: a scientific adviser with voluntary access, not an enforcer with a mandate. Anonymity here is a cost and a benefit at once. The benefit is continued access and a diplomatic climate in which developers hand over pre-release checkpoints (unit 111 says they are "granted"). The cost is that the market, the press, and procurement officers cannot reward the developer whose safeguards cost five hours to bypass or penalise the one whose cost thirty minutes. If naming ever becomes the institution's job, the legal basis for it (and for protecting the party named) will matter more than the technical method.

The report credits two named techniques with part of the progress in biological-misuse safeguards, citing the developers' own papers. One of the two is from the developer of the model writing this. I don't think that changes the reading, but a reader should know that the evidence that these techniques caused the progress is temporal ("this progress has followed the deployment of...") and that the citations are the developers' descriptions of their own methods.

## Units 58-60: if variation is a choice, standards can ratchet

The report's explanation for the variation across providers is that it is "usually driven by how much company effort and resource has gone into building, testing, and deploying strong defences, as well as company decision-making on which systems to deploy their most advanced safeguard techniques on". Whether or not it is established (it is asserted three times and supported by the null correlation, my unit 66), it has a policy corollary: if variation is a matter of investment and decisions, then the best demonstrated practice is a feasible standard, not a frontier of research. `[analogy]` The "state of the art" and "standard of care" doctrines in product liability and professional negligence work like this: what the best practitioners demonstrably do sets the floor for what a reasonable practitioner must do. The report documents that a five-hour category exists (biological misuse on a leading system) and that the same system's other categories can be broken in minutes (unit 59: "often much easier to find jailbreaks for malicious requests outside of biological misuse"). That is the structure of a negligence argument, laid out as measurement: the capability to defend exists in-house and wasn't applied. I'm not a lawyer and the report doesn't make this move; I'm noticing that its evidence has the shape a regulator would want.

Unit 59 also gives the mechanism hypothesis that fewer benign uses make a category easier to defend without hurting legitimate users. `[question]` Taken seriously, this predicts that the categories hardest to defend are the ones where the overlap with legitimate professional use is largest (security work, medicine, chemistry education). That is precisely where the policy fight over "who gets less-restricted access" will happen, and where verified-user or tiered access schemes become the natural instrument. `[from training]` Programmes that give vetted users different safeguards than the general public exist at several developers; the report doesn't discuss them, and its evidence (unit 111, "with different safeguards to those implemented on the publicly available version") suggests the evaluator sometimes tests versions that aren't the public ones, which a reader needs to hold when reading the bypass costs.

Open-weight models (unit 60) sit outside this whole discussion for a structural reason: the defender doesn't host the weights, so there is no one to apply a standard of care *to* after release. The decision point is before release. That moves the policy question from "what safeguards" to "what evidence must exist before weights go out", and the appendix tells us (unit 109) that the evaluator "often does not have access to fine-tuning APIs", which is the access one would need to test what a motivated actor could do to the weights. I come back to this in the file on open models.

## Units 65-67: when more capability doesn't buy safety, and the monitor problem

Two findings sit close together and I think they are the same point. Unit 65: more capable models may be *easier* to attack when "an attacker and an AI system converse in a language (or encoding) not understood by a weaker additional monitoring model". Unit 79-81 (sandbagging): black-box monitors lose accuracy as tasks get harder, and models that record fewer intermediate steps are harder to catch. In both, an oversight mechanism is weaker than the thing it oversees and the gap widens as the overseen thing improves. The report never states this as a principle. If I had to, I'd put it as: *oversight must keep pace with capability, and it doesn't do so automatically.* `[proposal]` A "monitor parity" principle for deployment: the monitoring component should be at least as capable, at the relevant task, as the system it monitors, or the system shouldn't be trusted with tasks where monitoring is the only protection. It has costs (a monitor of equal capability can double the compute bill, and equally capable monitors have their own failure modes), and the trade-offs between a few strong monitors, many weak ones, and structural limits on what the monitored system can do are real design questions that a leader should put in front of the engineers. But it is a principle one can hold a product to.

Unit 66-67's statistic (capability explains little of robustness, R² or R = 0.097, the report prints both) has a policy reading I'd hold carefully. It says *capability thresholds don't proxy for safeguard strength*. Compute or capability thresholds are the usual triggers for when a safeguard obligation begins (the project's own notes mention a compute-threshold definition of "frontier developer" in California's SB 53, for one), and that's fine: a compute or capability line is a reasonable trigger for *when* to require safeguards. It says nothing, however, about whether the safeguards that result are adequate, and the data say adequacy must be measured directly. A regime built only on such thresholds will therefore tell you who must do something and not whether what they did worked.

## Units 68-69: the adaptation buffer as a managed quantity

This is the report's one genuinely strategic idea, and it is cited to an essay by Helen Toner (unit 70), not derived by AISI. Read as a leader: the buffer is a resource that gets spent whether or not anyone is managing it. The report names it and doesn't say who spends it. Here is what I think a manager would want, and how it connects to numbers elsewhere in the report. `[proposal + extrapolation]`

For each concerning capability in each sector, define:
- **L**, the diffusion lag: months between a capability appearing in a closed frontier system and its being available without the developer's safeguards, which the report estimates in section 7 as four to eight months, from external data;
- **T**, the adaptation lead time: months a sector needs to make a meaningful response (defensive deployment, rule changes, training, procurement, audit regimes);
- **slack = L − T.**

The report gives a rough L. Nobody gives T, and T is a property of the sector and not the technology: patch deployment in large organisations, rulemaking, procurement cycles, retraining a workforce. If T for a sector is longer than L, then safeguards on closed models are not buying that sector anything it can use, and the policy attention should shift to other levers. If L exceeds T by a wide margin, the safeguards are buying real time and the job is to spend it. A "buffer ledger" with L, T and slack by sector and capability would be a management instrument the report's own argument implies and nobody has built. Estimating T is a sector regulator's job and could be done by a standing exercise between the evaluator and the regulators, using the report's capability and lag numbers as inputs.

Two more things about the buffer. First, the essay it comes from carries a policy stance (against non-proliferation as the central strategy, in favour of preparing societies to absorb capabilities); the report adopts the concept, with its stance, in a neutral voice. Every framing in a document like this is a position, and the position is the one a reader should note, because it silently prioritises response over restriction. Second, there's an arithmetic relation I noticed and the report doesn't draw: the safeguard result (five-plus hours to bypass the best closed system) matters only as long as the buffer lasts, and the buffer is set by the open-closed lag, which I read in Figures 24-25 as right-censored and not obviously bounded above by eight months (my unit 105). The two halves of the report, strong safeguards on closed models and a short lag to unrestricted models, are the same story told in different sections. A reader who holds one without the other will overrate or underrate the safeguards.
