# Second-pass wandering: societal impacts (my units 85-100)

*Hindsight, marked-imagination notes; see 02-wandering-chem-bio.md for the tags.*

## Unit 85: what "closely coupled with capability progress" leaves out

The section's scope rule is that AISI studies societal impacts "closely coupled with capability progress, as opposed to more diffuse economic or environmental effects". It is an honest boundary and also a *streetlight*: the effects an evaluator's tooling can see are the ones it studies, and the rule describes the tooling as if it described the world. Labour-market change, concentration of economic power, energy and water use, dependence of institutions on a few providers, effects on education: those are diffuse in the report's sense, and they are also the kinds of change that tend to be large, slow, and nobody's job. `[question]` Who owns them? In most governments these sit with statistical agencies, competition authorities, regulators of particular sectors, and departments of education and labour, none of whose methods look like an evaluation team's. The report doesn't claim otherwise, and a minister who reads the "Societal impacts" heading and concludes that AISI has covered societal impacts has misread the scope. `[proposal]` One sentence in the next edition naming who is expected to cover the excluded categories would turn a boundary into a handoff.

## Units 86-92: persuasion, scale, and thinking in cost per person

Figure 18's slope (I read it as roughly 2 percentage points of attitude shift per tenfold of pre-training compute across the open-model series, up to about 9-10 points, with closed models clustered at 8-12) tells a researcher that scale helps. A decision-maker wants a different quantity. `[question]` What does a percentage point of attitude change cost to produce, and how is that cost moving? Persuasion of a population is an industrial process whose economics matter: the harm scenario is not one conversation moving someone by 10 points, but many conversations at nearly zero marginal cost, tailored per person, run by someone with a reason. In that world the relevant number is *cost per persuaded person*, and it falls in two ways: cheaper inference, and, as unit 89 notes, persuasion-specific post-training that lets smaller models "rival larger, more expensive ones". `[proposal]` Report effect per unit cost (compute dollars, or tokens) across the model and post-training conditions, so that the cost curve is on the page and can be compared with the costs of persuasion by other means. `[analogy]` Political science has long measured the cost-effectiveness of canvassing and mailers per vote moved; putting model persuasion on that axis would say whether it is a rounding error or a new channel.

Magnitude and durability need care. A few points of immediate attitude change from a single conversation can matter in a close contest, and also typically decay, and the report measures change right after the conversation (units 86-88). The sentence a leader might be tempted to take away ("AI is more persuasive as it scales") is true on the evidence shown; the sentence a leader needs ("will it change an election, and for how long") isn't answered. The report's own second study points the other way: no detected increase in belief in misinformation relative to search in the real-world setting (units 91-92). The honest reading is two results at different levels (a capability of the system in the lab, a measured effect in one real-world setting) that do not contradict each other and don't yet add up to a prediction.

The accuracy finding (units 90-91) is, to my mind, the most interesting thing in this subsection for a different reason: the strongest persuasion strategy was to flood the conversation with "verifiable facts", and that *lowered* the accuracy of what was said, by roughly 10-20 percentage points on the closed models tested (Figure 20, bottom panel, as I read it). Persuasion by volume. `[analogy]` It resembles what rhetoricians and debate coaches call a Gish gallop: more claims than anyone can check in the time available, each individually plausible. The mechanism doesn't need the system to lie. It needs only that the audience can't verify at the rate claims arrive, and it follows that the defence is not "make the model more truthful per claim" alone but also limits on verification load: who checks, at what speed, with what tools. The report leaves it there. I'd want the next edition to say who rated claims accurate (the figure caption says "rated as accurate (>50 on 0-100 scale)") and whether the raters were people or models; a model rating a model's claims is a circular instrument for exactly this question.

For the null result (unit 91-92), a leadership caution of the opposite kind. "No evidence that AI makes users less informed than search" is a result for one country, one election week, 2024-era systems, and self-reported use. The report itself says the question "is still an open research question" and that "other studies found otherwise" (unit 92, footnote 29, which is about deliberately deceptive systems). The reassuring finding will be the quotable one. A regulator tempted to cite it as showing the issue is settled should be reminded that the study measured something narrower, and the report says so.

`[proposal]` A cheap structural improvement: don't run one-off studies; add questions on AI use and on attitudes to the long-running national election and social-attitude surveys that already exist, so that a panel gives within-person change and a time series. The British Election Study and the like already have the sampling design the institute is rebuilding from scratch each time. Whether they would accept the additions is a separate question.

## Units 94-97: prevalence, incidence, and the missing risk difference

33% of 2,028 adults had used AI for emotional purposes in the last year, 8% weekly, 4% daily (unit 95). `[extrapolation]` For n = 2,028, the 95% interval on 33% is about ±2 points, and on 4% about ±0.9 points, and 4% daily is a very large number of people in an adult population of the UK's size (roughly one in twenty-five). I'm doing the arithmetic because a leader should have a sense of scale: even a very small *incidence* of serious harm among such users is a public-health-sized number, and a very small *benefit* is too. The report gives exposure (how many use it). It does not give, in what I read, the number needed to turn exposure into burden or benefit: the risk difference, i.e. how much more (or less) harm or wellbeing there is among users than among comparable non-users, and in whom. `[analogy]` That is the standard epidemiological decomposition: attributable burden = exposure × (risk among exposed − risk among unexposed). The report supplies the first factor and, in this subsection, the first factor only. The randomised trials that would give the second are listed as a method in unit 23 and defined in the glossary (unit 117), and no result appears in 6.2 as I read it. They are the piece to ask for.

The outage study (units 96-97) is a natural experiment with an unusual feature: the intervention is the withdrawal of a service. A volume surge of more than thirty times the hourly average and "large numbers of posts" describing withdrawal-like symptoms are about a *self-selected community of companion users*. They show that people in that community depend, not how many do or whether the dependence is harmful in the way "withdrawal" would imply. But as a signal of what institutional risk looks like, it is clearer than any survey item. A service that people lean on emotionally is run by a company, has a business model, and can change or end. `[analogy]` In healthcare and social care there is a developed ethic of continuity of care: abrupt termination of a therapeutic relationship is itself a harm, and providers are expected to plan transitions. Companion services have none of that obligation today. `[proposal]` The relevant duty is "notice and transition": advance notice of changes to persona or availability, an export of the user's own history, a defined handover to other help. It's a design requirement that does not depend on resolving whether dependence is harmful. This is a different framing from the report's, which stays at "conditions under which harm could occur" and "safeguards that could enable beneficial use", and I think it follows naturally from the data it presents: the harm that can be demonstrated at population level is *discontinuity*, not use.

One more observation about evidence. The report's evidence of harm in this subsection is a press report of an allegation (reference 30) and self-descriptions in a forum. That is the level the public debate runs at, and the institute's role may be to supply what the debate lacks: denominators and comparison groups. The severity of single cases and the weakness of the population data pull a leader's judgement in different directions, and the asymmetry (vivid cases, sparse incidence data) is typical of the early life of any new exposure.

## Units 98-100: agents with execution rights, and the oldest lesson in the book

Figure 23 classifies finance-focused tool servers into five levels of autonomy, and finds, in the last month of an eight-month window, a jump in the share at "execution with boundaries". I've said what's weak in the evidence (unit 100: one month, listings, an LLM classifier, publishers' claims unvalidated). Setting that aside and taking it as a weak early signal, the interesting thing is the classification itself. `[analogy]` Levels of automation are a regulatory device with a long history: driving automation has its numbered levels, aviation and maritime have theirs. What they buy is *an allocation of responsibility per level*: at level N, who is the driver, who monitors, who is liable. The report's five levels (read trusted source; read/analyse unrestricted; execution enabler; execution with boundaries; execution unrestricted) are built from the permissions a tool grants, not from what the model can do, which is the correct basis for a responsibility scheme. A financial regulator could take it as a starting vocabulary and ask, per level, what controls must be in place before an agent may operate there. `[proposal + from training]` The history of automated trading is the lesson: after market events driven by algorithms acting faster than people could supervise them, regimes added pre-trade limits, circuit breakers and kill functions. "Execution with boundaries" is the report's name for the idea that the boundaries are the control. The question to ask of every Level 4 server is what the boundary is, who set it, who can change it, and whether the model can reach the thing that sets it. The report couldn't know that from public listings, as it says ("does not validate capability or access claims by publishers").

I'd also point out how the two parts of the report fit: the capability section measures that models can obtain money and compute in a simplified setting, and this subsection records that tool providers are granting execution rights over money. A single reader's question: if an agent that is capable at the first is given the second, with what oversight? That belongs to a financial-sector resilience owner and not to an evaluator, and it is the clearest place in the report where the evaluator's output should be handed to someone else's desk.
