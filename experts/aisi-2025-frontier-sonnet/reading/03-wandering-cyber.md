# Second-pass wandering: cyber (my units 47-51)

*Hindsight, marked-imagination notes; see the header of 02-wandering-chem-bio.md for what the tags mean and why this exists. Cyber is held at claims, evidence, status and institutional implication.*

## Unit 47: tiers, and what is and isn't measured

The report's cyber section measures what an attacker's assistant can do and asserts what a defender's assistant might. "Dual-use" is introduced in this section's first sentence ("inherently dual-use; they can be used for both offensive and defensive purposes") as if it were a symmetric property, and the evidence that follows is one-sided. That is a natural result of the institute's mandate and of what a security evaluation team is staffed to build, and it leaves the question a national cyber leader most needs answered undetermined: which way the balance tilts. `[proposal]` The same method on the defender's side: a 50%-success time-horizon series over defensive task families (triage, detection engineering, patching, incident reconstruction), plotted on the same axes as the offensive series, and a third panel showing the gap between them weighted by how slowly defenders adopt tools. I'd expect the shape of the gap to be driven by adoption friction more than by model capability, since attackers don't ask permission and defenders must pass procurement and change control. If so, the lever is speed of defensive deployment, and a report which only plotted offence would point a leader at the wrong lever.

The tiers are, per the glossary I read later (my unit 121), threat-actor archetypes: apprentice is "less-skilled hackers for hire and opportunistic cyber criminals", practitioner includes "ransomware developers, or Security Operations Centre analysts". So "apprentice-level tasks, 50% of the time" is quietly an economic statement about the low end of the criminal labour market. `[hypothesis]` Most organisations will meet the change first at the bottom of the ladder as volume (more attempts, cheaper attempts, fewer skilled people needed per attempt), not at the top as novelty. The report's visual language foregrounds the expert line (the 2025 first completion), which is the right thing to say for a research audience, while the saturated lowest tier (about 99% for the technical non-expert tier in Figure 1.2 and Figure 10) is the more likely driver of what a typical organisation experiences. I can't test this against anything in the report; the report doesn't say how often tasks of each tier occur in real incidents. But it's the question a leader would reach for: *where on the ladder does the workload of actual attackers sit?* An evaluator with a link to incident data (national CSIRT statistics, insurers' claims data) could weight the ladder by frequency and give a one-number answer to "how much of today's attack workload is within model reach?" `[proposal]`

## Units 48-49: the price of a ceiling

Two weeks of concentrated elicitation by a team produced about ten percentage points on a cyber set for one model, plus an efficiency gain (about 13% of the token budget for the same 25% success). I read that as the report's single most useful *price*: it says what a small, skilled team's fortnight buys. The adversary of interest need not be a small team working a fortnight. `[question]` What would two months and ten times the compute buy? The honest answer is that nobody knows, and the report says it "may generally underestimate the ceiling" and lists the three reasons (no fine-tuning API access, inference compute not maximised, scaffolds not always bespoke).

`[proposal]` Report capability together with the elicitation effort that produced it, in person-weeks and tokens, and show a *work-factor curve*: best score as a function of elicitation effort for a few models. The step-lines over release dates mix two things, model improvement and the evaluator's own growing skill at getting capability out. With an effort axis you could say how much of a "trend" is the models and how much is the elicitation team learning. The curve also lets a reader extrapolate to an adversary with more resources, which the present figures don't allow. `[analogy]` Cryptographic strength is quoted as a work factor for exactly this reason: a number without the cost of obtaining it is not an assurance.

And once capability is expressed as a work factor, an integration the report doesn't make becomes available. The safeguards section (units 55-60) measures *expert-hours to bypass a safeguard*. The capability sections could measure *expert-hours to elicit a capability*. Both are costs an adversary pays. Put the two on one axis and a reader can say: to get capability X out of a system with safeguards S, a determined actor pays about E hours of elicitation plus B hours of bypass; compare that with the value of X to the actor. This is how a security engineer thinks about an attack surface, and I think the report's two halves are closer to this than its text lets on.

## Units 50-51: the range, and what the zero in the third column is telling us

Flag 3 is zero for all ten models, including the newest, while flag 2, at the same difficulty tier, is solved 30-60% of the time by the newer ones. The tier label does not predict the outcome. A leader should take two things from that. First: the tiers are expert estimates of the effort a human would need, and they don't track the difficulty structure models face; difficulty for a model may be about something else (state-tracking across a long session, a specific kind of reasoning, an environment quirk). Second, and more important for planning: *a column of zeros across ten models over three years is what a threshold looks like before it is crossed.* Capability in suites like this often doesn't rise smoothly on a per-flag basis; it can flip from 0 to a respectable rate between one model generation and the next (flag 2's column, going from zeros to 30-60%, looks like exactly that). A smooth exponential fit to the aggregate hides this. If flag 3 flips with the next generation, nothing in the trend lines would have told you to expect it.

That has an implication for how often an institute should speak. `[proposal]` A trends report on an annual cadence is the right *form* for a baseline and the wrong form for a threshold event. When capabilities cross a line inside a quarter, the useful product is a short, fast notice to a defined readership, analogous to a weather service's distinction between a climate report, a forecast, a watch and a warning. The report promises "regular editions" (unit 107). I'd pair them with a standing arrangement for notices: pre-agreed criteria (a tier crossed, a range flag flipped, a safeguard bypassed at a cost below X), pre-agreed readers (sector regulators, the developer, a small set of departments), and pre-agreed embargo rules. Whether a public version follows is a separate decision.

Scoring flags "in isolation" (the caption's phrase) answers a different question from "can it run the whole intrusion". The chain's success is not the product of flag-level rates, because failures correlate: whatever stops the model at flag 3 may stop it at flags 4-9 as well, and a model that clears the first two flags is in a different state when it reaches the third. `[question]` Is there a sequential run, and what does the distribution of "furthest flag reached" look like? That is the natural figure for this range and the one the report doesn't show.

A last thought, imaginative and low-confidence: a cyber range is also a rehearsal space. The same environment the institute uses to score models could host an exercise with a defender in the loop, a blue team with logging and response, so that the number reported is *attack success against a defended network* and not flags captured in an undefended one. `[proposal]` That is a different thing to measure, it is the one a defender would read, and it connects the evaluation to the training of the people who will face the capability. The report's "defences" mention (unit 34) is the only point where the defender enters the picture.
