# Second-pass wandering: open models, conclusion, appendix, back matter (my units 101-123)

*Hindsight, marked-imagination notes; see 02-wandering-chem-bio.md for the tags.*

## Units 101-105: the lag is the clock for everything else

The open-closed gap gets three pages and no recommendation, but I think it is the quantity on which the rest of the report's practical meaning hangs. Safeguards on closed systems are worth something only for as long as it takes unrestricted systems to catch up; the adaptation buffer (units 68-69) is that interval seen from the defender's side; the persuasion finding that small open models can be post-trained to rival larger ones (unit 89) is the same lag compressed in one domain. If the lag changes, the value of several other findings changes with it.

`[question]` How is it estimated? The report's two estimators are both "time from a closed frontier model's release to the first open model matching it" on one benchmark family (the AA index, "around 4 months") and another (METR time-horizon tasks, "around 8 months (upper bound)"). Reading the figures (my unit 105), both series have newer closed models that no open model has matched yet: three on the AA chart, four on the METR chart, the oldest of the unmatched at 6.2 months and counting. Those lags are *right-censored*: we know they are at least that long, and the estimate that drops them is biased low exactly where the frontier is. `[proposal]` Treat it as time-to-event data: survival methods (Kaplan-Meier curves with the unmatched models as censored observations) give a median lag and a distribution that includes the models not yet matched. That is the number a planner needs ("with probability one half, matched within M months"), and it is the number most likely to be longer than the headline. The phrase "upper bound" is used for the doubling time (a conservative growth rate) and for the lag (a ceiling on the delay) in the same report, and these point in opposite directions in terms of caution; a reader doing planning should treat the 8 months as a *recent matched value* until the survival version exists.

Two benchmarks giving different lags is itself informative, and the report offers two hypotheses (units 103: benchmark-optimisation by non-leading developers, or open models struggling with long-horizon agentic tasks). A leader can use the disagreement: where the risk is about knowledge-and-text tasks, the four-month figure is the relevant one; where it is about long-horizon agentic autonomy, the eight-month figure (or longer) is. Each of the report's risk domains maps to one or the other. That mapping would make the lag domain-specific, and I think it is the right form for the buffer ledger I described in 04.

## The evaluator's blind spot on the exact question open weights raise

Put three of the report's statements side by side:
- Open-weight safeguards "can be quickly and cheaply removed" and "jailbreaks and other weaknesses can't be patched" (units 60, 102).
- "More permissive access types - such as access to fine-tuning APIs ... may also introduce additional vulnerabilities" (unit 60).
- The appendix: "We often do not have access to fine-tuning APIs, do not maximise inference time compute, and do not always conduct bespoke agentic scaffolding experiments" (unit 109).

The release of open weights is a decision after which no further control exists. The question for that decision is "what could a motivated actor do with these weights after adversarial fine-tuning". The evaluator says, in the appendix, that it often cannot test with fine-tuning. So for the very decision the report's section 7 is about, the evaluation the decision needs is the one the evaluator describes itself as often lacking access to. `[from training]` The practice sometimes called worst-case or adversarial fine-tuning evaluation exists in the field for this reason: before release, fine-tune the model toward the harmful capability with a realistic budget, and test the result. `[proposal]` For a leader, the implied ask is a precondition: no release decision for open weights without a worst-case fine-tuning evaluation, run or audited by someone independent of the releasing party, with its budget stated. The report doesn't say that. Its facts imply it, and I can't tell whether the institute has the access arrangement for it in the cases that matter.

There's a counter-point the report lists among the benefits, and which is easy to pass over: "wider independent scrutiny, and diverse oversight" (unit 101). Open artifacts are what an independent evaluator can study *without anyone's permission*, with fine-tuning and white-box access (the report's own probe work on sandbagging, units 79-81, used internal-activation methods). The evaluator's dependence on developers' goodwill (unit 111: "we are granted API access") is a cost of closedness. A policy that restricts open weights should therefore be asked what it does to the capacity for independent evaluation, since a world with only closed frontier models is a world in which the evaluators' access is a gift. I don't think this settles anything; it means the open/closed question has an evaluation-capacity term in it that rarely appears in the debate.

## Definitions that carry legal weight

Three formulations of "open-source" (units 10, 101, 119), each differing on training data versus training process, and no mention of licence terms. `[from training]` Some regulatory regimes attach exemptions or lighter duties to models released under open-source licences, so what counts as open-source can decide who owes what. That makes the report's looseness more than a lexical matter for anyone who borrows its definitions into a rule. For this project's lexicon: record the report's three formulations separately with their locations, and note that figure legends use the term for models that are probably weights-only releases.

## Units 106-107: a conclusion with no addressee

The conclusion lists needs ("safeguards that keep pace with accelerating capabilities, rigorous and independent evaluations to track emerging impacts, and collaboration across government, industry, and academia") with no subject, and I think that's a convention of government publishing and also a real weakness: a need without an owner is something everyone agrees with and no one does. `[proposal]` For the next edition, a short "who should do what" annex addressed in turn to developers, evaluators, sector regulators and funders: concrete, dated, with the report's evidence cited per ask. If the institute can't make asks because of its position, then the annex could be framed as *questions each audience should be able to answer*. Whichever form, the move from description to addressed implication is the step that turns a trends report into an instrument.

There's a second effect of becoming a series that a leader should plan for: the measure becomes a target. Once labs know the cyber time-horizon is the number that gets quoted, there is pressure to optimise it, to publicise it, to build against the institute's task families. The report's private suites reduce this and don't remove it. Series designers handle the opposite tension, comparability across editions versus refinement of the method, with an overlap rule. `[analogy]` Price indices do it by chain-linking: when the basket changes, you run the old and new baskets in parallel for a period so that the two series can be spliced. For the AISI series: a frozen core suite for continuity, a rolling suite for fresh coverage, and an overlap period when both are run so that a break in method isn't read as a break in the world. Meanwhile "we will continue to refine our methodology" (unit 107) is the sentence that tells a reader to expect a break.

Finally, the conclusion names benefits ("breakthroughs in research, healthcare, and productivity") that nothing in the report measured. The asymmetry is by mandate and not by accident: the institute evaluates risk. For a reader balancing the two, though, the report has the shape of a ledger with one column filled in by measurement and the other by assertion. The right response is a companion from someone with the mandate and tools to measure benefit, with a comparable evidentiary standard, so that the report is not mistaken for the whole account.

## Units 108-113: the appendix as the report's most useful part

`[reading]` It is, for the first time, where the report says *which way its errors point*. Two directions are named: results may underestimate the ceiling of capability (no fine-tuning access, inference compute not maximised, scaffolds not always bespoke), and lab-task performance "may not reflect or generalise to real world effectiveness where factors like latency, cost, and integration challenges apply" (unit 109). A leader should hold both and not average them mentally. `[proposal]` A small table of the form (claim, bias direction, plausible magnitude, evidence for the magnitude) for each headline number, kept in an annex, would make the "bias budget" explicit. For the cyber doubling time the two arrows point the same way ("an estimated upper bound"); for the real-world effectiveness of a lab-measured capability they point against each other.

A budget thought, from the institution's side. The appendix describes an evaluator working under constraints of access, time and effort: the fortnight of elicitation behind Figure 11 is, by the report's own account, the kind of effort that raises measured capability by ten points. The developers' training budgets are very large. `[question]` What is the ratio of independent evaluation effort to training effort, and is there a sense of what it ought to be? Fields with independent assurance have norms for this (audit fees, trial budgets as proportions of development cost), and none of them is obviously right. But naming the ratio makes the capacity of the oversight function a visible number. `[proposal]` I'd publish it.

The dating rule (unit 110) and the access conditions (unit 111) combine in a way I think a decision-maker should keep in mind: plotted dates are initial release dates, evaluated checkpoints may be later, some access is pre-release and sometimes "with different safeguards to those implemented on the publicly available version". So a figure is a statement about *some build, evaluated some time, under some safeguard configuration*, plotted at a date that may precede it. `[proposal]` Evaluation provenance: for each evaluated system, a record of build identifier, evaluation dates and safeguard configuration, held by the evaluator and disclosed to those who need it. It is the equivalent of a chain-of-custody record, and it is what lets a later reader tell whether two data points are comparable. The report withholds names for good reasons; provenance can be kept without naming.

## Units 114-123: references, glossary, back cover

The reference list is short enough to count: by my count eleven of the thirty-seven entries are AISI's own outputs (seven in the first twenty-three), seven are METR or Epoch, three are Anthropic-authored (the classifiers paper, the sandbagging paper, the system card), one OpenAI, one government body, one press article. For a leader the point is that the *measurement ecosystem* the report stands on is small. METR's method sits under the report's headline doubling time and its generalisation claim; Epoch under its compute estimates; Artificial Analysis under the four-month lag; Gray Swan under the robustness correlation. A claim that leans on several of these can look corroborated while resting on a few shared methods, which is the project's own concern about correlation and corroboration in miniature. `[proposal]` For a funder: a healthy evaluation ecosystem has several independent measurement traditions on the same questions, with different task designs and different human baselines, and the field currently looks thinner than that.

The glossary defines *safeguards* as "technical measures implemented by AI companies to prevent users from eliciting harmful information or actions from models" (unit 120). That definition decides what the claim "safeguards are improving" can be about: it excludes account-level controls beyond bans, customer verification, legal liability, enforcement, supply-side controls, and whatever sits outside the company. I take it as a considered scope for a technical evaluator and I'd flag it for anyone using "safeguards" in a policy document as a synonym for "controls": on this definition most of the controls that constrain actual misuse are not safeguards.

And the back cover has three nouns: SAFETY, SECURITY, OPPORTUNITY (unit 123). The report's content distributes across them in an interesting way: safety (loss of control, societal impacts), security (misuse, safeguards), opportunity (asserted, not measured). An institution with all three words in its public face has to decide, on the days they conflict (what to publish, what to withhold, whom to name), which it is being. The report's choices of withholding methodology for high-risk tasks and anonymising models are choices for security and continuing access over public accountability. I don't think those choices are wrong at this stage; they are the institution's position, stated by behaviour, and a reader should read the report as written from inside it.
