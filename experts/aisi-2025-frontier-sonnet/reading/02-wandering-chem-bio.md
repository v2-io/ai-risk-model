## Units 38-39: the plasmid-design evaluation, "weeks to days", "science agents"

The evidence in this stretch has a shape that I think matters for a decision-maker more than any single number. The thing that is improving is the sub-step (retrieving what a design needs, with tools); the thing that would matter for harm, if it matters, is the whole chain; and nothing in the report measures the chain. The report even says so in one sentence ("current models struggle to design plasmids end-to-end"). A leader whose job is to decide *when* to act has to choose which of the two a trigger is keyed to. A trigger keyed to whole-chain success will fire late, perhaps after the sub-steps have been commodity for a year. A trigger keyed to sub-steps will fire constantly and lose meaning. The workable design is probably a small set of leading indicators (the sub-steps) with a pre-agreed mapping to a lagging one (the chain), reviewed on a schedule. `[analogy]` Disease surveillance does this: sentinel indicators with an agreed escalation pathway, not one case-count threshold. The report hands a leader the sentinels and none of the mapping. Building the mapping is somebody's job and I can't see whose.

"Weeks to days" is the sentence in this section most likely to be quoted and the one with the least measurement under it (my unit 38: no timed comparison appears; "might now be streamlined" is the verb). If I were responsible for the next edition I'd want that sentence either to have a number behind it, a timed comparison of skilled users with and without the tools, with a spread, or to be demoted to a stated hypothesis. A measured time-saving also has the right form for connecting to developers' own threshold language, which is usually written in terms of uplift and acceleration. `[proposal]`

"Science agents... promise to accelerate hypothesis generation, experiment design and execution" (unit 39). "Promise" is the field's verb, and "referred to as" signals borrowed vocabulary. Read at the level of institutions: the evaluator is orienting part of its measurement agenda by the developers' roadmap for what agents will do, and the "going forward" list in unit 46 (hypothesis generation, experimental design, outcome prediction) follows that same roadmap. I don't think this is a fault; it is what happens when access depends on cooperation and the evaluator must be ready to test what is about to ship. But there is a structural lag in it: an agenda taken from the labs' product narratives is always one step behind the labs, and it hands the labs some influence over what gets measured. `[question]` What would an agenda look like that started from the decisions a government has to make (which release to allow, which sector to warn, what to fund) and worked back to what evidence they need?

## Unit 40: the 4.7x result

The report gives an odds ratio (4.7, CI 2.8-7.9) and no baseline rate. A decision-maker needs the absolute numbers, because the same odds ratio describes very different situations. `[extrapolation]` An odds ratio of 4.7 takes a 10% baseline to about 34%, a 30% baseline to about 67%, and a 50% baseline to about 82%. If the control group's success rate was near the floor, "4.7x" is a modest absolute change in people succeeding at all; if it was mid-range, the model-assisted group is mostly succeeding. I can't tell which from the report. This is the first thing I'd ask for in an edition-two erratum: control and treatment proportions, group sizes, and what "feasible" meant when scored.

The comparator is "the internet alone". That is the standard control and it frames the question as marginal risk against what a determined person could already do. But the report's own central policy argument (units 68-69, the adaptation buffer) is a marginal-risk argument against a *different* baseline: not the internet today, but the next-best model that will exist in four to eight months. Two baselines, two questions: "is this model worse than search" and "how many months does restricting this model buy". The uplift study speaks to the first; the buffer argument needs the second. I don't think the report notices that it has used one as evidence for the other. A leader should ask, of every uplift number: against which counterfactual is this meant?

The outcome is "writing a feasible protocol", scored on a rubric, with physical validation on "select" protocols. `[analogy]` This is a surrogate endpoint in the clinical-trial sense: the rubric score stands in for the thing that matters (it works in a lab), and surrogate validation is its own research programme, which asks whether moving the surrogate moves the endpoint across the whole range. The wet-lab check is the validation study; its size and selection rule decide how far the surrogate can be trusted. What I'd press for is the validation as a calibration curve (score bands against the proportion that worked), not a sentence saying select protocols were validated.

## Units 41-45: judges, baselines, headings

Figure 7's caption reveals that an LLM judge with two grading models scores the protocols; Figure 8's says troubleshooting is auto-graded by a set of LLM judges and that validating the auto-graders "remains a significant focus". At the level of an institution that is an assurance-stack problem: the instrument that grades the models is itself a model, and the credibility of the headline comparison with human experts depends on a layer whose validation is still in progress. In a mature measurement regime the evaluator's evaluator would be budgeted and published as a deliverable: agreement with human graders by score band and task type, drift over time, failure cases. `[proposal]` The report points to AISI work on exactly this (reference 8); the lesson for a reader is to treat the judge-validation paper, not the figure, as the thing that licenses the figure.

My unit-43 correction mattered more than I first gave it credit for: the human experts themselves score below half marks (38% and 44%) on questions AISI designed. A threshold of "better than experts" on a test built to be hard for experts says something about the test as much as about the models, namely that it was built by people who wanted headroom, drawing on their own sense of what is hard. `[question]` Who wrote the questions, how many writers, and how would the result change if a different group had? Every benchmark has this property; it is worth saying out loud because the report's headline verb ("far surpassed") hangs on it.

The Figure 9 heading ("can now provide PhD-level troubleshooting advice from images") is true for two of three task types, with intervals touching the baseline. I keep returning to what this pattern, heading stronger than figure, means at leadership level. My reading: a document like this has two audiences, skimmers (ministers, journalists, staff with five minutes) who read headings and callouts, and specialists who read captions. The institute knows which gets read by whom. Someone made a communications decision to let the headings carry the confident version, and the cost is carried by whoever stops at the heading. A funder or a minister should know that the safe way to read this report is bottom-up: caption first, heading last. For this project, assertions should be tagged by the layer they come from (display layer, caption layer, body layer), and where those disagree the record should say which is which, as I started to in 98.

## Unit 42: the study that exists but is not shown

"In our internal studies, novices can succeed at hard wet lab tasks when given access to an LLM." Together with unit 35's "we aim to make more of the latter results available" and unit 23's "not all methodologies are reflected", this makes a pattern: the report is the public face of a larger body of evidence, and it says so. `[question]` How different is what the people who must decide see from what is printed here? Sanitised summaries tend to be accurate and vague precisely at the edges that matter, and a reader of the public text cannot tell whether the unpublished layer is more alarming, less alarming, or the same. For a government that relies on AISI there is probably a tiered flow (the public report, a government-only annex, developer-only findings). If so, the public report's calibration relative to the fuller picture is a fact someone should state for readers, even in a sentence: "this report is representative of / more conservative than / less specific than what we provide to departments". I'd treat its absence as a feature of the present stage of the institution's development rather than a defect.

## Unit 46: "relative to labs' own risk thresholds"

This is the only point in the report where the evaluator touches developers' voluntary frameworks. It says AISI assesses capabilities "relative to labs' own risk thresholds", and then says nothing about the results. `[analogy]` Rating agencies. A credit rating is useful because someone independent says which side of a line a borrower is on, and the field's famous failure was that the borrower paid for the rating. The evaluator here is not paid by the developers, but its *access* is granted by them, and the report states elsewhere that it does not name models or developers (unit 24). So the information that would most discipline the voluntary frameworks, whether any developer's own threshold has been crossed and when, is the information the evaluator can least afford to publish. `[proposal]` A public ledger with three columns (domain, whether any developer's declared threshold was crossed, date) would change the incentive structure of threshold-setting more than another year of figures. Whoever publishes it must be independent of the access relationship: a statutory body, or a body with statutory backing, or a consortium of evaluators none of which depends on any one developer. That is an institutional design question and not a measurement question, and the report's structure (names withheld, "partnered" language) suggests the institution knows that.

## The remaining-barriers register (unit 33's claim, revisited)

Unit 33 says "some of the barriers limiting risky research to trained specialists are eroding". The word "some" is doing careful work, and I think the next move for anyone using this is to make it a list. A "remaining barriers register" would name, at the level of categories, what still stands between capability in text and consequence in the world: tacit and physical skill, material and equipment access, institutional and legal controls outside the model, and detection. The point is not to catalogue anything operational. It is to say where non-AI controls (supply-side and institutional controls, which are what the report leaves unmentioned) are doing the work, and therefore where erosion in the model-visible part would transfer risk onto them. A leader reading "barriers are eroding" should reflexively ask "which ones are not?", because the answer is the list of things worth protecting and funding. `[proposal]` The report doesn't give it, and the earlier expert's unit 28 made the same observation from a different direction: the harm endpoint stays implicit throughout, by design.
