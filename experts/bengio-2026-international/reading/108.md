# 108 · L1156–1164 · p.54 · "Real-world use of AI to influence people is documented but not yet widespread"

As predicted, "documented but not yet widespread". Specifics:
- **Commercial influence is named as a category:** "companies are beginning to place **sponsored content in AI chat conversations** or deploy **AI sales agents**". Advertising inside assistants, mentioned neutrally. Influence by deployers for profit, an actor not usually listed under "malicious use".
- **Companion harms, the gravest sentence so far:** "some users have developed strong emotional dependence, **delusions**, or even **taken their own lives** after extended interactions with chatbots, though investigations into these incidents is ongoing". Deaths are acknowledged, with causality held open ("investigations … ongoing"). Pointer to 2.3.2. (A grammatical slip, "investigations … is ongoing", is the source's.)
- **Consumers using AI to influence others:** AI-written complaints "**9 percentage points** more likely to secure compensation". A benign or neutral real-world effect.
- **The counter-evidence:** "Investigations **by AI providers** into AI-powered influence operations have found **little evidence that people widely shared the content**", and "only around **1%** of content flagged as misleading on social media is classified as AI-generated".
  - The first comes from the *providers* (OpenAI's and Meta's threat reports), who have an interest in the conclusion that misuse of their tools had little effect. The report doesn't note that. Evidence on low impact from interested parties is worth flagging in the project's provenance records. (Not a claim that the finding is wrong.)
  - "1% of flagged misleading content": flagged by whom (Community Notes?), classified how? Classification of AI-generated content is itself unreliable (detectors weak, 098). So "1%" could undercount. The report doesn't note that either. *My inference*, held tentatively.
- **Theoretical limits:** distribution costs exceed generation costs; real-world stakes make people less willing to change beliefs; competing viewpoints dilute any one source. That's the sceptical case (Kapoor/Narayanan-style), presented fairly.
- **Figure 2.4 text:** the x-axis "Model scale (training compute in FLOP, logarithmic scale)" runs 1e+01 to 1e+05. Those can't be absolute FLOP, so it's presumably relative or normalised. Models: the Qwen1.5 family, Llama 3.1, GPT-3.5/4o/4.5, Grok-3-Beta. The y-axis tops out around 12 pp. "Persuasiveness increases with model scale" is the figure's title. (Hackenburg's diminishing-returns finding is visible as flattening at the top, I'd guess; I can't see it.)
