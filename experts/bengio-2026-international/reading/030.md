# 030 · L484–494 · p.13 · ES 3: "Layering multiple approaches offers more robust risk management"

**Predicted:** defence in depth, the frameworks, open weights, resilience, gaps. **Got:** the five 3.x sections in miniature, one bolded claim each. Mostly as predicted. What's new is the vocabulary.

- **"'evaluation gap'"**, defined: "performance on pre-deployment tests does not reliably predict real-world **utility or risk**". Note "utility *or* risk": the gap runs both ways (tests can over- or under-predict). A third framing of the evaluation problem: 023 "testing harder", 028 "loopholes … under LoC", now a named *gap*. Lexicon: `iasr:evaluation-gap`.
- **The challenges are split technical/institutional:**
  - Technical: "capabilities sometimes **emerge unpredictably**", "inner workings … poorly understood", the evaluation gap.
  - Institutional: "developers have **incentives to keep important information proprietary**"; "pace … can create pressure to prioritise speed over risk management"; it "makes it harder for institutions to build governance capacity".
  - The report names developer incentives as a structural obstacle. That's critical of industry without blaming anyone: a structural framing.
- **Practices, a list of three:** "**threat modelling** to identify vulnerabilities, **capability evaluations** to assess potentially dangerous behaviours, and **incident reporting** to gather more evidence". Each is paired with its function. Incident reporting is framed as *evidence-gathering* (for the evidence dilemma), not as accountability. Interesting: the report sees incident reporting as feeding science.
- **Frameworks again:** 12 companies, Frontier AI Safety Frameworks; "a **small number of regulatory regimes** are beginning to formalise". A near-verbatim repeat of 023. The report repeats its own key sentences across layers (key developments → ES → presumably body). For search, that means the same claim appears 2–3 times with slight variations. **For a fork reordering search results: the body version is the most authoritative and best-sourced; the summary versions are restatements.** I'll confirm that when I reach the body.
- **Safeguards:** "attacks designed to elicit harmful outputs have become more difficult, but users can still sometimes obtain harmful outputs by **rephrasing requests or breaking them into smaller steps**" (decomposition attacks). **"'defence-in-depth'"**, defined: "layering multiple safeguards". So the ES heading's "layering multiple approaches" is the defence-in-depth claim, extended from safeguards to "approaches" in general (technical + institutional + societal).
- **Open-weight models**, three distinct challenges: "**cannot be recalled** once released", "safeguards are **easier to remove**", "actors can use them **outside of monitored environments** – making misuse harder to prevent and trace". The benefits come first: "significant research and commercial benefits, particularly for **lesser-resourced actors**" (the India minister's point). Balanced, in that order: benefit, then challenge.
- **Societal resilience:** "Because risk management measures have limitations, they **will likely fail to prevent some** AI-related incidents." A candid premise. Resilience = measures "to **absorb and recover from** these shocks": strengthening critical infrastructure, tools to detect AI-generated content, institutional capacity "to respond to novel threats".
