# 029 · L476–480 · p.12 · ES: systemic risks

**Predicted:** mixed labour evidence with early-career effects; autonomy as over-reliance, deskilling, companions; maybe power concentration. **Got:** all of that except power concentration, which isn't in the summary of systemic risks. So "concentration of power" (named by the Indian minister, 021) lives elsewhere, maybe in 1.3, in 3.x, or as a box.

**Labour:** "General-purpose AI **will likely** automate a wide range of cognitive tasks, especially in knowledge work." This is one of the few forward claims with "likely". Then the disagreement is reported: "Economists disagree on the magnitude": offset by new jobs versus significantly reduced employment and wages. The evidence: "**no effect on overall employment**, but some signs of declining demand for **early-career workers** in some AI-exposed occupations, such as writing." (Brynjolfsson et al.'s "Canaries in the Coal Mine", Aug 2025, probably, and freelance-writing studies.) "Some signs … some … such as" is triple-hedged. Fine.

**Human autonomy** is a new category; read hard. Defined by effect: "AI use may affect people's **ability to make informed choices and act on them**." So autonomy = informed choice + acting on it (two components: epistemic and practical). The evidence:
- reliance "can weaken critical thinking skills" ("early evidence suggests");
- **"'automation bias'"**, defined: "the tendency to trust AI system outputs without sufficient scrutiny". A term of art, in quotes. A Glossary candidate.
- **"'AI companion'** apps now have **tens of millions** of users, a **small share** of whom show patterns of increased loneliness and reduced social engagement."

Wandering:
- **The autonomy section is much more modest than "gradual disempowerment".** No civilisation-level erosion of human influence, no states and economies ceasing to need people. It's individual-level cognition and wellbeing. If Duvenaud and Douglas contributed, the consensus process softened their thesis to its measurable individual-level shadow, or put it elsewhere (scenarios in 1.3?). That's a guess I'll test.
- "Risks to human autonomy" bundles three different mechanisms: cognitive deskilling (critical thinking), miscalibrated trust (automation bias), and social-emotional effects (companions and loneliness). Their common thread is "erosion of the person's own agency". **A good place for this project's lexicon to watch**: IASR's "autonomy" is *human* autonomy, whereas in most AI-risk documents "autonomy" means *AI system* autonomy (autonomous operation, 028). The same word with opposite referents in the same summary, two paragraphs apart. A real collision: `iasr:human-autonomy` versus "autonomous operation" or "AI autonomy". Flag for the lexicon.
- "A small share of whom": the direction of causality is unstated ("show patterns of"). Lonely people may seek companions. The cautious wording doesn't claim causation. Good.
- Why "systemic"? Labour is systemic by aggregation (economy-wide). Autonomy effects are individual harms that become systemic by scale (tens of millions). So "systemic" here seems to mean *harm through widespread diffusion in society*, not "systemic" in the EU AI Act's sense (risk specific to high-impact capabilities with significant effects on the Union). **Another big lexicon collision: `iasr:systemic-risk` (diffusion- and scale-based harms to societal structures) vs `eu:systemic-risk` (a defined legal class tied to high-impact capabilities, covering CBRN, LoC, etc.).** Under the EU sense, IASR's malicious-use and LoC risks would be "systemic risks". Under IASR's, they aren't. This is likely the single most consequential terminology collision between two G4 pilot sources. I'll look for IASR's own definition of "systemic risks" in 2.3 and the Glossary.

**Next, I predict:** ES 3: "Layering multiple approaches offers more robust risk management": defence in depth; the state of frameworks; open-weight trade-offs; societal resilience; evidence gaps in mitigations.
