# 028 · L470–474 · p.12 · ES: malfunctions; **the loss-of-control definition**

**Predicted:** reliability, then LoC with scheming, sandbagging, evaluation awareness, shutdown experiments, framed as contested. **Got:** shorter, and *not* framed as contested in the summary. Instead there's a flat present-tense claim.

**Reliability:** "fabricating information, producing flawed code, and giving misleading advice". Agents raise the risk "because they act autonomously, making it harder for humans to intervene before failures cause harm". And "Current techniques can reduce failure rates but **not to the level required in many high-stakes settings**". That's a threshold claim (required reliability), unquantified.

**Loss of control, defined:** "'Loss of control' scenarios are scenarios where AI systems operate **outside of anyone's control**, with **no clear path to regaining control**."
- **This is a key lexicon item.** It's defined as a *kind of scenario* (not an event, a property or a risk) with two conjoined conditions: (a) outside *anyone's* control, and (b) no clear path back. Compare the EU Code's formula (CLAUDE.md, priming): "reliably direct, modify, or shut down". The EU's is about the *capacity* of humans to direct, modify or shut down; IASR's is about *the state of affairs* (outside anyone's control) plus *irrecoverability*.
- **"Anyone's"** is a strong quantifier. A system controlled by a criminal group but not by its developer is *not* LoC under IASR. That's a misuse or proliferation problem. The EU formula is relative to (implicitly) the provider or humans generally. A real divergence for the crosswalk: IASR's LoC excludes "controlled by the wrong party".
- **"No clear path to regaining control"**: temporary escapes that are easily contained wouldn't count. So LoC here is a *severe, persistent* condition, which fits putting it among the most uncertain-but-severe risks. Is there a lesser term ("control failures", "partial loss of control")? Watch the body (2.2.2). Joseph's alignment map and the lexicon's "actors and agent parts" will care: control *by whom* is the whole question, and IASR answers "by anyone".
- **"Current systems lack the capabilities to pose such risks"**: a flat, present-tense negative claim about capabilities, in the summary voice, without "experts disagree". That's bolder than I expected. It's an absence claim; what's the search behind it? Presumably evaluations of autonomous replication and so on. The body should give the basis.
- "but they are improving in relevant areas such as autonomous operation" is the trajectory caveat.
- **Evaluation awareness and loophole-finding are filed under LoC**, not under risk management. So the report treats test-gaming as an *LoC-relevant behaviour* (a precursor to undetected dangerous capability). The same fact appeared in "Key developments" (023) framed as "pre-deployment testing has become harder". Two framings of one fact in two places.
