# 082 · L952–956 · p.40 · §1.3 "Challenges for policymakers"

**Predicted:** chapter 2. **Got:** "Challenges for policymakers" for §1.3. **This corrects my 081 note**: chapter 1 does have this sub-part, at least in §1.3, after the evidence gaps. (The sub-part's name is "Challenges for policymakers", not "Key challenges".) So the structure is Key information → body → Updates → Evidence gaps → Challenges for policymakers. §1.1 and §1.2 seem to lack the last.

- **The compute-threshold implication, as close as the report comes:** algorithmic efficiency at "**3x per year**" (central, from past data), but rates of "**2–6x**" can't be ruled out. "If algorithmic progress continues at the upper bound … models could achieve equivalent capabilities with **10–100x less compute by 2030**. **Regulators will therefore need to consider frameworks that can adapt or remain robust to rapid changes … particularly in terms of the required resources.**" "Required resources" means compute thresholds, unnamed. **This is the nearest IASR comes to criticising fixed compute thresholds** (SB 53's 10²⁶, the EU's 10²⁵ presumption) without naming them. "Will need to consider" is soft, but it's a normative direction aimed at regulators. For the cross-source map: IASR implies fixed-threshold regimes face erosion from efficiency gains. A fork asked "what does IASR say about compute thresholds?" should cite this passage and 066 (largest runs "likely exceeded" 10²⁶), and note that IASR names no regime.
- A benchmark example: "**90% accuracy** on a programming benchmark … does not imply that it can build functional software applications."
- **Indicators to monitor:** "real-world task evaluations, the rate of algorithmic innovation, and the emergence of qualitatively new capabilities". A monitoring agenda, phrased as "it will be important". The report's recommendations take the form of *what to measure*, which suits a scientific body.
