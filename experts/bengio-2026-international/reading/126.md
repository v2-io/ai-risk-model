# 126 · L1300–1302 · p.63 · §2.1.3 Evidence gaps (**the evaluation gap is explicitly two-sided for cyber**)

- **Both directions are named:** benchmarks "can **overstate** performance" (contamination) and "**understate** real-world risk … by **failing to elicit the model's true capabilities**". The example: "**third parties have reportedly used scaffolding to reveal greater cyber capabilities than those measured in pre-deployment testing**." That's the understatement direction I predicted at 054/060 (deployed capability exceeds evaluated capability via scaffolding), now stated by the source with an example. **This is the passage to cite for "can pre-deployment testing underestimate dangerous capabilities?"**, with 028/023 (evaluation awareness) as the other mechanism.
- **A logically odd sentence:** benchmarks "can understate real-world risk by failing to account for cases where an AI system **fails in a situation that a human could easily handle**". An AI failing where a human wouldn't is a reason the benchmark *overstates* capability, not understates risk. Unless the intended meaning is that such failures cause *accidents* (reliability risk), which benchmarks of offensive capability don't measure. That reading works: risk includes harms from *failures*, not just capabilities. Possible, but it's compressed to the point of seeming backwards. Flag for forks: read it as "benchmarks miss failure-induced harms", hedged.
- **The attribution limit, stated plainly:** "Evidence of adoption of AI by attackers is drawn primarily from **incident reporting and threat-intelligence** (Table 2.3), but these sources **rarely allow for confident attribution**." The report itself acknowledges the evidence-base limitation I noted at 120 (though it doesn't mention the vendors' interests).
