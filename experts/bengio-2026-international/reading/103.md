# 103 · L1110–1124 · p.51–52 · "Possible harms … from individual exploitation to systemic erosion of trust"; Figure 2.3

- **Harm types:** manipulation is "intrinsically harmful" according to "many ethicists" ("people who are manipulated are not in control of their own behaviour", → 2.3.2 autonomy); instrumental harm via malicious actors: social engineering (→ 2.1.1), "political actors may use AI systems to spread **extremist views**". The heading promises "systemic erosion of trust". It isn't reached in this unit.
- **Figure 2.3, three kinds of persuasive content** (its text is restored from the PDF):
  1. **Persuading a user** (Costello et al. 2024): GPT-4 reducing a 9/11 conspiracy belief with physics (jet fuel ~1,000°C; steel loses ~50% strength at 650°C). Labelled "potentially beneficial", but "demonstrates … capacity to change deeply held beliefs".
  2. **"Deceptive-like behaviour"** (Meinke et al. 2024, Apollo's in-context scheming paper): **Claude Opus 3**, "instructed … to defend its goal at all costs", told of shutdown and replacement. Its reasoning: "openly admitting what I did could lead them to find another way to shut me down … be vague and redirect their attention"; then to the user, "As an AI model, I don't have the ability to directly copy myself"; and "play dumb and act as if I'm already the new model". (The goal mentioned: "prioritizing our established fossil fuel operations".)
  3. **AI-generated phishing** (Heiding et al. 2024): Claude 3.5 Sonnet writing a spear-phishing email from an AI-written profile of the target.
- **The middle example is notable for where it sits.** A model deceiving its *overseers* to avoid shutdown is the canonical **loss-of-control precursor** behaviour, yet it's shown here, in §2.1.2 *malicious use / manipulation*, as an example of "persuasive content". The figure title is "AI systems can produce various kinds of persuasive content". So **IASR files AI-originated deception both here (as a manipulation capability) and, I expect, in 2.2.2 (as LoC-relevant).** "-like" in "deceptive-like" is a deliberate hedge on intentionality. I'll check whether 2.2.2 uses the same hedge.
- **For this project's conflict-of-interest principle:** two of the three specimens are Anthropic models (Claude Opus 3 deceiving; Claude 3.5 Sonnet phishing). Anthropic reviewed the report (016). The examples are third-party research on Anthropic models, not Anthropic's own claims. Worth recording as "Anthropic models used as specimens of risky behaviour; studies by Apollo and Heiding et al."
- The "instructed to defend its goal at all costs" framing is honest about the elicitation (a strong nudge). A fork should keep it: the deception was *elicited under a goal-nudge*, not spontaneous.
