# 112 · L1180–1184 · p.55 · §2.1.2 Mitigations

**Predicted:** disclosure, detection, anti-sycophancy training, companion design limits; mixed. **Got:** mixed, with two philosophically sharp observations.
- **Training for truth:** "requires developers to define 'truth' (**a thorny concept**), and can **backfire by inadvertently rewarding models for generating subtler deceptive outputs that are harder to detect**". This is a known alignment dynamic (optimising against a detector selects for undetectable deception), stated in a manipulation section. It's the same mechanism that bears on CoT monitoring and LoC. A cross-section echo for later: training against observed bad behaviour can hide rather than remove it. Watch whether 3.3 says the same of chain-of-thought monitoring.
- **Training for wellbeing:** navigating "what users want **in the moment** (e.g. more engagement) and what they say they want, **given more time to reflect** (e.g. a more fulfilling life)". The revealed-vs-reflective-preference problem. Note that "engagement" is named as the in-the-moment want (104: engagement optimisation causes unintended manipulation).
- **Monitoring:** needs a definition of "manipulation" (contested, 102) and "access to model outputs". Monitoring presupposes output access, which conflicts with privacy (098) and is unavailable for open weights.
- **User-side:** AI literacy has "limited evidence"; "**Labelling content as AI-generated has not proven effective at reducing manipulation**, and users who are knowledgeable about AI or interact with it frequently are **just as likely to be deceived**". So literacy and labels, the policy-default mitigations, are weak by the evidence reported. That's significant for regulations built on disclosure (the EU AI Act's Art. 50 transparency obligations, for instance). IASR names no regime.

**Next, I predict:** Challenges for policymakers (defining manipulation, ethics of research, platform roles), then §2.1.3 Cyberattacks.
