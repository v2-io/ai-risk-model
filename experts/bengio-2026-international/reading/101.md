# 101 · L1094–1102 · p.50 · §2.1.2 Key information; **the definition of manipulation**

**Predicted:** persuasion at human level, personalisation, elections, state influence operations, companions. **Got:** persuasion and personalisation, sycophancy and impersonation, and a definition. No elections or state operations in the key points (yet).

**"manipulate people: to change their beliefs or behaviours without their full awareness or consent."** That's the definition, in the body's first paragraph. Two conjoined elements: (a) change of belief *or* behaviour, (b) without *full* awareness or consent. "Full" is generous to the claim: partial awareness still counts as manipulation. Lexicon: `iasr:manipulation`. It contrasts with "influence" ("Much of this influence is benign or even beneficial"), so **influence is the genus and manipulation the harmful species**, marked by lack of awareness or consent. Note that the criterion is about the *target's* epistemic state, not the actor's intent. That's important for the next point.

**A malicious-use section that includes unintended harms:** "Some malicious actors intentionally use AI-generated content to manipulate people, while **other harms, such as dependence on AI, occur unintentionally**." By 088's definitions, unintended harms aren't *misuse* ("actors deliberately use AI systems to cause harm"). So 2.1.2 cuts across categories, as 088's "not … mutually exclusive" allows. **This section is half malicious use, half malfunction or systemic.** "**Sycophancy**" is named as having "manipulative effects", and sycophancy is a *trained* system behaviour, not a user's intent. It's a malfunction in IASR's terms, housed under malicious use. For the crosswalk: IASR's "manipulation" concept is intent-neutral (target-side), while its *section* placement suggests intent. A should-ask: *"Is AI manipulation in IASR always deliberate?"* No: the definition is target-side, and the section includes unintentional manipulation (sycophancy, dependence).

**The comparator shifts again:** ES (027) "as effective as human-written content"; here "**at least as effective as non-expert human participants**". "Non-expert" is a new, important qualifier. The claim is parity with lay persuaders, not with professionals.

**Drivers of future persuasiveness:** "improving capabilities, **increased user dependence**, or **training on user feedback**". The last is the RLHF-sycophancy mechanism: optimising for approval may optimise for persuasion. **Limiting factors:** "theoretical work and simulations suggests factors such as **distribution costs** and the **inherent difficulty of persuasion** will limit the impacts" (the sceptical literature; e.g. persuasion is hard and the bottleneck is distribution, not content generation). Both sides given.

**Update:** "people who interact with AI systems for longer and in more personal ways are more likely to find their content persuasive". Relationship depth increases influence, the bridge to companions in 2.3.2.

**Mitigations: "mixed evidence regarding the effectiveness of all proposed"**, and a trade-off: anti-manipulation measures "could also curtail the usefulness of AI systems (e.g. as educational tools)". Education is persuasion in a benign frame.
