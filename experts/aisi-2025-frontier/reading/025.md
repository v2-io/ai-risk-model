**Predicted:** agents' limitations, or Section 3. Instead, footnotes and the cyber time-horizon figure.

**Got (text):** two footnotes: "³ How does time horizon vary across domains, METR, 2025" and "⁴ Measuring AI Ability to Complete Long Tasks, METR, 2025". The `<sup>` tags are left over from the conversion.

**Got (Figure 3, read off the image):** title "The length of cyber tasks that AI models can complete is increasing over time". Source: UK AISI. Y axis: "Average task duration for human experts", 0 to 1 hr 45 min. X axis: model release date, 2022–2026. Red dots are reasoning models, cyan non-reasoning. A dotted exponential fit.
- About 3 min (late-2022 release); about 13 min (late 2023); about 12 and 11 min (2024, *flat*); about 26 min (late 2024, non-reasoning); about 22 min (early 2025, reasoning); about 32, 31, 38 min (2025); **about 1 hr 20 min** (around 2025.6, reasoning), with an error bar from about 1 hr to 1 hr 37 min.
- Caption: "The length of tasks from AISI's cyber evaluations that models can complete with a **50% chance of success** over time. Task length is measured by the **estimated time** it would take a human expert to complete; here, this is based on **expert estimates** of AISI's cyber suite. **Methodology from the non-profit Model Evaluation and Threat Research (METR).**⁴"

**What differed.**
1. **This isn't a step plot.** Every model is shown, with an exponential fit, not a best-so-far envelope. The text in unit 20 ("Figures in this report include step lines…") describes most figures, not all. Here the trend comes from a regression, which is the better design for a doubling-time claim.
2. **The 50% time horizon is METR's method**, credited explicitly ("Methodology from… METR"). So AISI's cyber doubling time *is* a METR-method measurement on AISI's tasks, with durations from expert estimates instead of timed humans (cf. unit 24). This settles my unit-23 lineage question: method from METR, data from AISI. Partly independent: the task data is AISI's; the method is shared.
3. **"Over an hour by mid-2025" rests on one model.** The last point (about 1 hr 20 min) is the only one above an hour; the next best is about 38 min. The text's "from less than 10 minutes in early 2023 to over an hour by mid-2025" is true of the data, but a single model carries the "over an hour". And the last point sits *above* the fitted curve, so the fit itself isn't driven only by it.
4. **2024 was flat** (about 13 → 12 → 11 min) before the late-2024 jump. A smooth exponential fit hides a plateau then a jump. Plausibly the jump is the arrival of reasoning models and better scaffolds; the colours suggest it.

**Wandering.**
- **"An estimated upper bound" (on the doubling time), now with the figure in view.** I'm still not sure why it's an upper bound. Possibly because the suite tops out (tasks of only so many hours exist), so the longest horizons are censored and the fit is pulled down; a faster doubling might be truer. Or because elicitation is weak. The text doesn't say here. A question an expert should be asked: "Why is eight months an upper bound and not a central estimate?" My honest current answer: the report asserts it without showing the reasoning in this section.
- A doubling time fitted over 11 points with a plateau in the middle, from expert-estimated durations, is fragile. Fitting from late 2023 only, or from 2024 only, would give quite different rates. "Roughly eight months" is the summary of a noisy curve. The figure is honest with its error bars; the summary's "doubling every eight months" will travel without them.
- METR's own doubling time for software tasks was about 7 months (2019–2025), and faster (about 4 months) in 2024–25, as I recall. AISI's "similar" claim (unit 24) holds for the long-run figure. Interesting that AISI chose to say "similar", not "the same as METR's", which keeps distance.
- The y-axis label says "Average task duration for human experts", which on its own reads like the average length of tasks, not a horizon. The caption fixes it: the 50%-success horizon. A reader who skips captions would misread the axis. One more reason the caption, not the axis, should anchor any citation.
- Footnote 3, "How does time horizon vary across domains", is the METR piece behind "generalises across many domains". METR is cited twice in two consecutive footnotes; it's AISI's main external pillar so far.
