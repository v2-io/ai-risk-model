# Unit 28 — L230–234, p.14 ("12") — Section 3 opens: chemistry & biology framing

**Predicted:** cyber first, opening with methods and the apprentice figure. Wrong: chemistry & biology first. (The milestone page's order was the better predictor.) The barrier-lowering frame I predicted is here.

**Got:**
- "two domains critical to **security and innovation**: chemistry & biology, and cyber."
- "rapid progress in chemistry & biology **relative to human expert baselines**: models are becoming increasingly useful for **assisting scientific research and development (R&D)**."
- "Their ability to **ideate, design experiments, and synthesise complex, interdisciplinary insights** has the potential to **accelerate beneficial scientific research**."
- "But **without robust safeguards** (Section 4), these dual-use capabilities are **available to everyone, including those with harmful intentions**."
- "This means that **some of the barriers limiting risky research to trained specialists are eroding**."

**What differed.**
- **Benefit comes first here.** I criticised unit 27 for framing benefit as merely economic; one page later, AISI leads chem-bio with *beneficial scientific research*. "Security **and innovation**" sets the frame for both domains. Fair to note my criticism was premature.
- **The misuse argument, in three steps:**
  1. capability is dual-use;
  2. without robust safeguards it is "available to everyone";
  3. so barriers that confined risky research to "trained specialists" erode.

  The control, safeguards, sits in the middle of the risk argument. That's the bow-tie this project wants: capability (cause) → safeguards (prevention) → barrier erosion (event) → harm (not yet named).
- **The barrier is specialist training.** The threat model is *de-skilling*: non-specialists doing what only specialists could. That's the "novice uplift" model, and the troubleshooting results (unit 5) are its evidence: tacit knowledge was the specialist's moat.

**Wandering.**
- "Available to everyone **without robust safeguards**." That's true for closed models where safeguards fail, and for open-weight models with none. The section's logic leads straight to the open-source question (Section 7). Will Section 3 mention that open models' chem-bio capability trails by only months? That's where the barrier argument bites hardest.
- "Some of the barriers… are eroding": not "the barriers", and not "have eroded". Calibrated. Which barriers remain? Material access (pathogen samples, precursors, equipment), wet-lab skill, and the scale and time needed. LLMs touch knowledge and planning; physical barriers remain. An honest expert answer: AISI says *some* barriers erode; it doesn't say all, and its evidence is about knowledge, protocols and troubleshooting, not physical access.
- "Risky research": not "weapons". AISI is careful: "risky research" covers dual-use research of concern (gain-of-function, etc.), not only weapons programmes. The vocabulary is broad, and the omission of "weapons" is probably deliberate, for a public document. Still, the report so far never names a harm outcome: no "mass casualties", no "pandemic". The harm endpoint stays implicit. For the project's causal chain, AISI's chem-bio claims stop at the event ("barriers eroding"); the impact side is left to the reader.
- "Ideate, design experiments, synthesise interdisciplinary insights": these sound like AI-for-science (FutureHouse-style agents). AISI acknowledges AI as a research collaborator, with the corollary that the collaborator doesn't check credentials.
