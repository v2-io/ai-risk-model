# Unit 5 — L64, p.3 ("1") — Executive summary: chemistry and biology

**Predicted:** chem-bio next, with PhD-level experts. Yes to both.

**Got, and what differed:**
- "AI models have **far surpassed** PhD-level experts on **some** domain-specific expertise." Strong verb, narrow object.
- They "first reached **our** expert baseline for **open-ended questions** in 2024 and now exceed it by **up to 60%**" (FIGURE 5). "Our" expert baseline: AISI built its own human baseline. "Up to 60%": the ceiling of a range, and probably relative (score / baseline − 1), though it could be percentage points. Ambiguous.
- Protocols: "the first models able to generate protocols for scientific experiments that were judged to be accurate in **late 2024**" (FIGURE 7). Then "These have since been **proven feasible to implement in a wet lab**." I didn't predict this. It's an actual wet-lab check of model-generated protocols, a step from an eval score toward the real world. And the claim is *feasibility*, not *uplift*: the protocols worked, which is not the same as novices succeeding with them.
- Troubleshooting: "up to 90% better than human experts at providing troubleshooting support for wet lab experiments" (FIGURE 8). My priming said "beating PhD-level experts at troubleshooting", and it's there, but stronger, with a number.

**Wandering.**
- "Up to N% better" appears twice, and the phrase is slippery. "Up to" picks the best model or the best subset. "N% better" is a ratio of scores whose scale is unknown. If experts scored 40% and models 76%, "90% better" is 36 percentage points. An expert fork asked "does AISI say models are 90% better than experts?" must answer with that caveat. **These two numbers are the most likely to be misquoted downstream**, and I'll want to see Figures 5 and 8 to say what they are.
- "Real-time lab support" is a framing. The threat model behind it is presumably a novice or semi-skilled actor at the bench, with the model coaching through failures. Troubleshooting is the classic bottleneck of tacit knowledge in bio weapons development (the argument that knowledge isn't the constraint, hands-on skill is). AISI is saying models now address the tacit-knowledge bottleneck in text. That's the heart of the uplift debate. But note what it doesn't claim here: that a novice *with* the model succeeds in a wet lab. Is there a human-uplift study? I'd expect AISI to mention one, or to say none was done.
- The section mixes "chemistry and biology" but the examples sound biological (wet lab, protocols). Is chemistry measured separately? Watch for that.
- "Surpassed PhD-level experts on some domain-specific expertise" vs the earlier "expert baselines are being surpassed rapidly". The "rapidly" claim rests on chem-bio and on cyber expert-level tasks. So far "expert" carries three meanings: a cyber practitioner with 10+ years, a PhD-level domain expert, and a human expert at troubleshooting. Different populations, all called "experts". For the lexicon: AISI's "expert baseline" is a comparison population AISI constructed for each eval, not a standard level.
- Not said so far: anything about *harm*. Every sentence is about capability. The report seems to be a capability-trends report that sits on a national-security frame, leaving the step from capability to risk implicit. I'm curious whether it ever names threat actors or scenarios, or keeps to "capabilities relevant to X".
- The protocols were "judged to be accurate": judged by whom? Experts, presumably. The wet-lab feasibility test then turns a judgment into an observation. That's a nice evidential ladder (judged accurate, then shown feasible), and it's the kind of evidence-tier distinction this project records.

**Next:** I expect safeguards: universal jailbreaks in every system tested, but more effort needed to find them. Possibly one more capability sentence first (self-replication, or autonomy).
