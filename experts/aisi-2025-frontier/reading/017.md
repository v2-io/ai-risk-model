# Unit 17 — L153–162, p.7 ("5") — Our testing approach (2): the rest of the domains

**Predicted:** autonomy / loss of control, safeguards, societal, then methods. Yes, and the methods list is about to begin. The surprise is that autonomy and loss of control are *two* domains.

**Got:**
- **Autonomy skills**: "the extent to which AI systems can **autonomously conduct simplified versions** of self-replication tasks and AI research and development."
- **Loss of control**: "We **track emerging capabilities** such as self-replication that **could contribute towards** AI systems' ability to **evade human control**."
- **Safeguards**: "We test **how difficult it is to evade the safeguards** employed by AI companies **to prevent misuse**."
- **Societal impacts**: "We research **emerging societal risks** from AI systems, such as their ability to **influence humans** or **integrate into critical sectors**."
- "We use several evaluation methodologies… **Not all are applied across all domains.** These include:"

**What differed.**
- **Autonomy skills and Loss of control are listed as separate domains, with self-replication in both.** Autonomy is a capability domain ("can autonomously conduct simplified versions…"); loss of control is a risk lens ("track emerging capabilities… that could contribute towards… evade human control"). So the relation is: autonomy capabilities are *evidence for* the loss-of-control risk. That's the same pattern as precursors (unit 12). It's a clear capability→risk-contribution structure, and the lexicon could represent it directly: `aisi:autonomy-skills` measures; `aisi:loss-of-control` is the risk those measurements bear on, via the mechanism "evade human control".
- **Two evasions, and the parallel is exact.** Safeguards: *users* "evade the safeguards… to prevent misuse". Loss of control: *models* "evade human control". AISI's security lens has a single verb, **evade**, for both threat directions: human adversary against developer controls, and model against human control. A unifying frame I didn't expect, and probably not deliberate, but real in the text.
- **"Misuse"** appears for the first time, as the thing safeguards prevent. So far the report's risk taxonomy is implicitly misuse (cyber, chem-bio, safeguards), loss of control, and societal impacts. That's close to the standard three-way split (misuse, misalignment/loss of control, structural/systemic), the IASR's split too: malicious use, malfunctions, systemic risks. I'll watch whether AISI says it's using that split.
- **Societal impacts include "integrate into critical sectors."** Integration into critical sectors as a *societal* risk, not a security one, was a surprise. Probably the "asset transfers" agent finding (unit 8) belongs here: agents in finance. So "societal" in AISI's usage covers both mass-psychological effects (influence, emotional reliance) and structural dependence (critical-sector integration).

**Wandering.**
- "Simplified versions" again, this time in the *domain definition* itself. AISI is consistent in owning its simplification, which I respect. Whether downstream citers keep it is another matter.
- "Could contribute towards": a weak causal claim, apt for precursors. A fork asked "does AISI say self-replication leads to loss of control?" should answer: no, that it "could contribute towards" the ability to evade control; a contributory capability, not a cause.
- The ability to "influence humans" covers both persuasion (political) and emotional effects. "Influence" is broad, and I'd expect sub-domains. It's also where the line between benign and harmful is thinnest: all communication influences. How does AISI decide what's a risk here?
- Safeguards is the only domain defined by *adversarial difficulty* ("how difficult it is to evade"). Others are defined by capability or risk. Safeguard strength, measured as attacker cost, matches the unit-6 metric (expert effort, 40x).
- "Not all [methodologies] are applied across all domains": an honest scope note. A cross-domain comparison ("cyber improves faster than bio") would compare different methods. Probably not something AISI claims, but readers might.

**Next:** the methods list: maybe (1) automated task-based evaluations / benchmarks, (2) expert red-teaming, (3) human-uplift studies or RCTs, (4) surveys and observational data, (5) agentic evals in sandboxes. I'll bet "red-teaming" and "human uplift studies" both appear.
