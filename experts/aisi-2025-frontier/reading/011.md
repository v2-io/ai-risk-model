**What differed, and what I learned.**
1. **The x-axis is model release date**, confirming my unit-7 guess, and every curve is a step function of the best model so far. "Trends" in this report are frontier-envelope trends over release dates.
2. **Chemistry is in the chem-bio claims after all**: Protocol C is chemistry. My worry in units 5 and 10 is partly answered. Chemistry crosses later and lower than bacteriology.
3. **The protocol metric isn't a percentage.** "Feasibility score relative to expert baseline" is a difference on some scale (−4 to +6). So "up to 60%" in unit 5 must belong to a *different* figure (FIGURE 5, open-ended questions), not this one. The "outperform PhD-level experts on protocol generation" in unit 10 *is* backed by an expert comparison: the baseline here is a human expert score. That answers unit 10's question.
4. **Jaggedness in the cyber data, visible in the figure but not in the text.** Around 2025.3, the *expert* line (about 18%) sits *above* the *practitioner* line (about 5%). The difficulty levels aren't monotone in model success. A model could do some 10-year-expert tasks and fail nearly all practitioner tasks. This is the unevenness I wondered about in unit 4, and the summary sentence "started completing expert-level tasks" hides it. It might mean the expert set is small (a few tasks, so lumpy), or that "experience level" doesn't track AI difficulty.

**Wandering.**
- Expert at 25% while practitioner is at 34%: by mid-2025 they're closer than you'd expect if difficulty were a single ladder. If I were an AISI analyst I'd want to know whether the expert tasks completed are *one or two specific tasks* (small n). "We tested the first model that could successfully complete expert-level tasks" (unit 4) would fit a model that cracked one or two. A fork should be careful with "25% of expert-level tasks"; the task count isn't visible here.
- The step-line design embeds an argument: capability never goes backward. That's true of a frontier envelope by construction, so the *shape* of the trend is partly the plotting rule. Off-envelope dots show many models below. A reader must not read "AI capability is monotone" off a running-maximum plot.
- Error bars exist (small vertical bars on points), so there's sampling uncertainty per model. The envelope ignores it: taking the running max of noisy estimates biases the envelope upward (winner's curse). A subtle methodological point; I doubt the report discusses it. It's a question an expert should be asked.
- Feasibility "relative to expert baseline" at +5.4 for bacteriology: on what scale is 5.4 large? If the scale runs to 10, it's dramatic. I need FIGURE 7's detail.
- What the bio panel does *not* show: who benefits. Expert-level protocol generation helps legitimate labs as much as bad actors. The report's frame is risk, but the same curve is a capability for science. Does AISI acknowledge dual use? A good question to keep.
- "Technical non-expert" as a cyber level: someone with technical skill but no security training. At about 99%, the lowest rung is saturated. A saturated eval tells you nothing more; AISI needs harder tiers, which is presumably why "expert" exists.
