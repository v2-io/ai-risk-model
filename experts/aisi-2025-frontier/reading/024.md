**What differed.**
1. **"Well-scoped"** is in the figure title and caption, and in none of the three textual restatements (units 4, 12, 23). It's an important limiter: well-scoped tasks have clear specifications and checkable outcomes. Real-world autonomy is mostly badly scoped. A fork quoting ">40% on hour-long software tasks" should add "well-scoped", as AISI's own figure does.
2. **Task length is an *expert estimate***, not measured human completion time. "How long it might take a human expert" (unit 23) said "might"; the caption confirms it. METR, by contrast, timed human baseliners for many of its tasks (as I recall it). A methodological difference between AISI's and METR's time horizons, which means the "similar" doubling times aren't measured on the same footing.
3. **"Frequently"** in the figure title, for about 41% success. A generous word for less than half the time. The text said "over 40% of the time", which is precise. The figure's title has the infographic's lift (as unit 10 noticed).
4. **The cyber doubling is "an estimated upper bound"** on the doubling *time*. An upper bound on doubling time means the growth is *at least* that fast. That's the direction consistent with "underestimate the ceiling". But how is it an upper bound? Perhaps because the envelope's top bins are censored (no harder tasks to fail at). I'll look for the method in Section 3's FIGURE 3.

It's a cross-source dependency: the claim "time horizons extend across many domains" is METR's, relayed. For the corroboration ledger: AISI's cyber doubling and METR's software doubling are related in method (time horizon), and AISI cites METR for the generalisation. Not independent at the conceptual level, though AISI's data is its own.

**Wandering.**
- The bins show the shape of progress: a wave moving up the difficulty ladder. Short tasks saturate first (98%), medium next (75%), long ones rise last (41%).
