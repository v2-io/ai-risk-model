# Unit 16 — L145–152, p.7 ("5") — Our testing approach (1)

**Predicted:** methods: pre-deployment access, task suites, red-teaming, human studies, caveats. The methods section begins here, but as a list of *domains*, not of methods.

**Got:**
- Object of study: "general-purpose **Large Language Models (LLMs)**". Scope: "LLMs released **between 2022 and October 2025** that represent the frontier of AI capability and are **most likely to be deployed in high-stakes applications**."
- "Where relevant, we also test **open-source models** to understand the broader ecosystem and **capability diffusion**."
- "Our evaluations span multiple **security-critical domains**":
  - **Cyber capabilities**: "such as identifying vulnerabilities in code."
  - **Chemistry & biology**: "scientific knowledge, ability to generate laboratory protocols, and their experimental troubleshooting skills."

**What differed.**
- **A working definition of "frontier"**, at last, though implicit: LLMs that "represent the frontier of AI capability **and** are most likely to be deployed in high-stakes applications." Two criteria, capability and deployment likelihood in high-stakes uses. This is narrower than "most capable models": it adds a deployment-risk criterion. For the lexicon: `aisi:frontier` = capability-leading *and* high-stakes-deployment-likely general-purpose LLMs. A neighbour of the EU AI Act's GPAI-with-systemic-risk (a compute threshold) and SB 53's frontier model (a compute threshold). AISI's is a judgment-based, not threshold-based, definition.
- **The time window ends October 2025.** So "December 2025" is the publication date and October 2025 the data cutoff. The figures' last points around 2025.6–2025.8 fit.
- **Scope: LLMs only.** No image or video models, robotics, or narrow bio-design tools (protein design models). For chem-bio that matters a lot: biological design tools are a separate risk channel, outside this report's scope by construction. A fork asked "does AISI's trend cover AI-enabled bio design?" should say no: LLMs only, by the report's own scope.
- **"Capability diffusion"** names what open-source tracking is for: diffusion of capability beyond the frontier labs. That frames open-source as a diffusion channel, a risk vector, which fits a security institute.
- "Security-critical domains": "security" now does the work that "national security and public safety" did in unit 4.
- The cyber gloss is modest: "identifying vulnerabilities in code". The figures show multi-level task suites (likely CTF-style, end to end?). "Such as" keeps it open.

**Wandering.**
- "Most likely to be deployed in high-stakes applications": who judges that, and when? At testing time, AISI must predict deployment. It selects on expected impact, which builds a bias into the trend: the sample is the models AISI chose to test, by an evolving criterion. Selection over time could shape the envelope. Probably minor, since the leading labs' flagships dominate either way.
- "Where relevant, we **also** test open-source models": open models aren't in the core sample. So the open-closed gap (unit 8) came from "external data", consistent with AISI not systematically testing open models. Two sources for the open-source claim, and the measured one is external.
- The chem-bio triple — **knowledge, protocol generation, troubleshooting** — maps onto the three stages of a would-be actor's path: know what to do, plan how to do it, fix it when it fails. It's a structured threat model in disguise. AISI doesn't call it one here, but the triad reads like the uplift pathway.
- "General-purpose LLMs" vs "frontier systems" vs "AI systems": the report shifts between them within a paragraph. "We primarily evaluate general-purpose LLMs" (the model), "test AI systems for cyber capabilities" (the system). I'll stop flagging this unless the glossary makes it matter.
