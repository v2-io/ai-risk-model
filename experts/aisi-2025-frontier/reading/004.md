# Unit 4 — L56–62, p.3 (printed "1") — Executive summary, opening

**Predicted:** a framing paragraph (AISI tested N models over two years), then key findings as bullets.

**Got:** the framing paragraph, much as predicted, but with no N. The first finding is the headline numbers.
- Since **November 2023** (the Bletchley summit month, when the Taskforce became AISI), AISI has evaluated frontier AI systems "across domains critical to national security and public safety". So the domain scope is framed as national security *and* public safety, not security alone.
- It calls itself "our first public analysis of the trends". Its stated aims: "accessible, data-driven insights" and "promote a shared understanding among governments, industry, and the public". The audience is three-way, and "shared understanding" is a coordination aim, not a warning aim.
- The first sentence of the findings: "AI capabilities are improving rapidly across all tested domains." Then "Performance in **some** areas is doubling every eight months". Note the scope shift from *all* (improving) to *some* (doubling every eight months).
- Cyber: apprentice-level tasks 50% "on average", against "just over 10%" in "early 2024" (FIGURE 10). The first model to complete **expert-level** tasks ("typically requiring over 10 years of experience for a human practitioner") was tested **in 2025**. The length of cyber tasks models can do unassisted, measured in human-expert time, **doubles roughly every eight months** (FIGURE 3).
- Autonomy: "software tasks that would take a human expert over an hour" (FIGURE 2).

**The priming alarm, honestly.** My before-file guessed "about 10% to about 50%", and the text says just that. My memory was right here, which is the dangerous case: now every other half-memory feels more trustworthy, and it shouldn't. What memory *didn't* carry is the exact framing, which is what matters:
- "on average": average over what? Tasks, models, or attempts? 50% of what denominator?
- "the first model that could successfully complete expert-level tasks": does it complete *some* expert-level tasks, or a rate? "Successfully complete expert-level tasks" sounds like a crossing, not a rate. I want to see the figure.
- The 8-month doubling is the *length of cyber tasks*, a METR-style time horizon applied to cyber. It is not "performance" in general. The summary's "performance in some areas is doubling every eight months" generalises a cyber task-length metric into "performance". That is a small slippage of the kind a summary invites, and the kind the project should flag when this source gets quoted: "AISI says AI performance doubles every 8 months" would be a lossy re-quote.

**Wandering.**
- Figure numbering: "See FIGURES 1.1-1.3", then FIGURE 10, FIGURE 3, FIGURE 2. So the executive summary has its own Figures 1.1–1.3, but it also points forward into the body's numbering (2, 3, 10), *before* those figures exist for the reader. A forward reference in the experiential-reading sense, harmless in an executive summary, which is written to stand alone. I'll keep track of whether the body's Figure 3 says what the summary says it does.
- "Expert baselines are being surpassed rapidly." Which experts, and which baselines? Chem-bio PhDs on protocol troubleshooting, I'd guess, though that's my priming. Hold it.
- The domains: cyber first, then "autonomy skills" via software tasks. "Autonomy" here means doing long software tasks unassisted, a capability notion, not loss of control. The project's lexicon will care: AISI's "autonomy" (a capability: unassisted long-horizon task completion) is not the EU Code's "loss of control" (a risk outcome). I wonder whether AISI uses "loss of control" at all, and whether it ties autonomy to it.
- Human-expert-time as the unit of capability is a choice with consequences. It anchors capability to human labour (time-to-complete for an expert), which makes the result legible to economists and policymakers, and it hides that an AI's failure modes are distributed differently from a human's. A model that does a "10-year-expert" task might fail an apprentice task. The "on average" might be hiding exactly that unevenness ("jaggedness"?). I'd expect the word "jagged" somewhere.
- Who decides what counts as "apprentice-level" or "expert-level"? Probably AISI's own task taxonomy, calibrated by human practitioners. This is the sort of methodological definition the project should extract: the source's own level scale.
- "Data-driven" plus "accessible" is a promise that the evidence will be shown as figures, with the methods perhaps in an annex. Watch whether the methods are enough to judge the claims.
