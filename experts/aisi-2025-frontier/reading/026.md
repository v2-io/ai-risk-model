# Unit 26 — L216–220, p.13 ("11") — Post-deployment scaffolding

**Predicted:** one or two more agent findings (real-world use, reliability). One more finding, the one I'd hoped for in unit 21: scaffolding as a lever outside the weights.

**Got:**
- Heading: "**Scaffolding techniques applied after deployment** can further improve agentic capabilities."
- "agents with the **best externally developed scaffolds reliably outperform the best base models (minimally scaffolded)** at software engineering tasks."
- FIGURE 4: results from **SWE-bench**, "an open-source software engineering benchmark". So this is external benchmark data, not AISI's own suite.
- "largest in **late 2024**, when scaffolding provided **an almost 40% increase in average success rate** over the base state-of-the-art."
- "our most recent testing shows **signs of convergence**", but "it's difficult to determine whether this is due to some inherent trend in the effectiveness of scaffolds over time, or other factors like **benchmark saturation** and **lag time of scaffold development**. It is **possible** that scaffolding remains a key factor in pushing the frontier forward."

**What differed.**
- I wondered in unit 21 whether scaffolds mean measured capability is a lower bound. Here AISI makes the point with data, and frames it by *time*: "applied **after deployment**". So pre-deployment testing can't see capabilities that third parties unlock later. That's a structural limit on pre-deployment evaluation, stated by the institute that does it.
- "Almost 40% increase": again, relative or absolute? On SWE-bench in late 2024, base models were around 30–50%, and scaffolded systems around 50–60%, if I recall. That suggests a relative increase (e.g. 35% → 49%), but I can't tell from text. The figure may settle it. The third "N% increase" ambiguity in the report (cf. units 5 and 6).
- The convergence paragraph is a model of epistemic honesty: three candidate explanations, no choice among them, and a "possible" for the forward-looking claim. AISI doesn't over-read its own data here.

**Wandering.**
- "Externally developed scaffolds" means scaffolds built by others, e.g. open-source agent frameworks or SWE-bench leaderboard entries. The capability on the leaderboard isn't the developer's product; it's the ecosystem's. Who is responsible for a capability that emerges from a third-party scaffold on a developer's model? A governance question this report touches without asking: accountability for post-deployment capability gains. SB 53 and the EU Code place obligations on developers or providers; neither, as far as I know, covers third-party scaffolders. A good question for the experts to discuss together.
- "Benchmark saturation" as a confound appears again (cf. units 11, 12): AISI is aware that its own and others' evals run out of headroom. The convergence might be a ceiling effect: both scaffolded and base approach the top. Then the convergence says nothing about scaffolds' value.
- "Lag time of scaffold development": the newest base models haven't had scaffolds built for them yet. So the convergence at the latest point may be an artifact of timing, and the gap may reopen. This is a reason to discount the most recent data point in any time series that depends on ecosystem work. It's a general caution for reading the edges of all AISI's curves.
- SWE-bench being "open-source" (the benchmark is public) ties into contamination: public benchmarks can leak into training. AISI doesn't mention contamination here. Its own suites are private partly for that reason (unit 9, unit 20). A fork asked "why does AISI use a public benchmark here?" could say: to compare against externally built scaffolds, which only exist for public benchmarks.
