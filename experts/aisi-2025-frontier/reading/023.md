**What differed.**
- The same number appears for the third time, each time with a slightly different gloss: "software tasks that would take a human expert **over an hour**" (unit 4); "**hourlong** software tasks with >40% success" (unit 12, with the figure line labelled "Simplified AI R&D"); "software tasks… that would take a human **at least an hour**" (here). "Over", "hourlong" and "at least" are near-synonyms, but a careful assertion record would anchor to this body sentence, the most precise.
- **Autonomy = task length**, made explicit: "increased autonomy is largely reflected in the length of task". A measurement definition of autonomy. Note "largely": length isn't the whole of autonomy, but it's the measure AISI uses.
- **"Pursue sub-goals on the way to larger aims"**: an observation of instrumental behaviour, described neutrally as a capability. In the alignment literature, "instrumental sub-goals" is where power-seeking worries start. Here it's simply part of being good at tasks. AISI keeps the capability description apart from the risk interpretation, consistent with its method so far.

**Wandering.**
- "End-to-end" is the important word: success means completing the whole task, not parts. This makes ">40%" more meaningful than component scores, and contrasts with self-replication, where (I suspect) the evals are components.
- Where does the eight-month doubling come from? The summary attached it to *cyber* task length (FIGURE 3), not to these software tasks. So there are two task-length series: software/autonomy (FIGURE 2) and cyber (FIGURE 3). I wonder if the software series has its own doubling rate, and whether it matches METR's (about 7 months). If AISI's cyber series doubles every 8 months and METR's software series every 7, that would be cross-source convergence. Is AISI independent of METR here? It may use METR-derived methods. A lineage question for later.
- "Observed how models form and execute plans" suggests transcript analysis, not just success rates: qualitative evidence from agent trajectories. I'd want to know if AISI looked at *how* failures happen. Long-horizon failure is usually error accumulation and getting stuck. A rising success rate on hour-long tasks implies better error recovery, which is itself a "complexity" gain.
