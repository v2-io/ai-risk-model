- **Defence benefit acknowledged for cyber**, as for chem-bio: "help strengthen defences". AISI is consistent: dual use framed both ways in each domain.
- **"Bypass cryptographic checks"** is a new example, more specific than "identifying vulnerabilities" (unit 16). CTF-style tasks of this kind are classic: break a weak crypto implementation, forge a signature.
- "A **selection** of our findings": the third selection statement (after "representative subsets", unit 9, and "not all methodologies", unit 19). The report is a curated window, and it says so.

**Wandering.**
- "Just beginning to complete expert-level" matches the figure (expert tier at 18–25%), better than the summary's "tested the first model that could successfully complete expert-level tasks". "Just beginning" is calibrated, and the right phrase to quote.
- "Two years ago, they could barely complete tasks requiring one year of cyber expertise." The apprentice tier is "<1 year". "Barely" fits about 9–10% in late 2023/early 2024. The years-of-experience ladder is: technical non-expert, apprentice (<1 yr), practitioner, expert (10+ yrs). Practitioner's range hasn't been stated yet (1–10 years?). A small definitional gap; maybe the glossary or appendix fills it.
- The cyber threat model, by contrast with chem-bio, isn't described as barrier-lowering yet. In cyber, the risk is both de-skilling (novices attacking) and *scaling* (automated attacks at volume), and capability uplift for skilled actors (states). I'll watch whether AISI distinguishes these. A security institute will care most about state actors, though it may not say so publicly.
- "Strengthen defences": AI finding vulnerabilities is as useful to defenders patching as to attackers exploiting. The asymmetry question (does AI favour offence or defence?) is a big one in cyber policy. AISI probably won't take a side; it would be notable if it did.
