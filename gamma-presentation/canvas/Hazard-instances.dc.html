“Hazard” names at least seven concepts. Settle them first, then give the word to one. And since frequencies come only from documented instances, an instance record must carry what a base rate needs.

The seven concepts

Onomasiological work in practice (chapter 3): concepts first. H3, H4 and H5 already have established compound names, so the live question is H1 against H6.

H1a sourcean object, substance, energy, activity or disposition with the potential to cause harm. Guide 51, UK HSE, SRA, ICAO, the UN, bow-tie, Kaplan and Garrick. The HSE’s “intrinsic property or disposition” is close to the EU Code’s capabilities and propensities.
H2a potential occurrenceIPCC; IASR’s “event or activity that has the potential to cause harm”
H3a hazardous eventthe manifestation of a hazard in a place and time
H4a hazardous situationor exposure
H5the loss-of-control statebow-tie’s top event; STPA’s “a system state … together with … worst-case environmental conditions, will lead to a loss”
H6a non-malicious cause classthe NRR’s hazard, as opposed to its threat
H7a root causefrom AI Hazard Management (Schnitzer et al.); added to OVERVIEW as an eighth corpus sense

In every formal glossary the research checked, intent is carried on a separate axis: safety against security (SRA), adversarial against accidental threat sources (SP 800-30), the attack sense of “threat”. And in bow-tie and SP 800-30, “threat” explicitly includes accidental causes. So the NRR’s pair translates cleanly as H1 or a cause, crossed with intent.

A count is not a rate

Scenarios, threat models, arguments and expert elicitation give priors from reasoning. Frequencies come only from documented instances. AISI’s incident report counts “19 events” across 10 of 122 runs; most retellings give the 19 and drop the 122.

122 runs; the 10 with events in red. Which runs they were isn’t shown here, only the proportion. Without the 122, “19 events” cannot become a frequency.

So the instance record (G1(e)) must carry

a denominator: the population the instances came from
the reporting channel and its selection effects: mandated reports (SB 53’s critical safety incidents, the EU’s serious incidents), voluntary disclosures, press, automated detection each select different instances; SB 53 publishes only anonymized annual aggregates, so the corpus will hold counts without cases
status: allegation, company disclosure, regulator finding, or our reading
merging: which reports count as one instance, recorded as someone’s judgment
the reference class: which kind of event it counts as, a modelling decision that moves the prior, recorded with its author
near misses and precursors: most of the frequency information for rare events

The record itself stays thin (what happened, when, anchored). Who acted, with what intent and outcome, and whether two accounts describe one instance, are attributed assertions about it.

Optional depth

Lexicon work, not a semantics debate

Is AISI’s cyber-range incident, INC-2026-07-28-01, an instance of loss of control, of unsanctioned action, or of an incident inside an evaluation? The sources disagree, and the answer moves a prior more than the count does.

With the kinds of event separated in the lexicon, “is this loss of control?” becomes checkable questions: did control fail to prevent the act, or was the ability to halt lost? Recoverable, at what cost? Inside an evaluation, which SB 53’s fourth limb excludes when it was designed to elicit the behaviour? Which sources’ criteria does it meet?

The disagreement lands where it belongs: in different criteria, attributed, each with its effect on the count visible.
