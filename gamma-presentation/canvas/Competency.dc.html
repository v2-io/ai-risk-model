What the thin pass taught about questions

Say which direction a question runs. Q1 names source words, so it is semasiological: it asks which source terms apply. Its onomasiological twin: “which sources’ definitions reach a risk of more than 50 deaths from one occurrence?”
Split risk-shaped from incident-shaped. No source calls an incident catastrophic; they say it of risks. The ex-post form lands on conferred event statuses (SB 53’s “critical safety incident”, the companies’ incident ladders).
Every event question states three things: what is stipulated, whether unstated harms are zero, and an as-of date.
Weigh terms only with a question that can weigh them (change 13). A two-fact hypothetical can’t; a documented instance can. Report weights at the row, and list what the evaluator can’t evaluate.

Five acceptance tests: when the lexicon is ready to translate at scale

They sit under the questions. Lineage is deliberately not one of them; it belongs to the records.

1
Round trip

Re-express, clause by clause in our terms: SB 53’s “catastrophic risk”; the AI Act’s “systemic risk” (Art. 3(65)); Anthropic’s “misalignment risk”, defined twice in the August report, 25 lines apart, once with “catastrophic”; and IASR 2026’s “loss of control” in its glossary and its body, a loose source on purpose. Passing may end at an imported binding, a deliberately unbound term, or a declared ambiguity, but an ambiguity passes only if it names its candidates as lexicon terms, cites the passage for each, and says what evidence would decide. Otherwise it is a gap.
